**Definition**

Most AI projects follow a recognisable pattern of perceived progress that does not match actual progress. The pattern has three phases: an early high (the demo phase), where the team produces something impressive quickly and momentum feels great; a middle valley (the eval phase), where systematic testing surfaces failure modes the demo did not, and momentum stalls; and a late climb (the production phase), where the team grinds through edge cases, integration, and hardening to reach something genuinely shippable. Recognising this S curve in advance prevents the team and client from panicking in the valley, and from declaring victory at the demo high.

**Why it matters in delivery**

The demo high is dangerous because it produces overconfidence. The team feels productive, the client is impressed, and there is a temptation to commit to a timeline based on the demo trajectory. This is almost always wrong. The demo phase achieves 60 to 80 percent of perceived progress in the first 20 percent of the time. The remaining 80 percent of the time produces the last 20 percent of perceived progress, but it is the part that matters for production.

The eval valley is dangerous in the opposite way. The team feels stuck, the client wonders if the project is failing, and there is a temptation to abandon the rigorous testing in favour of returning to demo-like progress. This is also wrong. The valley is where the project is actually doing the most important work: surfacing the failure modes that would have caused production failure if not addressed. Naming the valley in advance, so that everyone expects it, is one of the most useful things a PM can do.

**How it works**

The S curve has three phases, each with characteristic signals and characteristic risks.

| Phase | Duration | Perceived progress | Actual progress | Key risks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Demo high | First 20 to 30 percent of project | Fast | Real but narrow | Overpromising, declaring victory too early, mistaking demo for production |
| Eval valley | Middle 30 to 50 percent | Stalled or regressing | Most valuable | Panic, abandoning evals, switching scope to recover momentum |
| Production climb | Final 30 to 50 percent | Slow but steady | Convergent | Time pressure causing corner-cutting, fatigue, missing edge cases |

The curve is not symmetric. The demo high is short and steep. The valley is long and uncomfortable. The production climb is the longest phase in calendar time but shows the most reliable progress.

**The demo high in detail**

The demo high happens because LLMs are surprisingly good at producing impressive outputs on cherry picked examples. A first prompt, applied to a hand chosen input, often produces something that makes the room go quiet. This is real capability, not magic, but it is a narrow capability. The same prompt applied to a random sample of real inputs fails on 30 to 50 percent of them in ways that the cherry picked demo did not show.

The team's job in this phase is to enjoy the momentum but not commit to it. Use the demo to build confidence with stakeholders, while privately tracking the broader distribution. A team that internally believes the demo represents the final product will be deeply disappointed when the eval valley arrives. A team that recognises the demo as one good point on a broader distribution is prepared.

**The eval valley in detail**

The valley begins when the team runs the first systematic evaluation against a representative test set (the eval set from 12.2). The score is almost always lower than the demo would have suggested. The team is surprised, the client is sometimes alarmed, and the temptation is to either explain it away or to start over with a different approach.

Neither is right. The right move is to take the eval result seriously, identify the categories of failure, and work systematically through them. This is grinding work. Each category of failure typically requires a specific intervention: prompt adjustment, retrieval improvement, additional context, refusal handling, output formatting. Each intervention produces a small incremental improvement, often 2 to 5 percentage points on the eval score. Multiple interventions accumulate over weeks.

During this phase, the team's status updates feel monotonous. "We are working through eval failures, currently at 78 percent pass rate, up from 72 last week." The work is real but unimpressive in narrative form. The PM's job is to keep the team confident that this work is the work that matters, and to keep the client patient.

The valley typically lasts 3 to 6 weeks on a 12 to 16 week project. It is the longest sustained period of slow visible progress in the entire project. Teams that have not experienced this before sometimes conclude the project is failing. Naming the valley in advance, and showing the gradual eval improvement, prevents this.

**The production climb in detail**

The climb begins when the eval score has stabilised at a level the team and client agree is acceptable for production. The work shifts from improving the model to hardening the system around the model: integration, monitoring, error handling, user interface, deployment, documentation, training. This is engineering work that is generally well understood, but takes time.

The climb is dangerous because it looks like the project is almost done. The model is working, the demo is impressive, the eval scores are at target. Stakeholders may push to launch sooner. The PM's job is to maintain discipline through the climb, ensuring that the unglamorous production hardening work happens, because skipping it produces production failures that are far more expensive than the wait.

**Communicating the S curve to the client**

The S curve should be introduced to the client in week one or two, as part of setting expectations. The framing is: "this is the typical trajectory we expect to see. Early progress will look fast. Middle progress will feel slow because we are doing the systematic testing that prevents production failures. Late progress will be steady but will not produce visible demos every week, because we are hardening for production."

Most clients, when shown this curve in advance, accept it. Most clients, when shown it for the first time in week eight after panicking about progress, find it more difficult. The conversation is much easier to have in week one.

A useful artifact: a simple chart showing the S curve, marked with the project's expected weeks for each phase. Update it weekly with actual progress. Both team and client can see where the project is and where it should be.

**PM decision points**

- Have I named the S curve to the client in week one or two, before the demo high produces overconfidence?
- During the demo high, am I privately tracking broader performance rather than only the demo highlights?
- During the eval valley, am I keeping the team and client confident that the slow work is the right work?

**Watch outs**

- A demo that looks too good is suspicious. If a first attempt produces 95 percent quality outputs on cherry picked examples, the distribution on real inputs is almost certainly worse. Investigate before celebrating.
- During the valley, resist the temptation to switch approaches just because progress is slow. The valley is supposed to be slow. Switching approaches restarts the valley rather than ending it.
- Some clients want to launch at the end of the demo high. The cost of this is production failures that damage the relationship more than a longer build would have.

**Takeaway: AI projects follow an S curve of perceived progress: demo high, eval valley, production climb. The valley is where the real work happens, even though it feels stuck. Name the curve to the client in week one, track real progress alongside perceived progress, and maintain discipline through all three phases.**

**Qantas story**

Imagine starting a Qantas AI project to build an internal assistant for the corporate sales team, helping them draft customised proposals for major corporate clients. The team is energetic, the operating sponsor is engaged, and the first two weeks produce a striking demo. A real RFP from a major mining company is fed into the assistant. The assistant produces a draft proposal that is, by all early indications, of very high quality. The room is impressed. The operating sponsor wants to know if they can show the executive sponsor next week.

The PM, who has seen this pattern before, makes two moves. The demo is shown to the executive sponsor with appropriate framing: "this is a single high quality example, we expect quality to be more variable on a broader range of inputs, and the next phase will tell us what the actual distribution looks like." The executive sponsor appreciates the directness and is not over-promised to.

In parallel, the team begins to construct a broader eval set drawn from the past two years of corporate sales RFPs. The eval set has 80 RFPs of varying complexity, industry, deal size, and customer maturity. The team runs the current prompt against all 80 and scores the outputs against criteria agreed with the corporate sales operating team.

The result is humbling. The pass rate is 64 percent. The demo RFP turns out to be one of the easier cases. RFPs in less familiar industries, with non-standard formats, or requiring specific technical commitments produce significantly worse outputs. The team is briefly demoralised. The operating sponsor wonders if the demo was misleading.

This is the start of the eval valley. The PM keeps the team focused. Each category of failure is identified: industry-specific terminology in mining and energy is causing terminology errors, RFPs that include non-standard pricing structures are not being handled, RFPs that require specific compliance commitments are missing them, very long RFPs are causing the model to lose context. Each category gets a specific intervention. Each intervention produces 2 to 4 percentage points of improvement on the eval score.

Over the next four weeks, the pass rate climbs from 64 percent to 72 to 76 to 81. The team's weekly updates are monotonous, but the trend is steady. The operating sponsor, kept informed of the chart that was introduced in week one, can see the project is in the expected valley and is climbing out. She maintains confidence with the executive sponsor by referencing the curve.

In week 10, the eval score hits 86 percent, the team and operating sponsor agree this is acceptable for production with a caveat that low confidence outputs are routed to human review. The production climb begins. The work for the next four weeks is integration with the corporate sales workflow, training the sales team on how to use the tool, building the human review handoff, and creating the dashboard that shows the team how the tool is being used.

The launch happens in week 14, on schedule. The corporate sales team uses the tool. The quality is consistent with the eval score, with the human review catching the low confidence cases. The relationship deepens.

The interesting comparison is with another Qantas AI project that did not name the curve in advance. That project also had a striking demo in week three, also had a sober eval result in week five, and the executive sponsor of that project (not having been told to expect the valley) panicked and ordered the team to "make it as good as the demo, immediately". The team scrambled, the rigorous evaluation work was reduced, the team focused on impressive looking demos that did not represent the broader distribution. The system was launched in week 12 looking good in cherry picked examples and failing 35 percent of real cases in production. The launch was rolled back. The project recovery took six weeks and the executive sponsor lost confidence in the team. None of this would have happened if the curve had been named in advance.

The lesson for AI delivery is that the S curve is real, predictable, and consequential. Naming it to the client in week one is a small intervention with disproportionate effect. The team that does this protects itself from both the overconfidence of the demo high and the panic of the eval valley. The team that does not, fights both battles in conditions made worse by surprise.

**定义**

大多数 AI 项目都遵循一个可识别的 pattern: "感知的进度" 跟 "实际的进度" 不匹配。这个 pattern 有三个阶段: 早期高点 (demo 阶段), 团队快速产出令人印象深刻的东西, 动量感觉很好; 中段低谷 (eval 阶段), 系统化测试浮出 demo 没浮出的失败模式, 动量停滞; 后期爬坡 (production 阶段), 团队磨过边缘情况、集成、加固, 到达一个真正能交付的东西。事先识别这条 S 曲线, 防止团队和客户在低谷恐慌、在 demo 高点宣告胜利。

**为何在交付中重要**

Demo 高点危险, 因为它产出过度自信。团队感觉有产出, 客户被打动, 有按 demo 轨迹承诺时间线的诱惑。这几乎总是错的。Demo 阶段在前 20% 的时间里达到 60 到 80% 的感知进度。剩下 80% 的时间产出最后 20% 的感知进度, 但那是对 production 重要的部分。

Eval 低谷以相反方式危险。团队感觉卡住, 客户怀疑项目是否在失败, 有放弃严格测试、回到 demo 般进度的诱惑。**这也错**。低谷是项目实际在做最重要工作的地方: 浮出 "如果不处理就会造成 production 失败" 的失败模式。事先命名低谷, 让所有人都预期它, 是 PM 能做的最有用的事之一。

**运作原理**

S 曲线有三个阶段, 每个有特征信号和特征风险。

| 阶段 | 时长 | 感知进度 | 实际进度 | 关键风险 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Demo 高点 | 项目前 20 到 30% | 快 | 真实但狭窄 | 过度承诺、过早宣告胜利、把 demo 误当 production |
| Eval 低谷 | 中段 30 到 50% | 停滞或回退 | 最有价值 | 恐慌、放弃 evals、为了恢复动量换范围 |
| Production 爬坡 | 末段 30 到 50% | 慢但稳定 | 收敛 | 时间压力造成偷工、疲劳、漏掉边缘情况 |

**曲线不对称**。Demo 高点短而陡。**低谷长而不适**。Production 爬坡是日历时间上最长的阶段, 但显示最可靠的进度。

**Demo 高点详解**

Demo 高点发生, 因为 LLM 在精挑细选的例子上意外地擅长产出令人印象深刻的输出。第一个 prompt, 应用到手挑的输入上, 经常产出让房间安静下来的东西。这是真实能力, 不是魔法, 但它是一个狭窄的能力。同样的 prompt 应用到真实输入的随机样本上, 在 30 到 50% 上以精挑 demo 没显示的方式失败。

团队在这个阶段的工作是享受动量但不承诺它。用 demo 跟 stakeholder 建立信心, 同时私下追踪更广的分布。内部相信 demo 代表最终产品的团队, 在 eval 低谷到来时会深度失望。把 demo 识别为更广分布上一个好点的团队, 是被准备好的。

**Eval 低谷详解**

低谷在团队对一份有代表性的测试集 (12.2 的 eval set) 跑第一次系统化评估时开始。分数几乎总是比 demo 暗示的低。团队意外, 客户有时担忧, 诱惑是要么解释掉它、要么用不同方式重来。

**两个都不对**。对的动作是认真对待 eval 结果, 识别失败的类别, 系统化地工作过它们。这是磨人的工作。每个失败类别通常需要一个具体干预: prompt 调整、检索改进、额外上下文、拒绝处理、输出格式。每个干预产出一个小的增量改善, 经常是 eval 分数上的 2 到 5 个百分点。多个干预在几周里累积。

这个阶段, 团队的 status 更新感觉单调。"我们在工作过 eval 失败, 当前通过率 78%, 上周 72"。工作是真实的但用叙事形式不令人印象深刻。PM 的工作是让团队保持信心 (这项工作就是重要的工作), 让客户保持耐心。

低谷在 12 到 16 周的项目上典型持续 3 到 6 周。它是整个项目里最长的、可见进度缓慢的持续期。之前没经历过这个的团队有时会结论说项目在失败。事先命名低谷, 并显示渐进的 eval 改善, 防止这个。

**Production 爬坡详解**

爬坡在 eval 分数稳定在团队和客户同意可生产的水平时开始。工作从 "改善模型" 转到 "加固模型周围的系统": 集成、监控、错误处理、用户界面、部署、文档、培训。这是一般良好理解的工程工作, 但花时间。

爬坡危险, 因为它看起来项目几乎完成。模型工作了, demo 印象深刻, eval 分数在目标上。Stakeholder 可能推动更早上线。PM 的工作是在爬坡阶段维持纪律, 确保不光鲜的 production 加固工作发生, 因为**跳过它产出比等待贵得多的 production 失败**。

**把 S 曲线沟通给客户**

S 曲线应该在第 1 或 2 周被引入给客户, 作为设定预期的一部分。**措辞是**: "这是我们预期看到的典型轨迹。早期进度看起来快。中段进度感觉慢, 因为我们在做防止 production 失败的系统化测试。后期进度会稳定但不会每周产出可见 demo, 因为我们在为 production 加固"。

大多数客户, 事先看到这条曲线时, 接受它。大多数客户, 在第 8 周对进度恐慌后第一次看到时, 觉得更难接受。这场对话在第 1 周容易得多。

**一个有用产物**: 一张简单的图, 显示 S 曲线, 标记项目每个阶段预期的周数。每周用实际进度更新。团队和客户都能看到项目在哪、应该在哪。

**PM 决策点**

- 我**有没有在第 1 或 2 周向客户命名 S 曲线**, 在 **demo 高点产出过度自信之前**?
- Demo 高点期间, 我**有没有私下追踪更广的表现**, 而不只是 demo 亮点?
- Eval 低谷期间, 我**有没有让团队和客户保持信心**, **慢工作就是对的工作**?

**注意事项**

- **看起来太好的 demo 可疑**。**如果第一次尝试在精挑例子上产出 95% 质量的输出, 真实输入上的分布几乎肯定更差**。**庆祝之前调查**。
- **低谷期间, 抵抗 "因为进度慢就换方式" 的诱惑**。**低谷应该慢**。**换方式重启低谷而不是结束它**。
- **有些客户想在 demo 高点末尾上线**。**这个代价是损害关系的 production 失败, 比更长的建造更损害**。

**要点总结: AI 项目遵循感知进度的 S 曲线: demo 高点、eval 低谷、production 爬坡。低谷是真正工作发生的地方, 即使感觉卡住。第 1 周向客户命名曲线, 跟着感知进度追踪真实进度, 三个阶段都维持纪律。**

**Qantas 案例**

设想你启动一个 Qantas AI 项目, 给企业销售团队建一个内部助手, 帮他们为大企业客户起草定制提案。团队有活力, 运营 sponsor engaged, 前两周产出一个 striking demo。一家大型矿业公司的真实 RFP 被喂进助手。助手产出一份提案草稿, 早期迹象显示质量非常高。**房间被打动**。运营 sponsor 想知道下周能否给 executive sponsor 看。

PM 之前见过这个 pattern, 做两个动作。Demo 用合适的措辞给 executive sponsor 看: "这是一个高质量的单一例子, 我们预期质量在更广的输入范围上更可变, 下一阶段会告诉我们实际分布长什么样"。Executive sponsor 欣赏直接, 没被过度承诺。

平行地, 团队开始从过去两年的企业销售 RFP 构建一份更广的 eval set。Eval set 有 80 份 RFP, 复杂度、行业、交易规模、客户成熟度各异。团队跑当前 prompt 对所有 80 份, 根据跟企业销售运营团队商定的标准给输出打分。

**结果令人警醒**。通过率 64%。Demo 的 RFP 原来是较容易的之一。陌生行业的 RFP、非标准格式的、需要具体技术承诺的产出明显更差的输出。**团队短暂沮丧**。运营 sponsor 想知道 demo 是不是误导。

这是 eval 低谷的开始。PM 让团队保持聚焦。每个失败类别被识别: 矿业和能源的行业特定术语造成术语错误、含非标准定价结构的 RFP 没被处理、需要具体合规承诺的 RFP 漏了它们、非常长的 RFP 让模型丢上下文。每个类别得到具体干预。每个干预产出 2 到 4 个百分点的 eval 分数改善。

接下来四周, 通过率从 64% 爬到 72、76、81。团队的每周更新单调, 但趋势稳定。运营 sponsor 知道第 1 周引入的那张图, 能看到项目在预期的低谷里、正在爬出来。她引用曲线在 executive sponsor 面前维持信心。

第 10 周, eval 分数到 86%, 团队和运营 sponsor 同意这对 production 可接受, 附加条件是低置信输出路由到人工 review。Production 爬坡开始。接下来四周的工作是跟企业销售工作流的集成、培训销售团队怎么用工具、构建人工 review 移交、创建展示团队怎么用工具的 dashboard。

第 14 周按时上线。企业销售团队使用工具。质量跟 eval 分数一致, 人工 review 抓住低置信案例。**关系加深**。

有意思的对照是另一个没有事先命名曲线的 Qantas AI 项目。那个项目也在第 3 周有 striking demo、第 5 周有清醒的 eval 结果, 那个项目的 executive sponsor (没被告知要预期低谷) 恐慌, 命令团队 "立刻让它跟 demo 一样好"。团队慌乱, 严格评估工作被削减, 团队聚焦在不代表更广分布的看起来印象深刻的 demo 上。系统在第 12 周以 "精挑例子上好看、真实案例 35% 失败" 的状态上线。**上线被回滚**。项目恢复花了六周, executive sponsor 对团队失去信心。这些都不会发生, 如果曲线被事先命名。

AI 交付的启示: S 曲线真实、可预测、有后果。第 1 周向客户命名它是一个有不成比例效果的小干预。这么做的团队保护自己不受 demo 高点的过度自信和 eval 低谷的恐慌。不这么做的团队, 在被意外恶化的条件下打两场仗。
