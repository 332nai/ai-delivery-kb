**Definition**

Some AI projects reach a point where the right move is to stop, throw away part of the work, and start that part over. The signals that this point has been reached are recognisable. The decision to act on them is hard, because stopping feels like failure and starting over feels like waste. This item is about recognising the moment when stopping is the right call, communicating the decision honestly to the client, and executing the restart in a way that preserves what is salvageable.

**Why it matters in delivery**

Continuing on a flawed foundation is one of the most expensive mistakes in delivery. The cost of stopping in week six and redoing two weeks of work is small. The cost of continuing on that flawed foundation through to launch, then discovering at week 14 that the system does not actually work, is much larger. Yet the social and psychological pressure to keep going is strong. Stopping feels like admitting failure. Stopping triggers difficult conversations with the client. Stopping might affect the team's reputation. Each pressure pushes the team to continue past the point where stopping would have been right.

The PM's job is to make the stopping decision when the signals warrant it, despite the pressures against it. Doing this well is one of the most senior judgments in AI delivery. It requires being honest about what the team has built, being willing to discard work that is not serving the project, and being skilled at communicating the decision so that it strengthens rather than weakens the client relationship.

**How it works**

Three categories of signal indicate that stopping and redoing may be the right call.

*Foundational error.* The team has built on an assumption that turns out to be wrong. Common examples: the eval set was constructed against criteria the operating team disagrees with, the prompt strategy is fundamentally mismatched to the use case, the data being used is the wrong dataset for the actual problem. When the foundation is wrong, continuing to build on it produces increasingly elaborate wrong outputs.

*Compounding mistakes.* The team has made a series of small decisions, each defensible individually, that together have produced an architecture that does not work. The system is patched, the patches are patched, and the team is spending more time on patches than on substantive work. The system is technically functional but is degenerating.

*Misaligned with what users actually do.* The team has built something that works against the eval set but does not work in real user contexts. This is related to the 16.3 wrong-thing diagnosis, but specifically refers to the case where the team has invested significantly and the misalignment is structural rather than cosmetic.

When one or more of these signals is present, the question is not "should we keep improving the current work" but "should we keep the current work as the basis for further improvement". Sometimes the answer is no.

**The cost of continuing versus the cost of restarting**

Before stopping, the PM should estimate honestly the cost of each path.

*Cost of continuing.* How many weeks until the project would have shipped, and how much of that time would be spent working around the flaw? What would the launched product be like, given the flaw? What would the client and users think of it?

*Cost of restarting.* How much of the existing work can be salvaged? How long would it take to redo the parts that need redoing? What is the impact on timeline, budget, and team energy?

The estimate is rarely precise, but the order of magnitude matters. If continuing costs 6 weeks of frustrating work to ship a flawed product, and restarting costs 4 weeks to ship a better one, restarting is obviously right. If continuing costs 2 weeks and restarting costs 8 weeks, restarting is probably wrong unless the launched product would be unusable.

The honest cost estimate is the work the PM has to do. Most teams underestimate the cost of continuing on a flawed foundation, because the cost is hidden in many small struggles that compound. Most teams overestimate the cost of restarting, because they psychologically count work already done as sunk cost they do not want to abandon.

**How to communicate the decision to the client**

The conversation has three parts.

*Part 1: Frame the discovery, not the failure.* The conversation opens with what the team has learned, not with what is wrong. "Through the work over the past four weeks, we have discovered something important about how this needs to be built." This framing makes the conversation forward-looking rather than apologetic.

*Part 2: Present the choice as a recommendation, not a problem.* The team comes to the client with a recommendation, not with a request for the client to decide. "We recommend stopping the current build and restarting with a different approach. Here is why, here is what we would do differently, here is what it would cost in time and budget." This is professional. Bringing the client a problem to solve is amateur.

*Part 3: Be specific about what is being kept and what is being discarded.* A blanket "we are starting over" is alarming. A specific "we are keeping the eval set, the operating team relationships, the infrastructure, and the integration plan, and redoing the prompt strategy and the data pipeline" is reassuring. It also forces the team to be honest about what is genuinely salvageable.

The framing that works: this is a normal part of AI delivery. The first version of any AI system is often not the right version. The team that recognises this and restarts at the right time delivers better outcomes than the team that pushes through. The client almost always accepts this when it is presented confidently.

**Internal team management during a restart**

Restarts are hard on team morale. People have invested in the work being discarded. The PM's job is to manage the emotional response while ensuring the restart happens cleanly.

A few specific practices.

*Acknowledge the discarded work, do not pretend it had no value.* The team learned things by doing the discarded work. Those learnings inform the restart. Name them explicitly.

*Make the restart fast and decisive.* A drawn out restart, where the team is half discarding and half preserving, is worse than a clean restart. Within two weeks of the decision, the team should be working on the new approach with the discarded work archived.

*Use the restart to address other latent issues.* The team often knows of related issues that have been festering. The restart is an opportunity to address them too. Ask the team what they would change while everything is being reconsidered.

*Reset stakeholder expectations honestly.* Some stakeholders were excited about the discarded work. They need to be told what is happening, why, and what to expect now. The PM owns these conversations, not the team.

**PM decision points**

- Am I willing to recommend stopping when the signals warrant it, or am I psychologically committed to continuing?
- Have I estimated the cost of continuing honestly, including the hidden costs of working around the flaw?
- When I bring the recommendation to the client, am I bringing it as a recommendation or as a problem?

**Watch outs**

- Stopping should not be the first response to a difficult phase. Most projects go through difficult phases without needing to restart. Reserve the stopping recommendation for genuine foundational issues, not for normal valley conditions (see 16.1).
- The team will resist a restart proposal at first. They have invested in the work. The PM has to be willing to make the case even against initial team resistance.
- After the restart decision is made, do not litigate it again. If the team keeps relitigating, the restart will be slow and partial. Make the decision, communicate it, and execute.

**Takeaway: some AI projects need to stop and restart parts of the work. The signals are foundational error, compounding mistakes, and misalignment with users. Estimate the cost of each path honestly. Communicate the decision to the client as a recommendation, framed as discovery. Manage the team through the restart with acknowledgment, decisiveness, and honest reset of expectations.**

**Qantas story**

Imagine running a Qantas AI project to build an internal tool for the cargo customer service team: a system that drafts responses to shipping enquiries by drawing on past correspondence and current shipping rates. By week six, the project is producing outputs that the team is privately uncomfortable with. The eval scores look acceptable (around 75 percent), but in real testing with cargo customer service agents, the drafts are routinely being rewritten substantially before being sent. The agents are polite about the tool, but in their own words, "it is faster to write from scratch than to fix what it produces".

The PM runs the 16.3 diagnostic and concludes the project is not quite in the wrong-thing category, but there is a foundational issue. The team had constructed the system around generating polished, complete responses, on the theory that a polished draft would save agent time. The reality is that cargo customer service is highly contextual: each customer has a relationship history, each shipping situation has specifics, and a polished generic response is harder to adapt than a structured outline. The agents do not want polished prose, they want structured input that they can quickly fill in.

This is a foundational error. Continuing to improve the polished prose generator would produce a tool that the agents would not use. Restarting with a structured outline generator would produce a tool that fits the agents' actual workflow.

The PM estimates the cost honestly. Continuing the current approach: probably four more weeks of prompt engineering to push the eval score from 75 to 85 percent, followed by an unenthusiastic launch and probable abandonment within three months. Restarting: probably three weeks to redo the prompt strategy and output format, while keeping the eval set, the data integration, the agent relationships, and the infrastructure. The cost comparison is clear: restarting is faster than continuing, and produces a tool that actually gets used.

The PM brings this to the operating sponsor. The framing is the three part structure described above. "Through the work over the past six weeks, we have learned something important about how cargo customer service works. We recommend changing our approach. Here is what we are keeping, here is what we are redoing, here is what it costs."

The operating sponsor's response is initially defensive. She had advocated for the polished prose approach internally and feels personally invested. The PM is patient. The framing is repeated: this is not a failure, this is a discovery. The work to date has produced learnings that make the new approach possible. The operating sponsor, given time to think, agrees with the recommendation.

The communication to the executive sponsor happens in the next steering committee, with the operating sponsor leading and the PM supporting. The message is the same: we have learned something important and we are adjusting accordingly. The executive sponsor accepts the recommendation and asks for an updated timeline.

The team is told the next day. The reaction is mixed. The tech lead, who had been growing uncomfortable with the prompt complexity required to produce polished prose, is relieved. Two engineers, who had invested heavily in the prompt design, are initially disappointed. The PM acknowledges the discarded work explicitly: the eval set is staying, the data pipeline is staying, the agent feedback patterns are informing the new approach. The discarded work is not wasted, it has produced the understanding that makes the new approach right.

The restart happens in 16 days. The new structured outline generator is built and tested with the same eval set, achieves similar eval scores, and produces outputs that the agents actively want to use. The launch happens in week 13, one week ahead of the original schedule. The agents use the tool consistently after launch.

The interesting comparison is with what might have happened without the stop. The team would have spent four more weeks improving prompts to produce more polished prose, launched a tool that the agents found cumbersome, and watched usage drop within a month of launch. The project would have been classified as a marginal failure six months later.

The lesson for AI delivery is that the willingness to stop and restart, when the signals warrant it, is one of the most senior skills in this work. It requires being honest about what is not working, willing to discard work that is not serving the project, and skilled at communicating the decision. Done well, a stop-and-restart strengthens the client relationship rather than weakening it, because the client sees a team that values getting the right outcome over preserving the appearance of progress. That is a deeply valuable reputation to build.

**定义**

**有些 AI 项目到达一个点, 对的动作是停下、扔掉部分工作、把那部分重做**。**到达这个点的信号可识别**。**对它行动的决定难**, 因为**停下感觉像失败, 重做感觉像浪费**。**这一节是关于识别 "停下是对的选择" 的时刻、诚实地把决定沟通给客户、以保留可挽救东西的方式执行重做**。

**为何在交付中重要**

**在有缺陷的基础上继续是交付里最贵的错误之一**。**第 6 周停下并重做两周工作的成本小**。**在那个有缺陷的基础上继续到上线, 然后第 14 周发现系统实际上不工作的成本大得多**。**但 "继续" 的社交和心理压力强**。**停下感觉像承认失败**。**停下触发跟客户的难对话**。**停下可能影响团队声誉**。**每种压力推动团队继续, 经过 "停下本应是对的" 的点**。

**PM 的工作是在信号支持时做出停下决定, 尽管有反对的压力**。**做好这件事是 AI 交付里最资深的判断之一**。**它需要诚实地看团队建了什么、愿意丢弃不服务项目的工作、擅长沟通决定, 让它加强而不是削弱客户关系**。

**运作原理**

**三类信号表明停下和重做可能是对的选择**。

*基础错误*。**团队建在一个事实证明错的假设上**。**常见例子**: **eval set 被对照运营团队不同意的标准构建、prompt 策略跟 use case 基本不匹配、被用的数据是实际问题的错数据集**。**当基础错, 在它上面继续建产出越来越精致的错输出**。

*累积的错误*。**团队做了一系列小决定, 每个单独看可辩护, 合起来产出了一个不工作的架构**。**系统打补丁, 补丁打补丁, 团队在补丁上花的时间多于在实质工作上**。**系统技术上工作, 但在退化**。

*跟用户实际做的事错位*。**团队建了在 eval set 上工作、但在真实用户上下文里不工作的东西**。**这跟 16.3 的错事诊断相关, 但具体指 "团队已经显著投资, 错位是结构性的而不是化妆性的"**。

**当一个或多个信号在场, 问题不是 "我们应该继续改善当前工作", 而是 "我们应该把当前工作作为进一步改善的基础"**。**有时候答案是不**。

**继续的成本 vs 重做的成本**

**在停下之前, PM 应该诚实估计每条路径的成本**。

*继续的成本*。**项目要几周才上线, 那段时间多少花在绕过缺陷上?** **鉴于缺陷, 上线的产品会怎样?** **客户和用户会怎么看待它?**

*重做的成本*。**多少现有工作能被挽救?** **重做需要重做的部分要多久?** **对时间线、预算、团队能量的影响是什么?**

**估计很少精确, 但数量级重要**。**如果继续花 6 周令人挫败的工作上线一个有缺陷产品, 重做花 4 周上线一个更好的, 重做明显对**。**如果继续花 2 周、重做花 8 周, 重做可能错, 除非上线产品不可用**。

**诚实的成本估计是 PM 要做的工作**。**大多数团队低估在有缺陷基础上继续的成本**, 因为**成本藏在累积的许多小挣扎里**。**大多数团队高估重做的成本**, 因为**他们心理上把已做的工作当作他们不想抛弃的沉没成本**。

**怎么跟客户沟通决定**

**对话有三部分**。

*第一部分: 把它框架成发现, 不是失败*。**对话开始于团队学到了什么, 不是什么错了**。**"通过过去四周的工作, 我们发现了关于这个怎么需要被建的重要东西"**。**这种措辞让对话向前看而不是道歉**。

*第二部分: 把选择呈现为建议, 不是问题*。**团队带着建议来找客户, 不是带着请求让客户决定**。**"我们建议停下当前建造、用不同方式重做。这是为什么、这是我们会不同地做什么、这是时间和预算成本"**。**这是专业的**。**带客户一个要解决的问题是业余的**。

*第三部分: 关于保留什么、丢弃什么要具体*。**一句笼统的 "我们重新开始" 令人警觉**。**具体的 "我们保留 eval set、运营团队关系、基础设施、集成计划, 重做 prompt 策略和数据管道" 令人安心**。**它也强迫团队对什么真的可挽救诚实**。

**奏效的措辞**: **这是 AI 交付的正常一部分**。**任何 AI 系统的第一版经常不是对的版**。**识别这个并在对的时间重做的团队, 比硬推过去的团队交付出更好的结果**。**客户在它被自信呈现时几乎总是接受**。

**重做期间的内部团队管理**

**重做对团队士气难**。**人们在被丢弃的工作上投资过**。**PM 的工作是管理情绪反应, 同时确保重做干净发生**。

**几个具体实践**。

*承认被丢弃的工作, 不要假装它没价值*。**团队通过做被丢弃的工作学到了东西**。**那些学习告知重做**。**明确命名它们**。

*让重做快速而决定性*。**拖延的重做, 团队一半丢弃一半保留, 比干净的重做更糟**。**决定后两周内, 团队应该在新方式上工作, 被丢弃的工作归档**。

*用重做处理其他潜在 issue*。**团队经常知道相关在发酵的 issue**。**重做是处理它们的机会**。**问团队他们会改什么, 当一切都在被重新考虑时**。

*诚实重置 stakeholder 预期*。**有些 stakeholder 对被丢弃的工作激动**。**他们需要被告知发生什么、为什么、现在期待什么**。**PM 拥有这些对话, 不是团队**。

**PM 决策点**

- 我**愿意在信号支持时建议停下吗**, 还是**心理上承诺继续**?
- 我**有没有诚实估计继续的成本**, 包括 **绕过缺陷的隐藏成本**?
- 当我把建议带给客户, 我**带的是建议还是问题**?

**注意事项**

- **停下不应该是对难阶段的第一反应**。**大多数项目度过难阶段不需要重做**。**把停下建议留给真正的基础 issue, 不是正常低谷条件 (见 16.1)**。
- **团队一开始会抵抗重做提议**。**他们在工作里投资过**。**PM 必须愿意做案子, 即使对抗最初的团队抵抗**。
- **重做决定做出后, 不要再次诉讼它**。**如果团队不停地再次诉讼, 重做会慢和局部**。**做决定、沟通、执行**。

**要点总结: 有些 AI 项目需要停下和重做部分工作。信号是基础错误、累积错误、跟用户错位。诚实估计每条路径的成本。把决定作为建议沟通给客户, 框架成发现。带着承认、决定性、诚实重置预期管理团队度过重做。**

**Qantas 案例**

设想你跑一个 Qantas AI 项目, **给货运客服团队建一个内部工具**: **一个系统通过引用过往通信和当前运费起草对运输咨询的回应**。**到第 6 周, 项目在产出团队私下不舒服的输出**。**Eval 分数看起来可接受 (大约 75%), 但跟货运客服坐席的真实测试里, 草稿经常在发送前被显著重写**。**坐席对工具客气, 但用他们自己的话, "从头写比修它产出的更快"**。

**PM 跑 16.3 诊断, 结论是项目不完全在错事类别, 但有一个基础 issue**。**团队围绕生成精致完整的回应构建了系统, 理论是精致草稿会节省坐席时间**。**现实是货运客服高度上下文相关**: **每个客户有关系历史、每个运输情况有具体细节、一份精致通用回应比结构化大纲难适应**。**坐席不想要精致 prose, 他们想要能快速填的结构化输入**。

**这是基础错误**。**继续改善精致 prose 生成器会产出坐席不会用的工具**。**用结构化大纲生成器重做会产出符合坐席实际工作流的工具**。

**PM 诚实估计成本**。**继续当前方式**: **可能再四周 prompt 工程把 eval 分数从 75% 推到 85%, 然后不热情上线、可能三个月内被抛弃**。**重做**: **可能三周重做 prompt 策略和输出格式, 同时保留 eval set、数据集成、坐席关系、基础设施**。**成本对比清楚**: **重做比继续快, 产出实际被用的工具**。

**PM 把这带给运营 sponsor**。**措辞是上面描述的三部分结构**。**"通过过去六周的工作, 我们学到了关于货运客服怎么工作的重要东西。我们建议改变方式。这是我们保留的、这是我们重做的、这是成本"**。

**运营 sponsor 的反应一开始是防御的**。**她在内部倡导精致 prose 方式, 感觉个人投资**。**PM 耐心**。**措辞被重复**: **这不是失败, 这是发现**。**到目前为止的工作产出了让新方式可能的学习**。**运营 sponsor, 给了时间想, 同意建议**。

**对 executive sponsor 的沟通在下次 steering committee 发生, 运营 sponsor 主讲, PM 支持**。**消息一样**: **我们学到了重要的东西, 我们在相应调整**。**Executive sponsor 接受建议, 要求更新的时间线**。

**团队第二天被告知**。**反应混合**。**技术负责人, 一直对 "产出精致 prose 所需的 prompt 复杂性" 越来越不舒服, 松了口气**。**两位工程师, 在 prompt 设计里投资重, 一开始失望**。**PM 明确承认被丢弃的工作**: **eval set 留着、数据管道留着、坐席反馈 pattern 在告知新方式**。**被丢弃的工作没浪费, 它产出了让新方式对的理解**。

**重做在 16 天里发生**。**新的结构化大纲生成器被建造和测试, 用同样的 eval set, 达到相似的 eval 分数, 产出坐席主动想用的输出**。**上线在第 13 周发生, 比原计划提前一周**。**坐席上线后持续使用工具**。

**有意思的对照是没停下会发生什么**。**团队会再花四周改善 prompt 产出更精致 prose, 上线一个坐席觉得笨重的工具, 看着上线一个月内使用下降**。**项目会在六个月后被分类为边际失败**。

AI 交付的启示: **在信号支持时停下和重做的意愿, 是这一行最资深的技能之一**。**它需要诚实看不工作的东西、愿意丢弃不服务项目的工作、擅长沟通决定**。**做好的停下和重做加强而不是削弱客户关系**, 因为**客户看到一个团队重视 "得到对的结果" 多于 "保持进度的外观"**。**那是一份深度有价值的声誉去建立**。
