**Definition**

In most AI projects, legal and security functions are engaged late: usually when the project is nearly ready to launch and a sign off is needed. By that point, the architecture is fixed, the data flows are committed, and the team is exposed to whatever objections legal or security raises. The discipline of bringing legal and security into the project in week one, before architecture is committed, turns these functions from late-stage blockers into early-stage collaborators. The cost is one or two extra meetings in week one. The payoff is avoiding the painful re-architecture or launch delay that happens when they engage late.

**Why it matters in delivery**

Legal and security teams are, in most client organisations, structurally cautious. Their job is to identify risks and require mitigation, and they are measured on what does not go wrong, not on what gets shipped. When they engage in week eight, they often discover concerns that should have been addressed in week one: data flows that violate the privacy policy, model providers that have not been security-reviewed, vendor terms that have not been negotiated, audit logs that do not exist. Each of these can delay launch by weeks.

The deeper issue is that late engagement creates an adversarial dynamic. The team feels legal and security are blocking their work. Legal and security feel the team has ignored them and is now trying to push past their concerns. The relationship becomes transactional and slow. Early engagement creates the opposite dynamic: legal and security feel respected and included, the team understands the constraints, and the design accommodates the constraints from the start.

**How it works**

The week one engagement has three parts.

*Part 1: A short introductory meeting with each function, in week one.* 30 to 45 minutes each. The purpose is not to make decisions, it is to introduce the project, listen to their initial concerns, and agree on a working cadence. The agenda is simple: here is what we are building, here is what we think the legal or security implications are, what are we missing, when should we come back to you next.

The single most important behaviour in this meeting is to listen more than talk. Legal and security people have seen many AI projects, often with poor outcomes. They have views. Inviting those views early is the work.

*Part 2: A jointly-produced constraints document by week two.* The team writes a one or two page document listing the constraints the project will operate under. Data handling rules. Model provider approvals. Audit and logging requirements. Disclosure obligations to end users. Whatever is relevant. The document is reviewed by legal and security, edited, and signed off. It is then the reference point for the rest of the project.

The constraints document protects everyone. The team has a clear specification of what to build against. Legal and security have a written commitment from the team. Future reviews can be against the document rather than against memory or impression.

*Part 3: A scheduled cadence of check-ins.* Once a month is typical, more often near launch. The check-ins are short (15 to 30 minutes) and focused: here is what has changed since last time, here are the open questions, when is the next check-in. The cadence keeps the conversation warm, so the launch readiness review at week 13 is not the first conversation about a critical issue.

**What to discuss with legal**

Legal concerns in AI projects cluster around a few topics, almost regardless of the specific use case.

| Topic | Question to bring to legal |
|---|---|
| Data rights | Do we have the right to use this data for this purpose? Any consent or notification gaps? |
| Model provider terms | Are the terms of the chosen model provider acceptable for our use case, especially around data handling and IP? |
| Intellectual property | Who owns the outputs the model generates? Any infringement risk? |
| Disclosure | Do we need to tell end users that AI is involved? Under what circumstances? |
| Audit and explainability | What records do we need to keep of AI decisions, for what duration, accessible to whom? |
| Liability | If the AI gets something wrong and causes harm, where does liability sit? |

Not every project needs all six. The conversation determines which are in scope.

**What to discuss with security**

Security concerns cluster around a different set of topics.

| Topic | Question to bring to security |
|---|---|
| Data flow | Where does the data go, through what channels, with what encryption? |
| Authentication | How do users authenticate, how are credentials managed? |
| Model provider security | Has the provider been security-reviewed, and what is the data handling agreement? |
| Prompt injection | What is the threat model for malicious inputs, and what is the mitigation? |
| Output safety | What is the worst case output, and what safeguards prevent it from reaching users or downstream systems? |
| Audit logging | What gets logged, where, with what retention, accessible to whom? |
| Incident response | If something goes wrong, what is the response plan, and who is on call? |

Like the legal list, not every project needs all of these. The conversation determines scope.

**PM decision points**

- Have I scheduled the legal and security introductory meetings in week one, or am I planning to engage them later?
- Is there a written constraints document, signed off by legal and security, that I can refer to as the project progresses?
- Do I have a regular cadence of check-ins, or am I only contacting legal and security when I need approval?

**Watch outs**

- Legal and security teams are often understaffed and overcommitted. Requesting time on their calendar with two days' notice will not work. Plan two weeks ahead.
- Avoid presenting legal and security with a finished design. Present a sketch or a set of options, and ask them to help shape it. The relationship and the design both improve.
- Some clients try to bypass legal and security on AI projects because they are seen as slow. Resist this. Bypassing them creates a much bigger problem at launch, when they discover the work was done without their involvement and demand a full re-review.

**Takeaway: legal and security are most useful when engaged in week one, not week eight. Hold a short introductory meeting with each, produce a jointly-owned constraints document by week two, and maintain a monthly check-in cadence. The cost is small. The protection is large.**

**Qantas story**

Imagine kicking off a Qantas AI project to build a tool that helps the operations team draft customer notifications during disruptions. The team is keen to start the technical work. The operating sponsor is supportive but busy. The legal and security teams have not been formally introduced to the project, though they are mentioned in the SOW. The temptation is to focus on the build and deal with legal and security when needed.

The PM resists this temptation. In the first week, the PM contacts the senior legal counsel for customer operations and the head of cyber security for IT applications. The pitch in each case is the same: "we are starting a new AI project, I would like 30 minutes to introduce it and hear your initial concerns, before we make any architectural decisions". Both agree to meetings in week one.

The legal meeting surfaces three concerns. First, customer-facing communications drafted by AI need to be reviewed by humans before they are sent, because Qantas has commitments under consumer law about the accuracy of disruption information. Second, the model provider's terms must include a commitment that Qantas customer data is not used for model training. Third, customers should be told, somewhere in the communication, that it was drafted with AI assistance, to avoid future regulatory exposure. None of these are surprising to legal, but they would have been late surprises to the team.

The security meeting surfaces four concerns. First, the connection to the customer database needs to go through Qantas's API gateway, not direct, for logging and rate limiting. Second, the chosen model provider must be on the approved vendor list, which requires a security review (estimated 4 weeks). Third, prompt inputs and model outputs must be logged for 90 days, with access controls. Fourth, there must be an incident response plan for cases where the model produces inappropriate output, with a 30 minute response time during business hours.

By week two, the team has produced a constraints document that incorporates all seven of these concerns. The document is reviewed by both legal and security, edited slightly, and signed off. The team's architecture is updated to accommodate: an API gateway connection, a human review step before any customer communication is sent, audit logging with 90 day retention, an AI disclosure line in the communications, and a vendor approval process started for the model provider.

The interesting test comes in week 11, during the launch readiness review. The legal counsel and security lead both join the review. Both confirm that the constraints document has been met. There are no surprises, no last-minute objections, no launch delay. The review takes 45 minutes instead of the multi-week back-and-forth that would have occurred if legal and security had been engaged only at this point.

The parallel comparison is with another Qantas AI project of similar size that ran in the same year. That project engaged legal in week 10 and security in week 9. Legal raised three concerns that required prompt re-design (4 weeks of rework). Security required a logging change that meant an architectural retrofit (2 weeks of rework). The project launched 5 weeks late. The project team was burned out from the rework. Legal and security felt their concerns had been ignored. The next AI project at Qantas had a frosty start, because legal and security were now skeptical of AI teams.

The lesson for AI delivery is that legal and security are not antagonists, they are essential collaborators whose involvement compounds over time. Engaging them in week one is a small investment that produces large returns: faster decisions, smoother launch, warmer relationships, and a stronger reputation for the team within the client organisation. The temptation to defer them is real but should be resisted. The first conversation with legal and security in week one is one of the highest-leverage uses of an hour in the entire project.

**定义**

大多数 AI 项目里, 法务和安全职能被晚 engage: 通常是项目快要上线、需要签字时。那时候, 架构定了、数据流承诺了、团队对法务或安全提出的任何反对都暴露在外。第 1 周就把法务和安全拉进项目、在架构承诺之前的纪律, 把这些职能从晚期阻塞者转变成早期协作者。成本是第 1 周一两次额外的会议。回报是避免他们晚期 engage 时发生的痛苦重新架构或上线延迟。

**为何在交付中重要**

大多数客户组织里, 法务和安全团队结构性谨慎。他们的工作是识别风险、要求缓解, 他们被衡量的是 "什么没出错", 不是 "什么交付了"。他们第 8 周 engage 时, 经常发现本应在第 1 周处理的关切: 违反隐私政策的数据流、没经过安全 review 的模型厂商、没谈判过的供应商条款、不存在的审计日志。这些每一个都能把上线推迟几周。

更深的问题是, 晚 engage 制造对抗动力学。团队觉得法务和安全在阻塞他们的工作。法务和安全觉得团队忽视了他们、现在在试图绕过他们的关切。关系变得交易性而缓慢。早期 engage 制造相反的动力学: 法务和安全感觉被尊重和被纳入, 团队理解约束, 设计从开始就容纳约束。

**运作原理**

第 1 周 engagement 有三部分。

*第一部分: 第 1 周跟每个职能开一次简短的介绍会议*。**每个 30 到 45 分钟**。**目的不是做决定, 是介绍项目、听他们最初的关切、商定一个工作节奏**。**议程简单**: **这是我们在建的、这是我们认为的法务或安全含义、我们漏了什么、什么时候下次回到你们这里**。

这次会议里最重要的单一行为是听多于说。法务和安全的人见过很多 AI 项目, 经常以差的结果告终。**他们有看法**。早期邀请那些看法, 就是工作。

*第二部分: 第 2 周共同产出一份约束文档*。**团队写一份一两页的文档, 列项目将在什么约束下运行**。**数据处理规则**。**模型厂商批准**。**审计和日志要求**。**对最终用户的披露义务**。**任何相关的东西**。**文档由法务和安全 review、编辑、签字**。**它然后是项目其余部分的参考点**。

约束文档保护所有人。团队有清晰的规格, 知道要建什么。法务和安全拿到团队的书面承诺。未来的 review 是对照文档, 而不是对照记忆或印象。

*第三部分: 一个定期的 check-in 节奏*。**每月一次是典型的, 接近上线时更频繁**。**Check-in 是短的 (15 到 30 分钟) 且聚焦的**: **这是上次以来变化的、这是开放的问题、下次 check-in 是什么时候**。**节奏让对话保持温热**, **这样第 13 周的上线 readiness review 不是关于一个关键问题的第一次对话**。

**跟法务讨论什么**

AI 项目的法务关切围绕几个主题, 几乎跟具体 use case 无关。

| 主题 | 带给法务的问题 |
|---|---|
| 数据权利 | 我们有权把这些数据用于这个目的吗? 有任何同意或通知缺口吗? |
| 模型厂商条款 | 选定模型厂商的条款对我们的 use case 可接受吗, 尤其是数据处理和 IP? |
| 知识产权 | 模型生成的输出, 谁拥有? 有侵权风险吗? |
| 披露 | 我们需要告诉最终用户涉及 AI 吗? 在什么情况下? |
| 审计和可解释性 | AI 决策需要保留什么记录、保留多久、谁能访问? |
| 责任 | 如果 AI 出错造成伤害, 责任在哪? |

不是每个项目都需要全部六个。对话决定哪些在范围内。

**跟安全讨论什么**

安全关切围绕一组不同的主题。

| 主题 | 带给安全的问题 |
|---|---|
| 数据流 | 数据去哪、通过什么渠道、加密怎样? |
| 认证 | 用户怎么认证, 凭证怎么管? |
| 模型厂商安全 | 厂商被安全 review 过吗, 数据处理协议是什么? |
| Prompt injection | 恶意输入的威胁模型是什么, 缓解是什么? |
| 输出安全 | 最坏情况输出是什么, 什么保护防止它达到用户或下游系统? |
| 审计日志 | 什么被记录、在哪、保留多久、谁能访问? |
| 事件响应 | 如果出岔子, 响应计划是什么, 谁在 on call? |

跟法务清单一样, 不是每个项目都需要全部。**对话决定范围**。

**PM 决策点**

- 我**有没有把法务和安全的介绍会议安排在第 1 周**, 还是计划之后再 engage 他们?
- **有没有一份书面的约束文档, 由法务和安全签字**, **我能在项目推进时引用**?
- 我**有没有定期 check-in 节奏**, 还是**只在需要批准时才联系法务和安全**?

**注意事项**

- **法务和安全团队经常人手不足、超负荷**。**提前两天约他们的日历不会奏效**。**提前两周计划**。
- **避免给法务和安全呈现一份完成的设计**。**呈现一个草图或一组选项, 请他们帮助塑形**。**关系和设计都改善**。
- **有些客户试图在 AI 项目上绕过法务和安全, 因为他们被视为慢**。**抵抗这个**。**绕过他们在上线时制造大得多的问题**, **当他们发现工作是在没他们参与的情况下完成的、要求一次完整的重审**。

**要点总结: 法务和安全在第 1 周 engage 时最有用, 不是第 8 周。跟每个开一次短介绍会议, 第 2 周产出一份共同拥有的约束文档, 维护每月 check-in 节奏。成本小。保护大。**

**Qantas 案例**

设想你启动一个 Qantas AI 项目, 建一个工具帮运营团队在中断时起草客户通知。团队迫不及待要开始技术工作。运营 sponsor 支持但忙。法务和安全团队还没正式被介绍给项目, 虽然他们在 SOW 里被提到。诱惑是聚焦建造, 需要时再处理法务和安全。

PM **抵抗这个诱惑**。第 1 周, PM 联系客户运营的资深法律顾问, 以及 IT 应用的网络安全负责人。每次的话术一样: "我们正在启动一个新的 AI 项目, 我想要 30 分钟介绍它、听你最初的关切, 在我们做任何架构决定之前"。双方都同意第 1 周开会。

法务会议浮出三个关切。**第一**, AI 起草的面向客户的沟通, 在发送前需要被人审, 因为 Qantas 在消费者法下有关于中断信息准确性的承诺。**第二**, 模型厂商的条款必须包括 Qantas 客户数据不被用于模型训练的承诺。**第三**, 客户应该在沟通中的某处被告知, 这是用 AI 协助起草的, 以避免未来的监管暴露。这些对法务来说都不意外, 但对团队来说会是迟到的意外。

安全会议浮出四个关切。**第一**, 跟客户数据库的连接需要走 Qantas 的 API gateway, 不是直接, 为了日志和限流。**第二**, 选定的模型厂商必须在批准供应商清单上, 这需要安全 review (估计 4 周)。**第三**, prompt 输入和模型输出必须被记录 90 天, 带访问控制。**第四**, 必须有事件响应计划, 应对模型产出不合适内容的情况, 工作时间内 30 分钟响应时间。

第 2 周, 团队产出一份纳入这七个关切的约束文档。文档被法务和安全 review, 略加编辑, 签字。团队的架构被更新以容纳: API gateway 连接、客户沟通发送前的人审步骤、90 天保留的审计日志、沟通中的 AI 披露行、为模型厂商启动供应商批准流程。

有意思的测试出现在第 11 周, 上线 readiness review 期间。法律顾问和安全负责人都加入 review。双方都确认约束文档被满足。没有意外、没有最后一刻的反对、没有上线延迟。Review 用 45 分钟, 而不是如果法务和安全只在这个时间点才被 engage 时会发生的多周来回。

平行对照是同年另一个相似规模的 Qantas AI 项目。那个项目第 10 周 engage 法务、第 9 周 engage 安全。法务提出三个需要 prompt 重设计的关切 (4 周返工)。安全要求一个日志变化, 意味着架构改造 (2 周返工)。项目晚上线 5 周。项目团队因为返工 burnout。法务和安全觉得他们的关切被忽视。Qantas 下一个 AI 项目有一个冷淡的开始, 因为**法务和安全现在对 AI 团队抱持怀疑**。

AI 交付的启示: 法务和安全不是对手, 是必要的协作者, 他们的参与随时间复利。第 1 周 engage 他们是一笔小投资, 产出大回报: 更快的决定、更顺的上线、更温的关系、团队在客户组织里更强的声誉。推迟他们的诱惑是真实的, 但应该被抵抗。第 1 周跟法务和安全的第一次对话, 是整个项目里一小时使用方式中杠杆最高的之一。
