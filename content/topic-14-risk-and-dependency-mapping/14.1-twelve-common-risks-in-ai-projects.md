**Definition**

Every AI project carries a set of risks that, while not identical across projects, fall into a small number of recurring patterns. This item enumerates twelve of the most common, grouped into four categories: technical, data, organisational, and regulatory. The goal is not to be exhaustive (no risk taxonomy can be), but to give the team a vocabulary and a checklist so that no recurring risk is overlooked at project start.

**Why it matters in delivery**

The most expensive risks in AI delivery are the ones nobody named in week one. By the time they materialise, the project is already committed, the team is already deep in the build, and the cost of mitigation is several times what it would have been if the risk had been raised early. The discipline of running a structured risk identification exercise in the first two weeks, against a known list, catches most of these before they become problems.

A second reason: clients in many industries are comfortable with risk conversations from traditional projects (procurement risk, vendor risk, schedule risk) but unfamiliar with AI-specific risks (model deprecation, prompt drift, hallucination in regulated contexts). Bringing a known list of AI-specific risks signals that the team has done this before, which builds credibility with the client.

**How it works**

The twelve risks below, grouped by category. Each has a brief description, a typical signal that it is present, and a default mitigation direction. The list is meant to be walked through in a two hour workshop with the team and the operating sponsor, marking each risk as present, partially present, or not applicable.

**Technical risks**

| # | Risk | Signal | Default mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Model capability gap | The use case requires reasoning, accuracy, or context length the current generation cannot reliably do | Reframe scope, narrow the use case, add human review |
| 2 | Provider deprecation or change | The system depends on a specific model version that may be retired or repriced | Abstract the provider behind an interface, monitor announcements, plan migration |
| 3 | Latency or cost at scale | The system works in pilot but the unit economics break at full user load | Run scale modelling early, define cost guardrails before build |

**Data risks**

| # | Risk | Signal | Default mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | Data not available in time | Required data lives in a system the team cannot access fast enough | Identify in week 1, escalate immediately, prepare synthetic fallback |
| 5 | Data quality below threshold | Available data is messy, inconsistent, or missing key fields | Plan a data preparation phase, communicate impact on timeline |
| 6 | Data rights or privacy constraints | The data exists but cannot legally be used for this purpose | Engage legal in week 1, define what can and cannot be used |

**Organisational risks**

| # | Risk | Signal | Default mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7 | Sponsor turnover | The executive sponsor changes role or leaves during the project | Build relationships with second-level stakeholders, document agreements |
| 8 | Operating team not engaged | The people who will use the system are not in the requirements or workshops | Insist on their involvement, escalate if blocked |
| 9 | Competing internal initiative | Another team is building something that overlaps or contradicts | Surface the overlap, coordinate or escalate to common authority |

**Regulatory and external risks**

| # | Risk | Signal | Default mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | Regulatory uncertainty | The relevant rules for AI in this context are not yet clear | Document the assumed regulatory position, plan for change |
| 11 | Public failure exposure | The system could fail in ways that are externally visible and brand damaging | Build conservative defaults, human review, kill switch |
| 12 | Third party dependency | A critical part of the system depends on a vendor, API, or partner outside the team's control | Map the dependency, negotiate SLAs, plan fallback |

**How to run the identification workshop**

The workshop is short, structured, and inclusive. Two hours, ideally in person, with the delivery team and the operating sponsor. The agenda:

1. Walk through each of the twelve risks. For each, ask: present, partially present, or not applicable to this project? (10 minutes per risk, 2 hours total.)
2. For each present or partially present risk, capture: what specifically makes it present, what would the impact be if it materialised, what is the rough probability today, and who could mitigate it.
3. Identify the top three risks by combined impact and probability. These are the ones that go to the steering committee.

The output of the workshop is a one page risk register (covered in detail in 14.5). It is signed off by the delivery team and the operating sponsor. It is reviewed every two weeks.

**Risks not on the list**

The twelve risks are common, not exhaustive. Every project has at least one or two risks that are specific to it: a relationship that is fragile, a system that is unusually fragile, a stakeholder with an idiosyncratic preference. The workshop should always end with "what else is not on this list that we should worry about". Often the most important risk for a specific project is the one that does not fit the standard categories.

**PM decision points**

- Have I run the twelve risk workshop in week one or week two, with the operating sponsor present?
- For each present risk, have I captured the specifics, not just the category?
- Is the workshop output documented in a form that survives team turnover?

**Watch outs**

- Resist the temptation to mark everything as a risk. A list of 30 risks is impossible to manage. Focus on the 5 to 8 that genuinely apply, at meaningful probability and impact.
- Some risks are uncomfortable to raise (e.g. sponsor turnover, competing initiative). Raise them anyway. The cost of not raising them is much higher than the discomfort of the conversation.
- A risk that was identified but never mitigated is worse than one that was never identified, because it creates a documented audit trail of negligence. If a risk is on the register, there must be an owner and an action.

**Takeaway: AI projects share a recurring set of risks. Twelve common ones, in four categories, cover most of what tends to go wrong. Walk through them in a two hour workshop in week one or two, mark each as present or not, focus on the top three to five for active management, and capture the result in a risk register.**

**Qantas story**

Imagine kicking off a Qantas AI project to build a flight crew rostering assistant: a tool that helps the crew planning team match available crew to flight schedules, accounting for qualifications, fatigue rules, and crew preferences. The SOW is signed, the team is keen, the operating sponsor is engaged. The temptation is to skip a formal risk workshop and "we will deal with risks as they come up".

The discipline is to do the two hour workshop anyway. The team and the operating sponsor (a senior crew planning manager) walk through the twelve risks.

Risk 1 (model capability gap): partially present. The use case involves constraint satisfaction across hundreds of crew members and dozens of rules, which is at the edge of what LLMs do reliably. Mitigation: hybrid approach with the LLM handling natural language interaction and a deterministic solver doing the constraint satisfaction.

Risk 2 (provider deprecation): present at low probability. The team plans to use a specific model version, but six months in production. Mitigation: abstract the provider, set up monitoring on announcements.

Risk 3 (latency or cost at scale): not applicable at v1, the user base is small. Note to reassess at v2.

Risk 4 (data not available in time): strongly present. Crew qualification data lives in a legacy crew management system, and the data export is owned by a small team with a six week queue. This is the biggest risk on the list.

Risk 5 (data quality): present. The crew preferences data has been entered by crew members in inconsistent formats over a decade. Significant cleaning required.

Risk 6 (data rights): partially present. Crew personal data is subject to fatigue rules and union agreements. Legal involvement required.

Risk 7 (sponsor turnover): low probability. Sponsor has been in role for years.

Risk 8 (operating team not engaged): not present. Operating team is co-located with the delivery team and meets weekly.

Risk 9 (competing initiative): present. Another team is upgrading the underlying crew management system, with a timeline that overlaps the AI project. Risk of integration conflict.

Risk 10 (regulatory uncertainty): present. The use of AI in safety-critical decisions like crew rostering is subject to evolving aviation regulator guidance. The team and client need to take a position.

Risk 11 (public failure exposure): partially present. A crew rostering error is visible to crew but not to the public, unless it results in a flight cancellation. Mitigation: conservative confidence thresholds, human review of every roster before publication.

Risk 12 (third party dependency): not applicable, the system is internal.

The workshop ends with the "what else" question. The operating sponsor raises a thirteenth risk: the team has not yet engaged the crew unions, and union acceptance is a hard requirement for any change to crew rostering process. This risk is not in the twelve, but is the second highest risk for this specific project.

The top three risks identified for active management are: data not available in time (risk 4), competing initiative around the crew management system (risk 9), and union engagement (the thirteenth, project-specific risk). Each is assigned an owner, an action, and a check-in cadence.

In week six, the data risk materialises in a manageable form: the crew management team agrees to an early export of a subset of the data, allowing the AI team to start work in parallel rather than waiting for the full export. This concession happened because the risk had been raised early, escalated to the right people, and tracked in the register. Without the workshop, the data would have been requested in week four, the six week queue would have started then, and the project would have lost six weeks.

The lesson for AI delivery is that risk identification is not paranoia, it is preparation. The same twelve risks recur across most projects. Running a structured workshop against this known list, in week one or two, catches most risks before they harden into problems. The two hour cost is one of the highest-leverage investments in the entire delivery.

**定义**

每一个 AI 项目都携带一组风险, **虽然不是完全一样**, **但落在少数几个反复出现的 pattern 里**。**这一节列举十二种最常见的, 分四类**: **技术、数据、组织、监管**。**目标不是穷尽** (**没有任何风险分类法能做到**), **而是给团队一份词汇表和 checklist, 让项目开始时没有任何反复出现的风险被忽略**。

**为何在交付中重要**

AI 交付里**最贵的风险**, 是**第一周没人指出名字的那些**。**等它们显现时**, **项目已经承诺、团队已经深入建造、缓解的成本是早提出来的几倍**。**针对一份已知清单, 在前两周跑一次结构化的风险识别练习, 抓住其中大多数, 在它们变成问题之前**。

**第二个原因**: **很多行业的客户对传统项目的风险对话是舒服的** (**采购风险、供应商风险、进度风险**), **但对 AI 特有的风险陌生** (**模型 deprecation、prompt 漂移、监管场景下的幻觉**)。**带一份已知的 AI 特有风险清单, 传递 "团队以前做过这件事" 的信号, 给客户建立可信度**。

**运作原理**

**下面是十二种风险, 按类别分**。**每个都有简短描述、一个表明它出现的典型信号、一个默认的缓解方向**。**清单是用来在一个两小时的 workshop 里走过的**, **团队和运营 sponsor 一起**, **把每个风险标为 "存在、部分存在、或不适用"**。

**技术风险**

| # | 风险 | 信号 | 默认缓解 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 模型能力差距 | use case 要求当前模型代际无法可靠做到的推理、准确率、或上下文长度 | 重新框架范围、收窄 use case、加入人工 review |
| 2 | 厂商 deprecation 或变更 | 系统依赖一个可能被退役或重新定价的特定模型版本 | 用接口抽象厂商、监控公告、规划迁移 |
| 3 | 规模下的 latency 或成本 | pilot 阶段能工作, 但全量用户负载下单位经济学崩了 | 早期做规模建模, 在建造前定义成本护栏 |

**数据风险**

| # | 风险 | 信号 | 默认缓解 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 数据来不及拿到 | 需要的数据住在团队访问不够快的系统里 | 第 1 周识别、立即升级、准备合成数据兜底 |
| 5 | 数据质量低于阈值 | 可拿到的数据脏、不一致、或缺关键字段 | 规划一个数据准备阶段、沟通对时间线的影响 |
| 6 | 数据权利或隐私约束 | 数据存在但**法律上不能用于这个目的** | 第 1 周拉法务参与、定义什么能用什么不能用 |

**组织风险**

| # | 风险 | 信号 | 默认缓解 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7 | Sponsor 流失 | Executive sponsor 项目中途换岗或离开 | 跟二级 stakeholder 建立关系、把协议文档化 |
| 8 | 运营团队不 engaged | 未来要用系统的人不在需求或 workshop 里 | 坚持要他们参与, 卡住就升级 |
| 9 | 竞争性内部 initiative | 另一个团队在建一个重叠或矛盾的东西 | 把重叠浮出来, 协调或升级到共同上级 |

**监管和外部风险**

| # | 风险 | 信号 | 默认缓解 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 监管不确定性 | AI 在这个场景里相关的规则还不清晰 | 文档化假设的监管立场, 规划应对变化 |
| 11 | 公开失败暴露 | 系统可能以**外部可见、损害品牌**的方式失败 | 建保守默认、人工 review、kill switch |
| 12 | 第三方依赖 | 系统的关键部分依赖团队控制之外的供应商、API、或合作伙伴 | 把依赖画出来、谈判 SLA、规划兜底 |

**怎么跑识别 workshop**

**Workshop 是短、结构化、包容的**。**两小时**, **最好面对面**, **交付团队加运营 sponsor**。**议程**:

1. **走过十二种风险中的每一种**。**对每一种问**: **对本项目存在、部分存在、还是不适用?** **(每条 10 分钟, 共 2 小时。)**
2. **对每一条 "存在" 或 "部分存在" 的风险, 记录**: **具体什么让它存在、显现的影响是什么、今天的粗略概率、谁能缓解**。
3. **按 "影响 × 概率" 综合排出前三**。**这三个上 steering committee**。

**Workshop 的产物是一页风险登记表** (**详细见 14.5**)。**由交付团队和运营 sponsor 签字**。**每两周 review**。

**清单上没有的风险**

**这十二种是常见的, 不是穷尽的**。**每个项目都至少有一两个是它特有的**: **一段脆弱的关系、一个不寻常脆弱的系统、一位有特殊偏好的 stakeholder**。**Workshop 应该总是以 "这份清单上没列、但我们应该担心的东西是什么" 结束**。**经常**, **一个具体项目最重要的风险, 就是那个不符合标准类别的**。

**PM 决策点**

- 我**有没有在第一或第二周跑过这次十二项风险 workshop**, **运营 sponsor 在场**?
- 对每一条存在的风险, 我**有没有捕捉到具体内容, 而不只是类别**?
- Workshop 的产物**有没有以能挺过团队成员更替的形式被文档化**?

**注意事项**

- **抵抗把所有东西都标为风险的诱惑**。**30 条风险的清单不可能管理**。**聚焦真正适用的 5 到 8 条, 概率和影响都有意义**。
- **有些风险提起来不舒服** (**例如, sponsor 流失、竞争性 initiative**)。**还是要提**。**不提的代价比对话的不适高得多**。
- **被识别但从未被缓解的风险, 比从未被识别的风险更糟**, 因为**它创造了一份有文档的失职审计轨迹**。**如果风险在登记表上, 必须有 owner 和 action**。

**要点总结: AI 项目共享一组反复出现的风险。十二种常见的, 四个类别, 覆盖大多数会出错的东西。在第一或第二周用两小时 workshop 走一遍, 每条标为存在或不存在, 聚焦前三到五条做主动管理, 把结果捕捉到风险登记表。**

**Qantas 案例**

设想你启动一个 Qantas AI 项目, **建一个机组排班助手**: **一个工具帮机组规划团队把可用机组匹配到航班时刻, 考虑资质、疲劳规则、机组偏好**。SOW 签了, 团队有干劲, 运营 sponsor engaged。**诱惑是跳过正式风险 workshop, "风险来了再说"**。

**纪律是无论如何做这两小时 workshop**。**团队和运营 sponsor (一位资深机组规划经理) 走过十二项风险**。

**风险 1 (模型能力差距)**: **部分存在**。**use case 涉及跨数百名机组和数十条规则的约束满足, 处于 LLM 可靠做到的边缘**。**缓解**: **混合方式, LLM 处理自然语言交互, 确定性求解器做约束满足**。

**风险 2 (厂商 deprecation)**: **存在, 概率低**。**团队计划用一个特定模型版本, 六个月生产**。**缓解**: **抽象厂商接口, 监控公告**。

**风险 3 (规模下的 latency 或成本)**: **v1 不适用, 用户群小**。**记录: v2 时重评**。

**风险 4 (数据来不及拿到)**: **强存在**。**机组资质数据住在一个旧的机组管理系统里, 数据导出由一个小团队负责, 排队六周**。**这是清单上最大的风险**。

**风险 5 (数据质量)**: **存在**。**机组偏好数据十年来由机组成员以不一致的格式录入**。**需要显著的清洗**。

**风险 6 (数据权利)**: **部分存在**。**机组个人数据受疲劳规则和工会协议约束**。**需要法务参与**。

**风险 7 (sponsor 流失)**: **概率低**。**Sponsor 在岗多年**。

**风险 8 (运营团队不 engaged)**: **不存在**。**运营团队跟交付团队同地, 每周开会**。

**风险 9 (竞争性 initiative)**: **存在**。**另一个团队在升级底层的机组管理系统, 时间线跟 AI 项目重叠**。**集成冲突风险**。

**风险 10 (监管不确定性)**: **存在**。**AI 在机组排班这种安全关键决策中的使用, 受演进中的航空监管指导约束**。**团队和客户需要表态**。

**风险 11 (公开失败暴露)**: **部分存在**。**排班错误对机组可见、对公众不可见, 除非导致航班取消**。**缓解**: **保守的置信阈值, 每份排班发布前人工 review**。

**风险 12 (第三方依赖)**: **不适用, 系统是内部的**。

**Workshop 以 "还有什么" 结束**。**运营 sponsor 提出第十三条风险**: **团队还没 engage 机组工会, 而工会接受是任何机组排班流程变更的硬性要求**。**这条风险不在十二条里, 但是这个具体项目的第二高风险**。

**被识别为主动管理前三的风险是**: **数据来不及拿到 (风险 4)、机组管理系统升级的竞争性 initiative (风险 9)、工会 engagement (第十三, 项目特有的风险)**。**每条都被分配 owner、action、check-in 节奏**。

**第六周, 数据风险以可管理的形式显现**: **机组管理团队同意提前导出数据的一个子集, 让 AI 团队能并行开始工作, 而不是等完整导出**。**这次让步发生, 是因为风险被早提出来、被升级到对的人、被在登记表里追踪**。**没有 workshop, 数据请求会在第四周提出来, 六周排队从那时开始, 项目会丢六周**。

AI 交付的启示: **风险识别不是偏执, 是准备**。**同样的十二种风险在大多数项目里反复出现**。**针对这份已知清单跑一次结构化 workshop, 在第一或第二周, 抓住大多数风险, 在它们硬化成问题之前**。**那两小时的成本是整个交付里杠杆最高的几项投资之一**。
