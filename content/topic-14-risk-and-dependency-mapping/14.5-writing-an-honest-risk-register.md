**Definition**

A risk register is the standing document that lists the identified risks on a project, their current status, who owns them, and what is being done about them. In most organisations the risk register is a bureaucratic artifact that gets filled out, signed off, and ignored. The discipline of this item is to produce a risk register that is short, honest, current, and shared with the client, in a form that actually helps people manage risk rather than just document its existence.

**Why it matters in delivery**

The risk register exists for one reason: to make sure no important risk is forgotten or under-managed. A register that does not achieve this is worse than no register, because it creates the illusion of risk management without the substance. Three pathologies make most risk registers useless: too long, too old, and too sanitised.

Too long: 30 to 50 risks listed, of which only 5 are actually being managed. Nobody reads it. Too old: last updated three weeks ago, statuses no longer accurate, owners no longer in their roles. Out of date. Too sanitised: risks worded to avoid alarming anyone, with status updates that say "monitoring" regardless of what is actually happening. The register tells nobody anything they did not already know.

A useful risk register is the opposite of all three: short, current, and honest. It is updated weekly or bi-weekly, contains 5 to 10 actively managed risks, and uses plain language that makes the actual state of each risk clear.

**How it works**

A useful risk register has the following structure. It fits on one page, which is the point.

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| ID | A short identifier, useful for cross-referencing |
| Title | One line in plain language, what the risk is |
| Category | Technical, data, organisational, regulatory (from 14.1) |
| Control type | Control, influence, track (from 14.2) |
| Probability | Low, medium, high, with a brief note on why |
| Impact | Low, medium, high, with a brief note on consequence |
| Owner | One named person on the delivery team |
| Status | Mitigated, in progress, escalating, materialised, retired |
| Last updated | Date of last meaningful update to the row |
| Next action | The specific next action with date and owner |

10 fields per risk, 5 to 10 risks, all on one page. The "next action" column is the most important: every active risk must have a specific next action with a date. Without it, the risk is not being managed.

**The status vocabulary**

Status is where most registers go wrong. The vocabulary needs to be specific and honest.

| Status | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mitigated | The risk has been addressed, with evidence. Either prevented, eliminated, or the impact is now acceptable. |
| In progress | Active mitigation work is happening, on schedule. |
| Escalating | The mitigation is not working, or new information has increased the risk. Needs senior attention. |
| Materialised | The risk has occurred and is now an issue, not a risk. |
| Retired | The risk is no longer relevant, with reason. |

What is not allowed: "monitoring" (too vague), "TBD" (lazy), "ongoing" (meaningless). If the status would be one of these, the risk is not being managed and needs either a real action or to be retired.

**How to share the register with the client**

Two common approaches. The first: keep the register internal to the delivery team and only share filtered or summarised versions with the client. This is sometimes appropriate, especially for commercially sensitive risks, but it has costs. The client cannot help with risks they do not know about, and the team's escalations have less weight if the client only sees them at moments of crisis.

The second: share the register openly with the operating sponsor, and a redacted version with the broader steering committee. This is usually the right default. The operating sponsor sees everything. The steering committee sees the risks that involve them. The team retains some discretion over highly sensitive items (e.g. risks involving specific individuals' performance) but discloses the existence of those items.

Sharing the register openly has several second-order effects. The operating sponsor becomes a partner in risk management, not just a recipient of bad news. The client's view of the team's competence usually rises because clients are not used to seeing this level of structure. And the team is forced to keep the register honest, because someone external is reading it.

**What goes wrong**

Three patterns that destroy register usefulness.

*Risk inflation.* The team lists every conceivable risk to look thorough. The register grows to 30 items. None of them have specific next actions. The register becomes a wall of noise. The fix: cap at 10 actively managed risks. Anything else is either parked or genuinely not a current concern.

*Status laundering.* The team marks risks as "in progress" even when no progress is being made, to avoid escalating. The register tells lies. The fix: status reviews where each risk's last action is named, and if the answer is "we have not done anything", the status changes to "escalating".

*Owner diffusion.* Multiple owners listed for each risk, or "the team" listed as owner. Nobody is actually accountable. The fix: one named person per risk, accountable for the next action. If the action requires coordination, that person is the coordinator, not the absence of one.

**PM decision points**

- Is the risk register short enough that someone will actually read it (5 to 10 risks, one page)?
- Does each risk have a specific next action with a date and a named owner, or are some risks in limbo?
- When was the register last updated, and is the update timestamp visible on the document?
- Is the register shared with the operating sponsor, or is it internal only?

**Watch outs**

- Avoid the trap of writing the register for legal or audit reasons. Those audiences want completeness. The operating audience wants usefulness. The two pull in opposite directions. Optimise for usefulness, and document completeness separately if required.
- A register full of "low / low" entries is performative. Either elevate the entries to honest probability and impact, or retire them.
- New risks should be added throughout the project, not just at the start. The register is a living document, not a one-time deliverable.

**Takeaway: a useful risk register is short, current, honest, and shared. 5 to 10 actively managed risks, each with specific next action, on one page. Update weekly. Share with the operating sponsor. The register is a working tool, not a compliance artifact.**

**Qantas story**

Imagine running a Qantas AI project to build an internal knowledge assistant for the cabin crew training team. The project is 16 weeks, the team has run the risk workshop in week one, and now has a risk register with 14 identified risks. The temptation is to keep all 14 in the register and dutifully update each one weekly.

The PM resists this. The register is reduced to 7 actively managed risks. The other 7 are moved to a "watched but not actively managed" section, which is reviewed monthly rather than weekly. The 7 active risks are: training data access (data), training content review by subject matter experts (organisational), legal review of training content scope (legal), an integration dependency on the learning management system (technical / influence), the risk of cabin crew union concerns about AI in training (organisational / track), the risk of regulator interest in AI in safety-critical training contexts (regulatory / track), and the risk of the training content owner changing roles (organisational / track).

Each risk has a one line title, category, control type, probability, impact, owner, status, and next action with date. The whole register fits on one page.

The register is shared with the operating sponsor (a senior training manager) and with the head of cabin crew operations, who chairs the steering committee. The first time the head of cabin crew operations sees the register, she comments that it is clearer than any she has seen on Qantas projects. The team's credibility rises.

The register is reviewed weekly between the PM and the operating sponsor, in a 20 minute call. Each risk's next action is checked, statuses are updated, new risks are added if they have arisen. In week four, a new risk arises: the cabin crew union has been consulted on the project and has raised concerns about the use of training data. This becomes an active risk, with status "escalating" (because the concern needs to be addressed before training data can be used) and a specific next action: "operating sponsor to meet with union representative by Friday, propose a data anonymisation protocol".

The honest "escalating" status is what makes the register useful. The operating sponsor takes the action seriously because the register reflects the seriousness. The meeting happens, the anonymisation protocol is agreed, and the status moves to "in progress" the following week. Without the explicit escalating status, the action might have slipped.

In week 10, the register surfaces a different kind of value. The risk of the training content owner changing roles materialises: she announces she is taking a new role in two weeks. The risk had been tracked all along, and the team had quietly documented her decisions and built relationships with two of her colleagues. The transition is smooth. The new content owner is briefed using the documentation. The project does not slip. Without the tracked risk and the parallel mitigation work, the transition could have caused weeks of delay.

At project end, the register has had 23 risks across its history: 7 retired as no longer relevant, 12 mitigated, 3 materialised (and handled), 1 escalated and resolved through senior intervention. The register is included as an annex in the closing report. The head of cabin crew operations specifically cites the register as a practice she wants to take forward to other projects.

The lesson for AI delivery is that the risk register is one of the cheapest and most under-used tools available. The cost of maintaining a good one is 30 minutes per week. The benefit is that important risks get attention, the client trusts the team's situational awareness, and the project handles its surprises with more grace. The discipline is to keep it short, current, and honest, even when honesty is uncomfortable.

**定义**

风险登记表是一份持续维护的文档, 列出项目上识别的风险、当前状态、谁拥有、正在做什么。在大多数组织里, 风险登记表是一个被填好、签字、然后被忽略的官僚产物。这一节的纪律是产出一份短的、诚实的、当前的、跟客户共享的风险登记表, 形式是真的帮人管理风险, 而不只是记录它存在。

**为何在交付中重要**

风险登记表为一个原因而存在: 确保没有重要风险被遗忘或管理不足。做不到这个的登记表比没有登记表更糟, 因为**它制造了风险管理的幻觉, 没有实质**。三种病理让大多数风险登记表无用: 太长、太旧、太消毒。

**太长**: 列了 30 到 50 条风险, 其中只有 5 条真的被管理。**没人读**。**太旧**: 上次更新是三周前, 状态不再准确, owner 不再在岗。**过时**。**太消毒**: 风险措辞避免让任何人 alarming, 状态更新无论实际怎样都说 "在监控"。登记表告诉没人任何他们不知道的东西。

一份有用的风险登记表跟这三者相反: 短、当前、诚实。每周或每两周更新, 包含 5 到 10 条主动管理的风险, 用让每条风险的实际状态清楚的大白话。

**运作原理**

一份有用的风险登记表有以下结构。它能装在一页上, 这就是重点。

| 字段 | 内容 |
|---|---|
| ID | 一个短标识符, 交叉引用有用 |
| 标题 | 大白话一行, 风险是什么 |
| 类别 | 技术、数据、组织、监管 (来自 14.1) |
| 控制类型 | 控制、影响、跟踪 (来自 14.2) |
| 概率 | 低、中、高, 简短说明原因 |
| 影响 | 低、中、高, 简短说明后果 |
| Owner | 交付团队上一位具名的人 |
| 状态 | 已缓解、进行中、升级中、已显现、已撤回 |
| 上次更新 | 这一行上次有意义更新的日期 |
| 下一步行动 | 具体的下一步行动, 带日期和 owner |

每条风险 10 个字段, 5 到 10 条风险, 全在一页上。"下一步行动" 列最重要: 每条活跃风险必须有具体的下一步行动加日期。没有它, 这条风险没在被管理。

状态词汇

状态是大多数登记表出错的地方。词汇需要具体且诚实。

| 状态 | 含义 |
|---|---|
| 已缓解 | 风险被处理了, 带证据。要么被阻止、被消除、要么影响现在可接受。 |
| 进行中 | 主动缓解工作正在发生, 按进度。 |
| 升级中 | 缓解不奏效, 或新信息增加了风险。需要资深关注。 |
| 已显现 | 风险已经发生, 现在是 issue, 不是 risk。 |
| 已撤回 | 风险不再相关, 带理由。 |

**不允许的**: "在监控" (太模糊)、"待定" (懒)、"持续中" (无意义)。如果状态会是其中之一, 这条风险没在被管理, 需要要么一个真正的行动、要么被撤回。

怎么跟客户共享登记表

**两种常见做法**。**第一**: 登记表保留在交付团队内部, 只把过滤过或总结过的版本跟客户共享。这有时合适, 尤其对商业敏感风险, 但有代价。客户不知道的风险, 客户无法帮助, 团队的升级也分量更轻, 如果客户只在危机时刻才看到它们。

**第二**: 跟运营 sponsor 开放共享登记表, 跟更广的 steering committee 共享一个脱敏版本。这通常是对的默认。运营 sponsor 看到一切。Steering committee 看到涉及他们的风险。团队对高度敏感的项目保留一些酌情权 (例如, 涉及特定个人表现的风险), 但披露那些项目的存在。

开放共享登记表有几个二阶效应。运营 sponsor 变成风险管理的伙伴, 不只是坏消息的接收者。客户对团队能力的看法通常上升, 因为**客户不习惯看到这种程度的结构**。团队被迫保持登记表诚实, 因为**外部的人在读**。

**会出什么岔子**

三个破坏登记表有用性的 pattern。

*风险通胀*。**团队列出每一种可想象的风险以显得彻底**。**登记表长到 30 项**。**没一项有具体的下一步行动**。**登记表变成噪音之墙**。**修复**: **主动管理的风险上限 10 条**。**其他要么 park, 要么真的不是当前关切**。

*状态洗白*。**团队把风险标为 "进行中", 即使没有进展, 为了避免升级**。**登记表说谎**。**修复**: **状态 review 时, 每条风险的上次行动都被命名, 如果答案是 "我们什么也没做", 状态改为 "升级中"**。

*Owner 弥散*。**每条风险列多个 owner, 或 "团队" 作为 owner**。**没人真的负责**。**修复**: **每条风险一位具名的人, 对下一步行动负责**。**如果行动需要协调, 那个人是协调者, 不是缺席协调者**。

**PM 决策点**

- 风险登记表**短到有人会真的读吗** (5 到 10 条, 一页)?
- 每条风险**有没有具体的下一步行动加日期加具名 owner**, 还是有些风险在悬置?
- 登记表**上次更新是什么时候**, **更新时间戳在文档上可见吗**?
- 登记表**跟运营 sponsor 共享了吗**, 还是只在内部?

**注意事项**

- **避免出于法务或审计原因写登记表的陷阱**。**那些受众想要完整性**。**运营受众想要有用性**。**两者拉向相反方向**。**优化有用性, 如果需要就单独文档化完整性**。
- **全是 "低/低" 条目的登记表是表演性的**。**要么把条目提升到诚实的概率和影响, 要么撤回它们**。
- **新风险应该在整个项目过程中加入, 不只是开始时**。**登记表是活的文档, 不是一次性交付物**。

**要点总结: 一份有用的风险登记表是短的、当前的、诚实的、被共享的。5 到 10 条主动管理的风险, 每条带具体的下一步行动, 在一页上。每周更新。跟运营 sponsor 共享。登记表是工作工具, 不是合规产物。**

**Qantas 案例**

设想你跑一个 Qantas AI 项目, 给客舱机组培训团队建一个内部知识助手。项目 16 周, 团队第 1 周跑了风险 workshop, 现在有一份 14 条识别风险的登记表。诱惑是把所有 14 条留在登记表里, 每周尽职更新每一条。

PM **抵抗这个**。登记表被缩到 7 条主动管理的风险。另外 7 条被移到一个 "被观察但不主动管理" 的部分, 每月而不是每周 review。7 条活跃风险是: 培训数据访问 (数据)、主题专家对培训内容的 review (组织)、培训内容范围的法务 review (法律)、对学习管理系统的集成依赖 (技术 / 影响)、客舱机组工会对 AI 培训的关切风险 (组织 / 跟踪)、监管对 AI 在安全关键培训场景下兴趣的风险 (监管 / 跟踪)、培训内容 owner 换岗的风险 (组织 / 跟踪)。

每条风险有一行标题、类别、控制类型、概率、影响、owner、状态、带日期的下一步行动。整个登记表装在一页上。

登记表跟运营 sponsor (一位资深培训经理) 共享, 也跟主持 steering committee 的客舱机组运营负责人共享。客舱机组运营负责人第一次看到登记表时, 评论说这比她在 Qantas 项目上见过的任何一份都清晰。团队的可信度上升。

登记表在 PM 和运营 sponsor 之间每周 review, 20 分钟通话。每条风险的下一步行动被检查, 状态更新, 如果有新风险出现就加入。第 4 周, 一条新风险出现: 客舱机组工会被就项目咨询, 对培训数据的使用提出了关切。它变成一条活跃风险, 状态 "升级中" (因为关切需要在培训数据能用之前被处理), 具体的下一步行动: "运营 sponsor 在周五之前跟工会代表会面, 提议一个数据匿名化协议"。

诚实的 "升级中" 状态是让登记表有用的东西。运营 sponsor 认真对待行动, 因为**登记表反映了严肃性**。会面发生, 匿名化协议被同意, 状态下周移到 "进行中"。没有明确的 "升级中" 状态, 行动可能滑掉。

第 10 周, 登记表浮出不同种类的价值。培训内容 owner 换岗的风险显现: 她宣布两周后接受新职位。这条风险一直在被跟踪, 团队悄悄文档化了她的决定, 跟她两位同事建立了关系。**过渡顺利**。新内容 owner 用文档化的东西做了简报。**项目没滑期**。没有被跟踪的风险和并行的缓解工作, 过渡可能造成几周延迟。

项目结束时, 登记表历史上有 23 条风险: 7 条撤回因不再相关、12 条已缓解、3 条已显现 (并被处理)、1 条升级并通过高层介入解决。登记表作为附录纳入收尾报告。客舱机组运营负责人特别引用登记表作为她想推广到其他项目的实践。

AI 交付的启示: 风险登记表是市面上最便宜且最被低估的工具之一。维护一份好的成本是每周 30 分钟。好处是重要风险得到关注、客户信任团队的态势感知、项目以更多的从容处理它的意外。纪律是保持它短、当前、诚实, 哪怕诚实让人不舒服。
