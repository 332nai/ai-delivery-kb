**Definition**

Not all risks can be acted on the same way. Some are within the team's direct control: the team chooses whether and how to mitigate. Some are within the team's influence: the team cannot decide unilaterally, but can shape the decisions of others who do. Some are entirely outside both: the team can only track them, prepare for their materialisation, and respond. Sorting every identified risk into these three categories is one of the most useful disciplines in scope and risk management, because it tells the team where their effort will produce results and where it will not.

**Why it matters in delivery**

Teams that do not make this distinction make two predictable mistakes. The first is treating influence-level risks as control-level: spending energy on something the team cannot actually decide, producing frustration and missed deadlines. The second is treating control-level risks as track-only: passively watching a risk materialise when the team could have prevented it. Both mistakes are common, especially under pressure, and both are corrected by sorting risks into the three categories at workshop time and reviewing the categorisation regularly.

The framework also helps communication with the client. Clients sometimes expect the team to take responsibility for risks the team cannot actually mitigate ("you said this would be a problem, why did you not stop it?"). Categorising risks transparently, in writing, signed off by both sides, prevents this misalignment. The team owns the control-level risks. The team and the client jointly own the influence-level risks. The client (or external parties) owns the track-only risks, with the team providing monitoring.

**How it works**

For each risk identified in the workshop (covered in 14.1), apply the categorisation test:

*Control:* Can the team decide and act unilaterally to mitigate this risk? Examples: choice of model provider, choice of architecture, structure of the eval set, frequency of testing. If yes, this is a control-level risk. The team owns it directly.

*Influence:* Can the team shape the decision of someone else who has the authority to act? Examples: data access (the client's IT team decides, but the team can advocate), regulatory engagement (legal decides, but the team can prepare evidence), competing initiative resolution (executive decides, but the team can surface trade offs). If yes, this is an influence-level risk. The team owns the advocacy work, not the decision.

*Track:* Can neither of the above be done, and the team's only meaningful action is monitoring and preparing for the risk materialising? Examples: provider deprecating a model, regulator releasing new guidance, economic conditions changing the client's appetite for the project. If yes, this is a track-only risk. The team's job is early warning and contingency planning, not prevention.

Each risk in the register gets a single label. Some risks span categories (e.g. data access starts as influence, becomes control once the access is granted). In those cases, label by the current state and re-evaluate at each review.

| Category | Team owns | Team does not own | Team's contribution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Control | The mitigation decision | (no one else) | Decide and execute |
| Influence | The advocacy and framing | The decision itself | Prepare evidence, raise issue, propose options |
| Track | The monitoring | The triggering event | Watch for signals, prepare contingencies |

**How the categorisation changes the action**

A worked example. Suppose a project has identified three risks: (1) the eval set is not yet comprehensive, (2) the customer data needed for the project requires legal review that has not started, (3) the model provider may deprecate the current model version during the project.

Risk 1 is control. The team decides what is in the eval set, the team can expand it. The action is "expand eval set by week 3, owner [team member], no escalation needed".

Risk 2 is influence. The team does not control legal review, but can advocate for it to start, prepare the materials legal will need, and offer to help legal understand the technical context. The action is "engage legal team in week 1, prepare submission packet, escalate to operating sponsor if no response in week 2".

Risk 3 is track. The team cannot prevent provider decisions. The action is "subscribe to provider announcement channels, abstract the provider behind a code interface so migration is feasible, define what a 'we need to switch' signal would look like, document fallback options".

The same level of attention is paid to each risk. The nature of the attention is different.

**Communicating the categorisation to the client**

The categorisation is useful internally and also a powerful communication device with the client. Most clients find it intuitive and helpful. The pitch: "we have categorised each risk by who can actually act on it. For the ones we control, here is what we are doing. For the ones we can influence but not decide, here is what we are doing and here is where we will need your help. For the ones we can only track, here is what we are watching for and what we will do if it happens."

This pitch achieves several things at once. It clarifies ownership. It surfaces the help the team needs from the client. It manages expectations about what the team can actually do. And it signals that the team has thought about each risk specifically, not just listed them.

**PM decision points**

- For each risk on the register, have I categorised it as control, influence, or track, with the reason?
- For influence-level risks, have I identified the specific person whose decision the team is trying to shape?
- For track-only risks, have I defined what signals would trigger contingency action, so the team is not surprised?

**Watch outs**

- Avoid labelling everything as "influence". The label is sometimes used to make the team feel like it is doing something without actually committing to a specific advocacy action. If a risk is labelled influence, name the specific person and the specific ask.
- Track-only risks are not "no action" risks. They require active monitoring, defined trigger signals, and prepared contingencies. The team's work is small but ongoing.
- Re-categorise as the project moves. A risk that started as influence may become control once a decision is made. A risk that started as track may become influence once a vendor invites consultation. The labels are not permanent.

**Takeaway: every risk on the register should be labelled control, influence, or track. Control means the team decides and acts. Influence means the team shapes someone else's decision. Track means the team monitors and prepares but cannot prevent. Each category requires a different kind of work, and missing the categorisation produces wasted effort or missed risks.**

**Qantas story**

Imagine running the risk workshop on a Qantas AI project, an agent for the disruption operations centre. The workshop identifies eight risks. The team and the operating sponsor then sort each into control, influence, or track.

Two risks are categorised as control. The eval set design (the team owns this entirely) and the choice of architecture (within the agreed scope, the team chooses). The actions are direct and the team owns the timeline.

Four risks are categorised as influence. Data access from the operations data warehouse (controlled by the data platform team, who require a formal access request and have a queue), legal review of the customer-facing communication outputs (controlled by Qantas Legal, who have their own priorities), integration with the operations dashboard (controlled by the dashboard owning team, with their own roadmap), and engagement with the disruption operations supervisors who will be the daily users (controlled by their line manager, who decides their time commitments).

For each of these four, the team identifies the specific person whose decision they are trying to shape, and the specific ask. For data access: the data platform manager, asked to prioritise the AI project's request in the next two weeks. For legal review: the senior legal counsel for customer operations, asked to set up a structured review cadence rather than ad hoc. For dashboard integration: the dashboard product manager, asked for a 30 minute discovery conversation by end of week 2. For supervisor engagement: the operations centre manager, asked to commit one supervisor 4 hours per week for 8 weeks.

Two risks are categorised as track. Provider model deprecation (cannot prevent, only prepare for) and aviation safety regulator guidance on AI in operational decisions (cannot influence at the project's scale, but a significant policy change could materially affect the project). For the first, the team sets up a monitoring channel and abstracts the provider behind a code interface. For the second, the team identifies the relevant regulator publications, subscribes to updates, and defines a signal ("if the regulator issues guidance requiring explicit human approval for AI outputs in operational contexts, we need to add a review step") that would trigger a project plan revisit.

The categorised list is presented to the operating sponsor and then to the steering committee. The steering committee finds the structure clarifying. The operating sponsor is able to act immediately on the influence-level risks because the specific asks are clear. The Chief Operating Officer, who chairs the steering committee, takes responsibility for two of the influence-level risks (data access and dashboard integration) and uses her authority to accelerate the data platform team and the dashboard team's involvement.

The interesting consequence is what happens in week six, when the aviation regulator does release new guidance on AI in operational decisions. Because this risk had been tracked, the team had already identified the relevant publication and was monitoring it. The new guidance is read and assessed within 48 hours. The team recommends a small addition to the project scope: an explicit human approval step before any AI-generated operational communication is published. The recommendation is accepted, the change is small because the team had pre-architected for this possibility, and the project remains on schedule.

The lesson for AI delivery is that the categorisation of risks by control, influence, and track is one of the most leverage-rich practices in the discipline. It tells the team where to spend its energy, what to ask the client for, and what to merely monitor. Without the categorisation, every risk feels equally pressing and equally the team's responsibility, which produces both burnout and blind spots. With the categorisation, the team's work is focused, the client's contribution is clear, and the risks that can only be tracked are still being watched, just by the right kind of attention.

**定义**

**不是所有风险都能用同样方式处理**。**有些在团队的直接控制下**: **团队选择是否以及如何缓解**。**有些在团队的影响范围内**: **团队不能单方面决定, 但能影响他人的决定**。**有些完全在两者之外**: **团队只能跟踪、为它显现做准备、然后回应**。**把每一条识别出的风险分到这三类, 是范围和风险管理里最有用的纪律之一**, 因为**它告诉团队他们的努力会在哪里产生结果、在哪里不会**。

**为何在交付中重要**

**不做这个区分的团队会犯两个可预测的错误**。**第一**, **把影响层级的风险当作控制层级处理**: **在团队其实不能决定的事情上花精力, 产出挫败和错过的 deadline**。**第二**, **把控制层级的风险当作只能跟踪**: **被动看着一个风险显现, 而团队本可以阻止**。**两个错误都常见, 尤其在压力下**, **两个都通过在 workshop 时把风险分类、并定期 review 分类来纠正**。

**这个框架也帮助跟客户沟通**。**客户有时期待团队为自己其实无法缓解的风险负责** (**"你说过这会是个问题, 你为什么没阻止?"**)。**透明地分类风险、写下来、双方签字, 防止这种错位**。**团队拥有控制层级的风险**。**团队和客户共同拥有影响层级的风险**。**客户 (或外部各方) 拥有只能跟踪的风险**, **团队提供监控**。

**运作原理**

**对 workshop (14.1 描述) 里识别的每一条风险, 应用分类测试**:

*控制*: **团队能不能单方面决定并行动来缓解这个风险?** **例子**: **选模型厂商、选架构、eval set 的结构、测试频率**。**如果是, 这是控制层级风险**。**团队直接拥有它**。

*影响*: **团队能不能塑造另一个有授权行动的人的决定?** **例子**: **数据访问 (客户 IT 团队决定, 但团队能倡议)、监管 engagement (法务决定, 但团队能准备证据)、解决竞争性 initiative (高管决定, 但团队能浮出 trade off)**。**如果是, 这是影响层级风险**。**团队拥有倡议工作, 不拥有决定**。

*跟踪*: **以上两个都做不到, 团队唯一有意义的动作是监控、为风险显现做准备?** **例子**: **厂商 deprecate 模型、监管发布新指导、经济条件改变客户对项目的胃口**。**如果是, 这是只能跟踪的风险**。**团队的工作是早期预警和应急规划, 不是预防**。

**登记表上每条风险拿一个标签**。**有些风险跨类别** (**例如, 数据访问开始是影响, 一旦访问被批准就变成控制**)。**那种情况下, 按当前状态标, 每次 review 时重评**。

| 类别 | 团队拥有 | 团队不拥有 | 团队的贡献 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 控制 | 缓解决定 | (没人) | 决定和执行 |
| 影响 | 倡议和措辞 | 决定本身 | 准备证据、提出问题、提议选项 |
| 跟踪 | 监控 | 触发事件 | 注意信号, 准备应急 |

**分类怎么改变行动**

**一个例子**。**假设一个项目识别了三条风险**: **(1) eval set 还不全面**、**(2) 项目需要的客户数据需要法务审阅, 还没开始**、**(3) 模型厂商可能在项目期间 deprecate 当前模型版本**。

**风险 1 是控制**。**团队决定 eval set 里有什么, 团队能扩大它**。**行动是 "第 3 周前扩大 eval set, owner [团队成员], 不需要升级"**。

**风险 2 是影响**。**团队不控制法务审阅, 但能倡议它开始、准备法务会需要的材料、主动帮法务理解技术背景**。**行动是 "第 1 周 engage 法务团队, 准备提交包, 第 2 周没回应就升级到运营 sponsor"**。

**风险 3 是跟踪**。**团队不能阻止厂商决定**。**行动是 "订阅厂商公告渠道、用代码接口抽象厂商让迁移可行、定义 '我们需要切换' 的信号长什么样、文档化兜底选项"**。

**每条风险被同样程度地关注**。**关注的性质不同**。

**把分类沟通给客户**

**分类对内部有用, 对客户也是一个强有力的沟通工具**。**大多数客户觉得它直观且有帮助**。**话术**: **"我们把每条风险按 '谁能真的对它行动' 分类了。对我们控制的, 这是我们正在做的。对我们能影响但不能决定的, 这是我们正在做的、也是我们会需要你帮助的地方。对我们只能跟踪的, 这是我们盯着的信号、以及如果发生我们会做什么。"**

**这个话术同时做到几件事**。**澄清拥有权**。**浮出团队需要客户帮助的地方**。**管理对团队实际能做什么的预期**。**传递团队对每条风险都具体想过、不是只列了一下**。

**PM 决策点**

- 登记表上的每条风险, 我**有没有把它分类为控制、影响、或跟踪, 带理由**?
- 对影响层级风险, 我**有没有识别出团队正在试图塑造其决定的那个具体的人**?
- 对只能跟踪的风险, 我**有没有定义什么信号会触发应急行动**, **这样团队不被惊吓**?

**注意事项**

- **避免把所有东西都标为 "影响"**。**这个标签有时被用来让团队感觉自己在做事, 但其实没承诺一个具体的倡议动作**。**如果一条风险被标为影响, 说出具体的人和具体的请求**。
- **只能跟踪的风险不是 "不行动" 的风险**。**它们需要主动监控、定义的触发信号、准备好的应急**。**团队的工作小但持续**。
- **项目推进时重新分类**。**一条以影响开始的风险, 在决定做出后可能变成控制**。**一条以跟踪开始的风险, 在厂商邀请咨询后可能变成影响**。**标签不是永久的**。

**要点总结: 登记表上每条风险都该被标为控制、影响、或跟踪。控制意味着团队决定和行动。影响意味着团队塑造他人的决定。跟踪意味着团队监控和准备但不能阻止。每类需要不同的工作, 缺失分类会产出浪费的精力或被错过的风险。**

**Qantas 案例**

设想你在一个 Qantas AI 项目跑风险 workshop, **给中断运营中心的 agent**。**Workshop 识别八条风险**。**团队和运营 sponsor 然后把每条分到控制、影响、跟踪**。

**两条被分到控制**。**eval set 设计 (团队完全拥有这个) 和架构选择 (在商定范围内, 团队选)**。**行动是直接的, 团队拥有时间线**。

**四条被分到影响**。**从运营数据仓库的数据访问 (由数据平台团队控制, 他们要求正式访问请求, 有排队)**、**面向客户的沟通输出的法务 review (由 Qantas Legal 控制, 他们有自己的优先级)**、**跟运营 dashboard 的集成 (由 dashboard 拥有团队控制, 有他们自己的路线图)**、**跟将作为日常用户的中断运营主管们的 engagement (由他们的直属经理控制, 他决定他们的时间承诺)**。

**对这四条中的每一条, 团队识别出他们正在试图塑造其决定的具体的人, 以及具体的请求**。**数据访问**: **数据平台经理, 请求在接下来两周里优先处理 AI 项目的请求**。**法务 review**: **客户运营的资深法律顾问, 请求设立结构化 review 节奏, 而不是临时**。**Dashboard 集成**: **Dashboard 产品经理, 请求第 2 周末前一个 30 分钟的发现对话**。**主管 engagement**: **运营中心经理, 请求承诺一位主管 8 周内每周 4 小时**。

**两条被分到跟踪**。**厂商模型 deprecation (不能阻止, 只能准备) 和航空安全监管对 AI 在运营决策中的指导 (在项目规模上不能影响, 但重大政策变化可能对项目有实质影响)**。**对第一条, 团队设立监控渠道, 把厂商抽象在代码接口后面**。**对第二条, 团队识别相关监管出版物, 订阅更新, 定义一个信号** (**"如果监管发布指导, 要求 AI 在运营场景下的输出有明确的人工批准, 我们需要加一个 review 步骤"**) **会触发项目计划重审**。

**分类清单呈给运营 sponsor, 然后呈给 steering committee**。**Steering committee 觉得这个结构有澄清作用**。**运营 sponsor 能立刻对影响层级风险行动, 因为具体请求清晰**。**主持 steering committee 的 Chief Operating Officer 为两条影响层级风险 (数据访问和 dashboard 集成) 承担责任, 用她的权威加速数据平台团队和 dashboard 团队的参与**。

**有意思的后果发生在第六周, 当航空监管真的发布了关于 AI 在运营决策中使用的新指导**。**因为这条风险被跟踪过, 团队已经识别了相关出版物、正在监控它**。**新指导在 48 小时内被读和评估**。**团队建议项目范围里加一个小添加**: **任何 AI 生成的运营沟通发布之前的明确人工批准步骤**。**建议被接受, 变更小, 因为团队已经为这个可能性预先架构, 项目保持在进度上**。

AI 交付的启示: **按控制、影响、跟踪给风险分类, 是这个学科里杠杆最高的实践之一**。**它告诉团队在哪里花精力、跟客户要什么、什么仅仅是监控**。**没有这个分类, 每条风险感觉同样紧迫、同样是团队的责任, 产出 burnout 加盲点**。**有这个分类, 团队的工作是聚焦的、客户的贡献是清楚的、只能跟踪的风险仍然被看着 (只是被对的那种关注看着)**。
