**Definition**

Every AI requirements process produces a list of capabilities the system will have in v1. This is normal. The less common but more valuable companion artifact is the "v1 will not do" list: an explicit, written enumeration of capabilities that the system will not have, will not attempt, and will not be expected to perform in v1. Locking this list down, getting it signed off by the client, and referencing it through the project is the single most powerful tool for preventing scope creep.

**Why it matters in delivery**

In a deterministic software project, scope creep is bounded by the cost of writing code. Adding a new feature has visible cost. In an AI project, scope creep often arrives in the form "can the AI also do X", where X is a small extension that feels like it should be free. "We are already building a summariser, can it also classify sentiment?" "The agent is already booking, can it also recommend?" Each one of these requests, on its own, sounds reasonable. Together, they expand scope by 30 percent without anyone noticing.

The "will not do" list is the artifact that lets the team say "yes, we discussed this in week two, here is the record of why it is not in v1, let's add it to the v2 conversation". Without the list, the team has to relitigate the decision every time, with the disadvantage of having to argue against a single small ask in isolation.

**How it works**

The list is produced in week two, alongside the eval set. It has four categories.

| Category | What it contains | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Out of scope by intent | Capabilities the team and client agreed not to build | "v1 will not handle complaints in languages other than English" |
| Deferred to v2 | Capabilities that will come later, with rough timing | "v1 will not include sentiment analysis, scheduled for v2 in Q3" |
| Refused outright | Capabilities the team has assessed as inappropriate | "v1 will not recommend specific medical actions, this requires human clinician" |
| Out of bounds for AI | Capabilities that belong in another system | "v1 will not update CRM records, that goes through existing CRM workflow" |

Each entry has a reason. The reasons are short and direct. "Not in v1 because scope was negotiated to focus on the highest-value 80 percent". "Not in v1 because data not available within engagement timeline". "Not in v1 because regulatory approval required". The reason is what makes the entry stick in future conversations.

The list is signed off by the same client representatives who sign off on the v1 capability list. It lives in the same document. It is referenced in steering committees.

**How to produce it**

The simplest method is to take the long list from the original requirements gathering, and explicitly mark which items did not make it into v1, with the reason. This produces a list that is grounded in real client discussion rather than invented by the team.

A more useful method is to ask, at the end of each requirements workshop, "what is the system not going to do, that someone might assume it should?" This question is usually met with silence the first time, and then revelations. Operating teams often have unstated assumptions about what the AI will do, sometimes wildly beyond what the team is building. Surfacing these assumptions and writing them down on the "will not do" list saves the team from production-time disappointment.

Another useful method is to read the SOW carefully for verbs and adjectives that imply capability. Phrases like "comprehensive customer service automation", "end-to-end workflow", "fully integrated experience" each imply capabilities that the team probably does not intend to build in v1. Each of these phrases becomes a candidate for the "will not do" list, with a specific description of what the v1 boundary actually is.

**How to use the list during the project**

The list comes out at three predictable moments:

1. *When a stakeholder requests a new capability.* The PM's response is "good idea, let me check the v2 list. Yes, it is there, and we had agreed in week two it would not be in v1. Should we re-discuss whether to escalate it back into v1, knowing what we know now?" This framing converts the request from a free addition into a deliberate trade off, with the team's prior work as the anchor.

2. *During the launch readiness review.* The list is read out alongside the capability list. The client confirms again that the v1 scope is the v1 scope, and the deferred items are appropriately on the v2 backlog. This prevents launch-time surprises.

3. *In the v2 planning conversation.* The list is the natural starting agenda for v2. The team and client review which deferred items have become urgent, which ones can stay deferred, and which ones are now obsolete. This is one of the easiest v2 planning conversations to run, because the foundation is already documented.

**PM decision points**

- Have I produced an explicit "v1 will not do" list, or am I assuming clients will infer the boundary?
- Is the list signed off by the same stakeholders who signed off on the v1 capability list?
- For each "will not do" entry, is the reason captured, and is the reason still valid as the project progresses?

**Watch outs**

- The list will surface disagreement. Some stakeholders thought the AI would do things that the team did not intend to build. Surfacing this in week two is healthy. Surfacing it in week ten is a crisis.
- Resist the temptation to make the list comprehensive. Aim for 10 to 20 items, focused on the most likely confusions. A list of 200 items is read by no one.
- The list is not a tool to dismiss requests. The right response to a new request is engagement, not "no, see the list". The list is the anchor for the trade off conversation, not the answer to it.

**Takeaway: v1 is defined by what it does and what it does not do. The "will not do" list, locked in week two, is the cheapest scope protection available. Use it as an anchor for trade off conversations, not as a shield to dismiss requests.**

**Qantas story**

Imagine being asked to build an AI customer service assistant for Qantas frequent flyer members. The v1 scope is described in the SOW as "handle common membership account queries, including points balance, tier status, and recent flight crediting". Sounds simple. The temptation is to build to that description and call it a day.

The discipline is to also produce a "v1 will not do" list. The team and the client work through it in week two. The list ends up with the following entries, each with a reason.

The system will not handle bookings or changes to bookings: reason, these flow through the booking system and the v1 scope is membership only. The system will not handle complaints or disputes: reason, these require a clear human handoff path and are deferred to v2. The system will not handle requests in languages other than English: reason, language detection and Mandarin output quality were assessed and require additional time, deferred to v2. The system will not provide tier upgrade or downgrade decisions: reason, these depend on data not yet integrated and a business rule layer that is outside the engagement. The system will not access partner airline data: reason, contractual restrictions, out of bounds for v1. The system will not handle requests on behalf of a member by a third party: reason, identity verification not implemented in v1. The system will not provide travel recommendations: reason, this was discussed but explicitly excluded as it overlaps with marketing tools owned by another team.

Seven entries, each with a reason, signed off by the operating sponsor and the IT representative.

In week six, a new frequent flyer manager joins the steering committee. In the meeting, the manager asks "the assistant can also help members with their bookings, right?" The PM has the list available. The PM does not say "no". The PM says "good question, this was discussed in week two with the operating team, and we agreed to scope booking handling to v2 because the booking workflow is owned by a different team. Here is the documented reason. Should we revisit, knowing what we know now?" The manager reviews the reason, agrees it still applies, and the conversation moves on. Three minutes, no scope drift, no friction.

In week ten, a different stakeholder asks for tier upgrade decisioning. Same response, same outcome.

In the launch readiness review, the list is read out alongside the capability list. The client signs off both. There are no surprises on day one of production.

In the v2 planning conversation eight weeks after launch, the list is the agenda. The Mandarin support deferral is now urgent because the client has expanded into a Chinese market. The booking handling deferral can stay deferred for another quarter. The tier upgrade deferral is reassessed and dropped from v2 entirely because the relevant business rule project has been cancelled. The conversation is efficient because the team is starting from a written record, not from memory.

The lesson for AI delivery is that the "will not do" list is one of the most underrated artifacts in the discipline. It costs an hour to produce in week two, it prevents days of scope arguments over the project, and it makes v2 planning trivial. The cost is small, the payoff is enormous, and yet most projects do not produce one because the team is focused on what to build, not on what to explicitly not build. The discipline is to do both.

**定义**

每一个 AI 需求过程都会产出**系统 v1 会有的能力清单**。**这是正常的**。**不太常见但更有价值的搭档产物**是 "v1 不做" 清单: 明确写下来的、列举系统 v1 不会有、不会尝试、不会被期待执行的能力。锁定这份清单、让客户签字、在项目中持续引用它, 是防止 scope creep 最强的单一工具。

**为何在交付中重要**

在确定性软件项目里, scope creep 被写代码的成本约束住。加一个新 feature **有可见的成本**。AI 项目里, scope creep 经常以 "AI 能不能也做 X" 的形式到达, X 是一个看起来应该免费的小扩展。"我们已经在建一个总结器, 它能不能也做情感分类?" "agent 已经在订位, 它能不能也推荐?" 每一个请求单独看都很合理。合起来, 它们在没人注意时把 scope 扩了 30%。

**"不做" 清单**就是那个让团队能说**"是的, 我们第二周讨论过, 这是为什么它不在 v1 的记录, 我们把它加到 v2 对话里"** 的产物。没有这份清单, 团队每次都要重新打这场官司, 而且处于不利位置: 不得不孤立地反对一个看起来很小的请求。

**运作原理**

清单**在第二周产出, 跟 eval set 并列**。**它有四个类别**。

| 类别 | 包含什么 | 例子 |
|---|---|---|
| 故意排除 | 团队和客户商定不建的能力 | "v1 不处理非英文的投诉" |
| 推到 v2 | 后续会有的能力, 带粗略时间 | "v1 不含情感分析, 计划 v2 Q3" |
| 直接拒绝 | 团队评估为不合适的能力 | "v1 不推荐具体医疗动作, 这需要人类临床医师" |
| 不属于 AI 边界 | 属于另一个系统的能力 | "v1 不更新 CRM 记录, 那走现有 CRM 工作流" |

每一条都有理由。**理由短而直接**。"不在 v1, 因为 scope 被协商成聚焦最高价值的 80%"。"不在 v1, 因为数据在合作时间线内拿不到"。"不在 v1, 因为需要监管批准"。理由是让这一条在未来对话里站得住脚的东西。

清单由跟签 v1 能力清单的同一批客户代表签字。它住在同一份文档里。它在 steering committee 上被引用。

**怎么产出它**

最简单的方法: 把原始需求收集时的长清单拿来, 明确标出哪些没进 v1, 带理由。这产出一份扎根于真实客户讨论的清单, 而不是团队编的。

**更有用的方法**: 每次需求 workshop 结束时问, "这个系统不会做什么, 但可能有人假设它会做?" 这个问题第一次问通常会得到沉默, 然后是揭示。运营团队对 AI 会做什么经常有没说出口的假设, 有时候远远超过团队在建的东西。把这些假设浮出来、写到 "不做" 清单上, 让团队免于生产时的失望。

**另一个有用方法**: 仔细读 SOW, 找暗示能力的动词和形容词。像 "全面客户服务自动化"、"端到端工作流"、"完全集成的体验" 这种短语, 每一个都暗示着团队大概率不打算在 v1 建的能力。每一个这种短语都是 "不做" 清单的候选, 附上 v1 边界实际在哪的具体描述。

**项目过程中怎么用这份清单**

清单**在三个可预测的时刻出场**:

1. *某位 stakeholder 请求一个新能力时。* PM 的回应是: **"好主意, 让我查一下 v2 清单。是的, 它在那里, 我们第二周同意它不在 v1。我们要不要重新讨论是否在已知这些情况后把它升级回 v1?"** **这种措辞把请求从 "免费增加" 转成 "刻意的 trade off", 用团队之前的工作作为锚点**。

2. *上线 readiness review 时。* **清单和能力清单并排被宣读**。**客户再次确认 v1 范围就是 v1 范围, 推迟项在 v2 backlog 上是合适的**。**防止上线时的惊吓**。

3. *V2 规划对话时。* **清单是 v2 的自然起手议程**。**团队和客户审阅哪些推迟项变得紧迫、哪些可以继续推迟、哪些已经过时**。**这是最容易跑的 v2 规划对话之一, 因为基础已经文档化**。

**PM 决策点**

- 我有没有产出一份**明确的 "v1 不做" 清单**, 还是我假设客户会推断出边界?
- 这份清单**有没有被签 v1 能力清单的同一批 stakeholder 签字**?
- 每条 "不做" 条目, **理由有没有记下来**, **理由在项目推进时是否仍然成立**?

**注意事项**

- 这份清单**会浮出分歧**。**有些 stakeholder 以为 AI 会做团队不打算建的事**。**第二周浮出来是健康的**。**第十周浮出来是危机**。
- **抵抗把清单做得详尽的诱惑**。**瞄准 10 到 20 条, 聚焦最可能的混淆**。**200 条的清单没人读**。
- **清单不是用来打发请求的工具**。**对新请求的对的回应是 engage, 不是 "不, 看清单"**。**清单是 trade off 对话的锚, 不是对话的答案**。

**要点总结: v1 由两件事定义: 它做什么, 它不做什么。"不做" 清单在第二周锁定, 是市面上最便宜的 scope 保护。把它当作 trade off 对话的锚, 不要当作打发请求的盾。**

**Qantas 案例**

设想你被要求给 Qantas frequent flyer 会员建一个 AI 客服助手。SOW 描述 v1 范围是 "处理常见的会员账户查询, 包括积分余额、tier 状态、近期航班积分"。**听起来简单**。诱惑是照着这个描述建, 然后收工。

纪律是同时产出一份 "v1 不做" 清单。团队和客户第二周一起过一遍。清单最后是这样, 每条带理由。

系统不处理订位或订位变更: 理由, 这些走订位系统, v1 范围只覆盖会员。系统不处理投诉或争议: 理由, 这些需要清晰的人工移交路径, 推到 v2。系统不处理非英文的请求: 理由, 语言检测和中文输出质量已经被评估过, 需要额外时间, 推到 v2。系统不提供 tier 升降级决策: 理由, 这些依赖尚未集成的数据, 以及一个超出本次合作范围的业务规则层。系统不访问伙伴航司数据: 理由, 合同限制, 在 v1 的边界之外。系统不处理第三方代会员提出的请求: 理由, 身份验证在 v1 没实现。系统不提供旅行推荐: 理由, 这一条讨论过但明确排除, 因为跟另一个团队拥有的营销工具重叠。

七条, 每条带理由, 由运营 sponsor 和 IT 代表签字。

第六周, 一位新的 frequent flyer 经理加入 steering committee。会上, 这位经理问 "这个助手也能帮会员处理订位, 对吧?" PM 手上有清单。PM 不说 "不"。PM 说: "好问题, 这件事第二周跟运营团队讨论过, 我们同意把订位处理 scope 到 v2, 因为订位工作流由另一个团队拥有。这是文档化的理由。我们要不要重审, 在已知现在的情况后?" 经理审视理由, 同意它仍然适用, 对话继续往前走。三分钟, 没有 scope 漂移, 没有摩擦。

第十周, 另一位 stakeholder 要求 tier 升级决策。同样回应, 同样结果。

上线 readiness review 上, 清单和能力清单并排被宣读。客户给两份都签字。生产第一天没惊吓。

上线八周后的 v2 规划对话里, 清单就是议程。中文支持的推迟现在变紧迫了, 因为客户扩张进入了中国市场。订位处理的推迟可以再延一个季度。Tier 升级的推迟被重新评估, 整个从 v2 撤掉了, 因为相关业务规则项目已经取消。对话效率高, 因为团队从一份书面记录开始, 不是从记忆开始。

AI 交付的启示: "不做" 清单是这个学科里最被低估的产物之一。第二周花一小时产出, 防止项目里几天的 scope 争吵, 让 v2 规划变得简单。成本小, 回报大, 但大多数项目不产出它, 因为团队聚焦在 "建什么", 不聚焦在 "明确不建什么"。纪律是两件都做。
