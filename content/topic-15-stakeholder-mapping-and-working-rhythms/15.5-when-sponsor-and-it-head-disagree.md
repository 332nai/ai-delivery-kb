**Definition**

Most AI projects have at least two senior stakeholders who must be aligned for the project to succeed: the operating sponsor (who owns the business outcome) and the IT or technology lead (who owns the platforms, security, and architecture the project will integrate with). When these two are aligned, the project flows. When they are not aligned, the project enters a familiar pattern of competing instructions, blocked dependencies, and political stress that the delivery team has to navigate without taking sides. This item is about recognising the disagreement early, understanding its sources, and operating effectively in the gap.

**Why it matters in delivery**

Sponsor-versus-IT disagreement is one of the most common and most expensive dysfunctions in AI delivery. The sponsor wants to move fast, often using a vendor or model the IT team has not approved. The IT team wants to ensure standards, security, and integration coherence, which slows the sponsor down. Each side has a legitimate position. Each side controls something the project needs. The team that can recognise this dynamic, name it, and operate effectively in the middle, delivers projects that the team taking a side does not.

The deeper reason this matters: AI is, in most client organisations, a contested space between the business and IT. AI is exciting to business leaders who want to deploy it quickly, and concerning to IT leaders who have seen what happens when business deploys technology without coordination. The disagreement is structural, not personal. It is going to be present in some form on most AI projects. Recognising this and planning for it is part of the work.

**How it works**

The disagreement has a recognisable pattern. Four typical sources, each with a different resolution path.

| Source of disagreement | Sponsor view | IT view | Resolution path |
|---|---|---|---|
| Speed vs standards | "We need this fast to capture business value" | "We need to ensure long term maintainability and security" | Negotiate a fast path that meets minimum standards, document the trade off |
| Vendor or model choice | "This vendor solves our problem now" | "This vendor is not on our approved list, has not been security reviewed" | Run the vendor through approval in parallel with build, with explicit acceptance of risk if approval slips |
| Build vs buy | "Vendors are faster and more proven" | "We can build with our existing platform" | Compare honestly with criteria documented in advance, escalate if persistent |
| Ownership after launch | "My team owns the outcome" | "My team owns the system, you cannot operate without us" | Define operating model upfront, including monitoring, change management, and on-call |

The skill is recognising which source is at play. The same surface disagreement can come from any of the four, with different right responses.

**Operating in the gap**

A few practices for operating effectively when sponsor and IT are not aligned.

*Practice 1: Take both meetings seriously.* The temptation is to lean into the sponsor's view (because they hired you) and dismiss IT's view (because they are slowing things down). This is the wrong move. Take IT's concerns seriously, even when they sound bureaucratic. IT has seen many failed projects and their concerns are usually informed by something real.

*Practice 2: Never let one side speak for the other.* If the sponsor says "IT has agreed to this", verify with IT directly before acting on it. If IT says "the sponsor has approved that constraint", verify with the sponsor. Either side may have selectively reported the other's position, sometimes unconsciously. Direct verification protects against this.

*Practice 3: Maintain a single shared document of agreed positions.* When sponsor and IT do reach agreement on something, write it down in a place both sides can see. Memory is selective. Written agreements are easier to refer back to when later disagreement emerges.

*Practice 4: Surface disagreements early, in front of both.* If you notice sponsor and IT have different views on something important, do not try to bridge it quietly. Schedule a joint meeting where both can speak, the team can present what each side has said, and a resolution can be sought with both present. This is uncomfortable but produces durable agreement.

*Practice 5: Identify the escalation path before you need it.* Who would the sponsor and IT escalate to if they could not agree? Sometimes this is a shared executive (the CIO and CMO both report to the COO, who could break a tie). Sometimes there is no shared escalation short of the CEO. Knowing the escalation path in advance lets you propose it calmly if the disagreement does not resolve.

**When the disagreement is over a specific technical decision**

Some disagreements are over specific technical decisions: which model provider to use, which deployment platform, which integration approach. For these, the right move is to convert the disagreement from preference to evidence.

The team produces a decision memo (using the format from 15.3) that compares options against criteria both sides agree are important. The options are evaluated honestly, including their disadvantages. The recommendation is the team's, based on the evaluation. Both sponsor and IT see the same memo, in the same meeting.

This works because it shifts the conversation from "what do you prefer" to "what do the criteria suggest". Most disagreements collapse when the criteria are explicit and evidence is presented. The few that persist were never about the technical decision in the first place, and the escalation path is now available because the team can demonstrate they have done the analysis honestly.

**When the disagreement is structural and persistent**

Sometimes the disagreement is not about a specific decision but about the broader relationship between business and IT. The sponsor sees IT as a blocker. IT sees the sponsor as reckless. Neither side will resolve this for you. In this situation, the team's job is to operate professionally with both while quietly raising the structural issue with the engagement partner or the team's own leadership.

The signs of structural disagreement: multiple decisions take longer than they should, both sides routinely speak about the other in private with the team, agreements made in joint meetings get re-negotiated unilaterally afterwards, and neither side trusts the other's commitments. When this is the pattern, the project will struggle regardless of what the team does, and the right move is to surface the structural issue at the engagement-partner level so that it can be discussed at the right altitude.

**PM decision points**

- Have I taken both sides' concerns seriously, or am I leaning into the sponsor and dismissing IT?
- Am I verifying claims about the other side's position directly, rather than relying on what one side reports?
- When disagreement surfaces, am I surfacing it in joint meetings, or trying to bridge it quietly?

**Watch outs**

- IT teams can be slow but they are rarely wrong. Their concerns are usually grounded. Dismissing IT is a common mistake that hurts later.
- Sponsors can be impatient but they are usually right about urgency. Their pressure on timeline is usually informed by business realities the IT side does not always see.
- Avoid becoming the messenger between the two. If you find yourself relaying messages, that is a sign you should be scheduling a joint meeting instead.

**Takeaway: sponsor and IT disagreement is structural in many AI projects. Recognise the source (speed vs standards, vendor choice, build vs buy, ownership), take both sides seriously, verify claims directly, write down agreements, surface disagreements in joint meetings, and know the escalation path before you need it. The team that operates effectively in this gap delivers projects that other teams cannot.**

**Qantas story**

Imagine running a Qantas AI project to build a customer insight tool for the marketing team: a system that summarises customer feedback from multiple sources and surfaces themes for marketing campaigns. The operating sponsor is the head of marketing analytics, an experienced executive eager to deploy AI. The IT lead is the head of platform engineering, an experienced executive cautious about new vendors and integration debt.

The disagreement surfaces in week two. The operating sponsor wants to use a specific vendor's AI platform, which she has seen demonstrated at a conference. The vendor is not on Qantas's approved vendor list, has not been security reviewed, and uses a model deployment approach that does not fit Qantas's standard integration patterns. The IT lead wants to either build on Qantas's existing platform or go through full approval for the vendor, which would take 8 to 12 weeks.

Each has a legitimate position. The sponsor's marketing team has competitive urgency; competitors are deploying AI marketing tools and Qantas is behind. The IT lead has a portfolio view; he has seen vendor integrations that became maintenance burdens for years afterward. Neither is wrong.

The PM operates in the gap deliberately. First, take both seriously. The PM has a 45 minute conversation with the IT lead, listens fully to his concerns, and acknowledges that the concerns are valid. The IT lead, who had expected to be steamrolled, becomes more open after this conversation.

Second, never let one speak for the other. The sponsor mentions in passing that "IT has agreed in principle, they just need to approve the specific vendor". The PM verifies directly with the IT lead, who has not agreed in principle to anything beyond having a structured conversation. The mismatch is noted privately and addressed in the next joint meeting.

Third, maintain a single shared document. The PM produces a one page summary of where sponsor and IT are aligned and where they are not. It is shared with both. The shared view of disagreement is itself useful, because it surfaces that the disagreement is narrower than either side thought.

Fourth, surface the disagreement jointly. The PM schedules a 60 minute joint meeting in week three, framed as "decision: vendor approach". The decision memo, sent 48 hours in advance, lays out three options: pursue the sponsor's preferred vendor with parallel security review (risk: vendor may not pass review, project rework), build on the existing Qantas platform (slower but lower long-term risk), or pursue a smaller scope using a vendor already on the approved list (compromise). The criteria are agreed: time to first business value, long term maintenance cost, integration debt, vendor lock-in risk. The team's recommendation is the third option.

In the meeting, both sides engage with the criteria. The sponsor advocates for option one. The IT lead advocates for option two. After 40 minutes, the conversation shifts because the criteria framing has made the trade offs explicit. The compromise option is accepted by both, with documented conditions: the smaller-scope vendor is used, the security review on the sponsor's preferred vendor proceeds in parallel for potential v2 use, and a quarterly review will assess whether to migrate.

The agreement is documented. Both sides sign off. The project proceeds.

The interesting follow up: in week 10, a new concern arises from IT about how the data is being passed to the vendor. Rather than escalating, the IT lead raises it directly with the PM, who works through the technical detail with both sides. The relationship has shifted from adversarial to collaborative because the team operated even-handedly throughout. The IT lead trusts that his concerns will be taken seriously. The sponsor trusts that the team is moving as fast as is responsible.

The parallel comparison is a different Qantas AI project where the team aligned with the sponsor and dismissed IT's concerns. That project launched with a vendor that had not been approved. Three months later, an IT security review found compliance gaps. The project was paused while compliance was retrofitted, costing six weeks of work and damaging the relationship with the IT function for the next round of AI projects.

The lesson for AI delivery is that sponsor-IT disagreement is one of the most predictable patterns in this work, and one of the most expensive when handled poorly. The skill is to operate even-handedly in the middle, take both sides seriously, surface disagreements in joint meetings, and convert subjective preference into evidence-based decision. The team that develops this skill becomes the team that organisations trust to navigate the politics of AI, which is a deeply valuable reputation in this discipline.

**定义**

**大多数 AI 项目都至少有两位资深 stakeholder 必须对齐, 项目才能成功**: **运营 sponsor (拥有业务结果) 和 IT 或技术负责人 (拥有项目要集成的平台、安全、架构)**。**这两位对齐时, 项目流动**。**他们不对齐时, 项目进入一种熟悉的 pattern**: **互相竞争的指示、被卡住的依赖、政治压力, 交付团队必须导航而不站队**。**这一节是关于早期识别这个分歧、理解它的来源、有效在缝隙里操作**。

**为何在交付中重要**

**Sponsor 对 IT 的分歧是 AI 交付里最常见、最贵的功能障碍之一**。**Sponsor 想跑快, 经常用 IT 团队没批准的厂商或模型**。**IT 团队想确保标准、安全、集成连贯, 这慢化 sponsor**。**双方都有合法立场**。**双方都控制项目需要的东西**。**能识别这种动力学、命名它、在中间有效操作的团队, 交付出的项目是站队的团队做不到的**。

**这件事重要的更深原因**: **在大多数客户组织里, AI 是业务和 IT 之间被争夺的空间**。**AI 让想快速部署的业务领导兴奋, 让见过 "业务在没协调情况下部署技术会发生什么" 的 IT 领导担忧**。**分歧是结构性的, 不是个人的**。**它会以某种形式在大多数 AI 项目里出现**。**识别这点并为它规划, 是工作的一部分**。

**运作原理**

**分歧有可识别的 pattern**。**四种典型来源, 每种有不同的解决路径**。

| 分歧来源 | Sponsor 视角 | IT 视角 | 解决路径 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 速度 vs 标准 | "我们需要这个快, 捕获业务价值" | "我们需要确保长期可维护性和安全" | 谈判一条满足最低标准的快速路径, 文档化 trade off |
| 厂商或模型选择 | "这个厂商现在解决我们的问题" | "这个厂商不在我们的批准清单上, 没被安全 review" | 跟建造并行地把厂商走完批准流程, 明确接受 "批准滑期" 的风险 |
| Build vs buy | "厂商更快、更经过验证" | "我们能用现有平台建" | 用事先文档化的标准诚实对照, 持久就升级 |
| 上线后拥有权 | "我的团队拥有结果" | "我的团队拥有系统, 没有我们你们无法运营" | 上线前定义运营模式, 包括监控、变更管理、on-call |

**技艺是识别哪种来源在场上**。**同样表面的分歧能来自四种中任何一种, 带不同的正确回应**。

**在缝隙里操作**

**几条在 sponsor 和 IT 不对齐时有效操作的实践**。

*实践一: 认真对待两边的会议*。**诱惑是倾向 sponsor 的视角 (因为他们雇了你)、打发 IT 的视角 (因为他们慢化事情)**。**这是错的动作**。**认真对待 IT 的关切, 哪怕它们听起来官僚**。**IT 见过很多失败的项目, 他们的关切通常被某些真实的东西告知**。

*实践二: 永不让一边为另一边说话*。**如果 sponsor 说 "IT 同意了这个", 行动前直接跟 IT 验证**。**如果 IT 说 "sponsor 批准了那个约束", 跟 sponsor 验证**。**任一边可能选择性报告另一边的立场, 有时无意识地**。**直接验证防止这点**。

*实践三: 维护一份单一共享的、关于商定立场的文档*。**当 sponsor 和 IT 确实就什么达成协议, 把它写在双方都能看到的地方**。**记忆是选择性的**。**书面协议在后来分歧出现时更容易回去引用**。

*实践四: 早期浮出分歧, 在双方面前*。**如果你注意到 sponsor 和 IT 在重要的事情上有不同视角, 不要试图悄悄桥接**。**安排一次联合会议, 双方都能发言, 团队呈现各方说了什么, 在双方在场时寻求解决**。**这不舒服, 但产出耐用的协议**。

*实践五: 在需要之前识别升级路径*。**Sponsor 和 IT 如果不能同意会升级到谁?** **有时这是一位共享高管 (CIO 和 CMO 都汇报给 COO, 后者能打破平局)**。**有时除了 CEO 没有共享升级**。**事先知道升级路径, 让你能在分歧不解决时冷静地提议它**。

**当分歧是关于具体技术决策**

**有些分歧是关于具体技术决策**: **用哪个模型厂商、哪个部署平台、哪种集成方式**。**对这些, 对的动作是把分歧从偏好转换成证据**。

**团队产出一份决策 memo (用 15.3 的格式), 用双方都同意重要的标准对照选项**。**选项被诚实评估, 包括它们的缺点**。**建议是团队的, 基于评估**。**Sponsor 和 IT 看同一份 memo, 在同一次会议里**。

**这奏效, 因为它把对话从 "你偏好什么" 转换成 "标准建议什么"**。**当标准明确、证据被呈现时, 大多数分歧崩溃**。**那些持久的少数, 从开始就不是关于技术决策, 现在升级路径可用, 因为团队能展示他们诚实做了分析**。

**当分歧是结构性和持久的**

**有时分歧不是关于具体决策, 而是关于业务和 IT 之间更广的关系**。**Sponsor 把 IT 看作阻断者**。**IT 把 sponsor 看作鲁莽**。**任一方都不会为你解决这个**。**这种情况下, 团队的工作是跟双方专业地操作, 同时悄悄向 engagement partner 或团队自己的领导提出结构性问题**。

**结构性分歧的标志**: **多个决策花的时间比应该的长、双方常规跟团队私下谈论对方、联合会议上做出的协议事后被单方面重谈、双方都不信任对方的承诺**。**当这是 pattern, 项目无论团队做什么都会挣扎, 对的动作是在 engagement-partner 层级浮出结构性问题, 让它能在对的高度被讨论**。

**PM 决策点**

- 我**有没有认真对待双方的关切**, 还是**倾向 sponsor、打发 IT**?
- 我**有没有直接验证关于另一边立场的声明**, 而不是依赖一边报告的?
- 当分歧浮出, 我**有没有在联合会议里浮出它**, 还是**试图悄悄桥接**?

**注意事项**

- **IT 团队可能慢, 但他们很少错**。**他们的关切通常有根据**。**打发 IT 是一个之后伤人的常见错误**。
- **Sponsor 可能急躁, 但他们对紧迫性通常对**。**他们对时间线的压力通常被 IT 侧不总能看到的业务现实告知**。
- **避免变成两者之间的传话员**。**如果你发现自己在传递消息, 那是一个你应该改为安排联合会议的信号**。

**要点总结: Sponsor 和 IT 分歧在很多 AI 项目里是结构性的。识别来源 (速度 vs 标准、厂商选择、build vs buy、拥有权)、认真对待双方、直接验证声明、写下协议、在联合会议里浮出分歧、在需要之前知道升级路径。在这个缝隙里有效操作的团队, 交付出其他团队做不到的项目。**

**Qantas 案例**

设想你跑一个 Qantas AI 项目, **给营销团队建一个客户洞察工具**: **一个系统从多个来源总结客户反馈、为营销活动浮出主题**。**运营 sponsor 是营销分析负责人, 一位渴望部署 AI 的有经验高管**。**IT 负责人是平台工程负责人, 一位对新厂商和集成债务谨慎的有经验高管**。

**分歧第 2 周浮出**。**运营 sponsor 想用一家具体厂商的 AI 平台, 她在一次会议上看过演示**。**这个厂商不在 Qantas 批准供应商清单上, 没被安全 review, 用一种不符合 Qantas 标准集成 pattern 的模型部署方式**。**IT 负责人想要么在 Qantas 现有平台上建, 要么走完厂商的完整批准, 后者要 8 到 12 周**。

**双方都有合法立场**。**Sponsor 的营销团队有竞争紧迫性**: **竞争对手在部署 AI 营销工具, Qantas 落后**。**IT 负责人有组合视角**: **他见过几年后变成维护负担的厂商集成**。**没人错**。

**PM 在缝隙里刻意操作**。**第一**, **认真对待双方**。**PM 跟 IT 负责人有一次 45 分钟对话, 完整听他的关切, 承认关切有效**。**IT 负责人, 本来预期被推倒, 在这次对话后变得更开放**。

**第二**, **永不让一边为另一边说话**。**Sponsor 顺带提到 "IT 原则上同意了, 他们只需要批准具体厂商"**。**PM 直接跟 IT 负责人验证, 他没原则上同意任何超过 "有一次结构化对话" 的东西**。**错位被私下 note 下来, 在下次联合会议处理**。

**第三**, **维护一份单一共享文档**。**PM 产出一份一页的总结, 关于 sponsor 和 IT 在哪对齐、在哪不对齐**。**它跟双方共享**。**关于分歧的共享视角本身有用**, 因为**它浮出分歧比双方以为的窄**。

**第四**, **联合浮出分歧**。**PM 第 3 周安排一次 60 分钟联合会议, 框架是 "决策: 厂商方式"**。**决策 memo, 48 小时前发, 列三个选项**: **追求 sponsor 偏好的厂商, 带并行的安全 review (风险: 厂商可能不过 review, 项目返工)、在现有 Qantas 平台上建 (慢但长期风险低)、用一个已经在批准清单上的厂商追求更小范围 (折中)**。**标准商定**: **到第一个业务价值的时间、长期维护成本、集成债务、厂商锁定风险**。**团队的建议是第三个选项**。

**会议里, 双方 engage 标准**。**Sponsor 倡导选项一**。**IT 负责人倡导选项二**。**40 分钟之后, 对话转向, 因为标准框架化让 trade off 明确**。**折中选项被双方接受, 带文档化的条件**: **使用更小范围的厂商、sponsor 偏好厂商的安全 review 并行进行供 v2 可能使用、每季度 review 评估是否迁移**。

**协议被文档化**。**双方签字**。**项目推进**。

**有意思的后续**: **第 10 周, IT 出现新的关切, 关于数据怎么传给厂商**。**IT 负责人不升级, 直接跟 PM 提, PM 跟双方一起走过技术细节**。**关系从对抗转变成合作, 因为团队全程不偏不倚地操作**。**IT 负责人信任他的关切会被认真对待**。**Sponsor 信任团队在以负责任的速度推进**。

**平行对照是另一个 Qantas AI 项目**, **团队跟 sponsor 对齐、打发 IT 的关切**。**那个项目用一个没被批准的厂商上线**。**三个月后, 一次 IT 安全 review 发现合规缺口**。**项目被暂停, 同时合规被改造, 花了六周工作、损害了跟 IT 职能的关系, 影响下一轮 AI 项目**。

AI 交付的启示: **Sponsor-IT 分歧是这一行最可预测的 pattern 之一, 处理不好时最贵之一**。**技艺是在中间不偏不倚地操作、认真对待双方、在联合会议里浮出分歧、把主观偏好转换成基于证据的决策**。**发展这种技艺的团队变成 "组织信任他们能导航 AI 政治" 的团队, 在这个学科里是一份深度有价值的声誉**。
