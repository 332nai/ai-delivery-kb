**Definition**

Weekly cadence is the recurring structure of meetings, written updates, and asynchronous touchpoints that keep an AI project running. Designed well, the cadence makes stakeholders feel informed without overburdening anyone. Designed poorly, the cadence becomes a treadmill of redundant meetings producing little decision and much fatigue. This item is about designing a cadence that fits the project's stage, the stakeholder map, and the practical constraints of people's calendars.

**Why it matters in delivery**

Every additional weekly meeting is a tax on the project. Six hours of meetings per week across five team members is 30 hours of capacity, which is most of one person. Yet many AI projects accumulate meetings without ever pruning them, because each one was added for a reason and nobody wants to be the one to say "we should stop this one". The team that designs the cadence deliberately at project start, and reviews it at month boundaries, runs more efficiently and protects time for the actual work.

The second reason cadence matters: different stakeholders need different kinds of contact. Sponsors need confidence-building structured updates. Users need hands-on working sessions. Blockers need narrow targeted check-ins. Influencers need light-touch awareness. Designing the cadence with these differences in mind is more efficient than running every stakeholder through the same weekly meeting.

**How it works**

A typical AI project cadence has four layers, each serving a different purpose. The exact structure varies by project size and client culture, but most projects need something in each layer.

| Layer | Purpose | Frequency | Attendees | Output |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Team standup | Coordinate the delivery team | Daily or 3x weekly, 15 minutes | Delivery team only | Internal alignment |
| Working session | Build with the operating team | Weekly, 60 to 90 minutes | Delivery team plus operating team members | Eval rows, prompt updates, decisions |
| Operating sponsor check-in | Keep the operating sponsor informed and engaged | Weekly, 30 minutes | PM, operating sponsor, sometimes engagement partner | Decisions, risk status, surprises caught early |
| Steering committee | Strategic decisions and visibility | Every 2 to 4 weeks, 60 minutes | Operating sponsor, executive sponsor, key blockers, occasionally influencers | Big decisions, scope changes, escalations |

Beyond the four layers, most projects have ad hoc engagement with specific stakeholders (legal, security, IT) on monthly check-ins. These are scheduled but not full cadence.

**Designing each layer**

*Team standup.* Short and focused. The right question is "what is blocking us" not "what is everyone doing". A 15 minute standup where each person answers two questions (what is blocking me, what do I need from someone else today) is more useful than a 30 minute round robin. If the team is in different time zones or working in different modes, async standups in a shared channel work better than forced video calls.

*Working session.* The working session is where the project actually moves. The agenda is built around specific work products: this week's prompt iteration, the new eval rows, the integration question, the edge case the user team raised. The output is concrete: decisions made, prompts updated, eval rows committed. If a working session ends without concrete output, it was a status meeting in disguise.

*Operating sponsor check-in.* The 30 minute weekly with the operating sponsor is the most important meeting on the schedule. The agenda is consistent: what is the status of the top three risks, what decisions are needed this week, what is the team doing about issues from last week, what is on the horizon. The format is conversational, not a slide deck. The output is a list of decisions and actions, captured in 5 sentences in a follow up email.

*Steering committee.* The steering committee is where strategic visibility happens. Most steering committees fail by being too long (90 minutes of slides), too granular (status updates on individual tasks), or too rare (every 6 weeks, by which point any issue has either resolved or exploded). The right cadence is every 2 to 4 weeks, 60 minutes maximum, with a short standing format: project health (5 minutes), key decisions needed (15 minutes), risks requiring senior attention (15 minutes), open discussion (15 minutes), wrap up (10 minutes).

**What each stakeholder reads versus attends**

A useful distinction: not every stakeholder needs to attend every meeting they have an interest in. Many can be served by reading a written update rather than attending a meeting. The team's job is to make the right things readable so attendance can be reserved for the things that need it.

A useful pattern:

| Stakeholder type | Attends | Reads |
|---|---|---|
| Operating sponsor | Weekly check-in, steering committee, working sessions as desired | Decision memos, change requests, weekly summary |
| Executive sponsor | Steering committee | Pre-read for steering committee, monthly written summary |
| Users | Working sessions | Working session notes |
| Blockers | Monthly check-in (their function specific) | Pre-read for monthly check-in, constraints document |
| Influencers | Occasional working sessions, occasional steering committee | Monthly written summary |

The team's effort in producing readable written materials pays off in reduced meeting load for everyone. A two page weekly summary that 30 people read is cheaper than a 30 person weekly meeting.

**Reviewing the cadence**

The cadence is reviewed at month boundaries, with three questions:

*Which meetings produced decisions or movement this month?* These stay.
*Which meetings produced only status?* These get replaced with written updates if possible.
*Which decisions did not get made because the right meeting did not exist?* These are signals to add a meeting or modify an existing one.

The review is short (15 minutes) but produces real pruning. A project that runs 14 weeks should have its cadence reviewed at least twice. A project that runs longer should review monthly.

**PM decision points**

- Have I designed the cadence deliberately at project start, or is it accumulating organically?
- For each meeting on the cadence, can I name the specific purpose and the expected output? If not, why is the meeting happening?
- Have I reviewed and pruned the cadence at the end of the first month?

**Watch outs**

- A weekly steering committee is almost always too frequent. Most issues do not need executive attention every week. Bi-weekly or monthly is usually right, with ad hoc escalations available.
- Beware of meetings where the senior person attends but mostly listens. They either should not be in the meeting (their time is wasted) or they should be invited to actually decide something (and the meeting agenda should support that).
- Async updates work for some stakeholders and not others. Some senior people will not read written summaries. For those, a 15 minute call is more effective than a 5 page memo nobody opens.

**Takeaway: design the weekly cadence with four layers (team standup, working session, sponsor check-in, steering committee), serving different stakeholders with the right contact intensity. Prefer written updates for awareness, meetings for decision. Review and prune at month boundaries.**

**Qantas story**

Imagine starting a Qantas AI project to build a tool for the airport ground operations team: a system that helps duty managers respond to operational disruptions by drafting initial customer communications and surfacing relevant policy. The team is six people. The client side has an operating sponsor (senior ground operations manager), an executive sponsor (head of airport operations), key blockers (legal, security, customer experience), and several user groups (duty managers across multiple airports).

The PM designs the cadence in week one, deliberately. The structure has four layers.

A daily 15 minute team standup, video, focused on blockers. Async-friendly for team members in different airports. Output: a daily Slack message with blockers surfaced.

A weekly 90 minute working session with two duty managers (selected to represent different airports), focused on iterating prompts, reviewing model outputs, and capturing eval rows. Output: committed prompt updates and a growing eval set.

A weekly 30 minute check-in between the PM and the operating sponsor. Format: conversational, agenda emailed two hours before. Output: decision summary emailed afterwards.

A bi-weekly 60 minute steering committee, attended by operating sponsor, executive sponsor, key blockers (rotating, not all at once), and the engagement partner. Pre-read sent 24 hours before. Output: documented decisions.

In parallel, monthly 30 minute check-ins with legal and security, separate from the steering committee. Quarterly briefings with the customer experience leadership team (who are influencers, not in formal governance).

Total weekly meeting time across the project: roughly 4 hours for the PM, 1 to 2 hours for each team member, plus the working sessions. This is meaningful but not excessive.

The first month review surfaces two adjustments. The daily standup is reduced to 3x weekly because nothing was happening on the missing days. The bi-weekly steering committee is shifted to be 75 minutes rather than 60, because the previous version was consistently running over. The monthly legal check-in is collapsed into the steering committee because it was only producing a status update that could be written.

The second month review surfaces more. The working session expands to 2 hours because the team and duty managers are working on increasingly complex prompts and need more time. A new ad hoc meeting type is introduced: 30 minute "edge case clinics" with the customer experience team, scheduled twice over the next two months, to work through specific edge cases the eval set has surfaced.

The cadence is not static. It is a designed thing that evolves with the project. By project end, the meeting load has been roughly stable but the meeting content has shifted from setup and exploration in early weeks to iteration and refinement in middle weeks to launch readiness and handover in late weeks. The total time investment is appropriate to the value produced.

The interesting comparison is with another Qantas AI project that ran in parallel. That project never designed its cadence deliberately. It accumulated meetings: a daily standup, two weekly working sessions, a weekly sponsor check-in, a weekly steering committee, and ad hoc meetings whenever a question arose. By month two, the team was spending 12 hours a week in meetings, complaining about lack of time to do actual work. By month three, several meetings were being skipped, and the discipline of the project was eroding.

The lesson for AI delivery is that the weekly cadence is a designed artifact, not an organic accumulation. The design has four layers, each serving a specific purpose. The cadence is reviewed and pruned at month boundaries. The total time spent in meetings should be in proportion to the value those meetings produce. Most AI projects, left to themselves, accumulate too many meetings. The PM's job is to design and prune deliberately, protecting the team's time for the work that actually moves the project forward.

**定义**

每周节奏是**项目运转所依赖的会议、书面更新、异步触点的反复结构**。设计得好, 节奏让 stakeholder 感觉被知会, 不让任何人过载。设计得差, 节奏变成跑步机, 充满冗余会议, 产出少量决策和大量疲倦。这一节是关于设计一个匹配项目阶段、干系人地图、人们日历实际约束的节奏。

**为何在交付中重要**

每一次额外的每周会议是项目的一笔税。五个团队成员每周 6 小时会议是 30 小时容量, 几乎一个人。但很多 AI 项目积累会议从不修剪, 因为**每个会议加进来时都有理由, 没人想做那个说 "我们应该停掉这个" 的人**。项目开始时刻意设计节奏、月度边界 review 的团队, 跑得更高效, 保护实际工作的时间。

节奏重要的第二个原因: 不同 stakeholder 需要不同种类的接触。Sponsor 需要建立信心的结构化更新。用户需要动手的工作会议。阻断者需要窄而靶向的 check-in。影响者需要轻触的觉知。带着这些差异设计节奏, 比让每个 stakeholder 都参加同一个每周会议更高效。

**运作原理**

典型的 AI 项目节奏有四个层级, 每个服务不同目的。确切结构按项目大小和客户文化变化, 但大多数项目每个层级都需要点东西。

| 层级 | 目的 | 频率 | 参加者 | 产出 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 团队 standup | 协调交付团队 | 每日或每周三次, 15 分钟 | 仅交付团队 | 内部对齐 |
| 工作会议 | 跟运营团队一起建造 | 每周, 60 到 90 分钟 | 交付团队加运营团队成员 | eval 行、prompt 更新、决策 |
| 运营 sponsor check-in | 让运营 sponsor 被知会和 engage | 每周, 30 分钟 | PM、运营 sponsor、有时 engagement partner | 决策、风险状态、早抓的意外 |
| Steering committee | 战略决策和可见性 | 每 2 到 4 周, 60 分钟 | 运营 sponsor、executive sponsor、关键阻断者、偶尔影响者 | 大决策、范围变更、升级 |

四个层级之外, 大多数项目跟具体 stakeholder (法务、安全、IT) 有 ad hoc engagement, 每月 check-in。这些被安排但不是完整节奏。

**设计每个层级**

*团队 standup*。**短而聚焦**。**对的问题是 "我们卡在什么上" 而不是 "每个人在做什么"**。**15 分钟的 standup, 每个人答两个问题 (今天什么卡住我、今天我需要别人什么), 比 30 分钟的轮流报告更有用**。**如果团队在不同时区或不同工作模式下, 在共享渠道里的异步 standup 比强迫视频通话更好**。

*工作会议*。**工作会议是项目实际推进的地方**。**议程围绕具体工作产物**: **本周的 prompt 迭代、新的 eval 行、集成问题、用户团队提出的边缘情况**。**产出是具体的**: **做出的决策、更新的 prompt、提交的 eval 行**。**如果工作会议结束时没具体产出, 它是一个伪装的 status 会议**。

*运营 sponsor check-in*。**跟运营 sponsor 的 30 分钟每周, 是日程上最重要的会议**。**议程一致**: **前三条风险的状态、本周需要的决策、团队对上周问题做了什么、地平线上有什么**。**格式是对话性的, 不是 slide deck**。**产出是一份决策和行动清单, 用 5 句话写在后续邮件里**。

*Steering committee*。**Steering committee 是战略可见性发生的地方**。**大多数 steering committee 失败, 因为太长 (90 分钟 slide)、太粒度细 (单个任务的 status 更新)、或太稀 (每 6 周一次, 那时候任何问题都已经解决或爆炸)**。**对的节奏是每 2 到 4 周, 最多 60 分钟, 用一个短的标准格式**: **项目健康 (5 分钟)、需要的关键决策 (15 分钟)、需要资深关注的风险 (15 分钟)、开放讨论 (15 分钟)、收尾 (10 分钟)**。

**每位 stakeholder 读什么 vs 参加什么**

一个有用的区分: 不是每位有兴趣的 stakeholder 都需要参加他们感兴趣的每个会议。很多人可以通过读书面更新而不是参加会议得到服务。团队的工作是把对的东西做成可读的, 这样出席可以留给真的需要的事。

一个有用的 pattern:

| Stakeholder 类型 | 参加 | 读 |
|---|---|---|
| 运营 sponsor | 每周 check-in、steering committee、按需的工作会议 | 决策 memo、change request、每周总结 |
| Executive sponsor | Steering committee | Steering committee 的预读、每月书面总结 |
| 用户 | 工作会议 | 工作会议笔记 |
| 阻断者 | 每月 check-in (他们职能特有) | 每月 check-in 的预读、约束文档 |
| 影响者 | 偶尔的工作会议、偶尔的 steering committee | 每月书面总结 |

团队产出可读的书面材料的努力, 通过减少所有人的会议负担来回报。30 个人读的两页每周总结, 比 30 人每周会议便宜。

**Review 节奏**

节奏在月度边界 review, 带三个问题:

*本月哪些会议产出了决策或推进?* **这些留下**。
*哪些会议只产出了 status?* **如果可能换成书面更新**。
*哪些决策没做出来, 是因为对的会议不存在?* **这些是加一个会议或修改现有会议的信号**。

Review 短 (15 分钟), 但产出真的修剪。14 周的项目至少 review 节奏两次。更长的项目每月 review。

**PM 决策点**

- 我**有没有在项目开始时刻意设计节奏**, 还是**它在有机积累**?
- 节奏上的每个会议, 我**能不能说出具体目的和预期产出**? **如果不能, 这个会议为什么在发生**?
- 我**有没有在第一个月末 review 和修剪节奏**?

**注意事项**

- **每周 steering committee 几乎总是太频繁**。**大多数 issue 不需要每周高管关注**。**双周或每月通常对的, 可以 ad hoc 升级**。
- **警惕那些资深人物出席但大部分时间在听的会议**。**他们要么不应该在会议里 (时间被浪费), 要么应该被邀请来真的决定什么 (会议议程应该支持那个)**。
- **异步更新对某些 stakeholder 奏效、对某些不**。**某些资深人不会读书面总结**。**对那些人, 15 分钟通话比一份没人打开的 5 页 memo 更有效**。

**要点总结: 用四个层级 (团队 standup、工作会议、sponsor check-in、steering committee) 设计每周节奏, 用对的接触强度服务不同 stakeholder。觉知用书面更新, 决策用会议。月度边界 review 并修剪。**

**Qantas 案例**

设想你启动一个 Qantas AI 项目, 给机场地面运营团队的工具: 一个系统帮值班经理通过起草初始客户沟通和浮出相关政策来应对运营中断。**团队六人**。客户侧有运营 sponsor (资深地面运营经理)、executive sponsor (机场运营负责人)、关键阻断者 (法务、安全、客户体验)、几个用户群体 (多个机场的值班经理)。

PM 第 1 周刻意设计节奏。结构有四个层级。

每天 15 分钟团队 standup, 视频, 聚焦阻断者。异步友好, 因为团队成员在不同机场。**产出**: 每日 Slack 消息, 浮出阻断者。

每周 90 分钟工作会议, 跟两位值班经理 (选自代表不同机场), 聚焦迭代 prompt、review 模型输出、捕捉 eval 行。**产出**: 提交的 prompt 更新和增长的 eval set。

每周 30 分钟 check-in, PM 和运营 sponsor。**格式**: 对话性, 议程两小时前邮件。**产出**: 之后邮件发的决策总结。

双周 60 分钟 steering committee, 运营 sponsor、executive sponsor、关键阻断者 (轮换, 不一起) 和 engagement partner 参加。预读 24 小时前发。**产出**: **文档化的决策**。

平行地, 每月 30 分钟跟法务和安全的 check-in, 跟 steering committee 分开。每季度跟客户体验领导团队 (他们是影响者, 不在正式治理) 的简报。

项目期间每周会议总时间: PM 大约 4 小时, 每个团队成员 1 到 2 小时, 加上工作会议。有意义但不过分。

第一个月 review 浮出两个调整。每日 standup 减到每周三次, 因为缺失的那几天没事情发生。双周 steering committee 调到 75 分钟而不是 60 分钟, 因为之前的版本一直超时。每月法务 check-in 被并入 steering committee, 因为它只产出能写下来的 status 更新。

第二个月 review 浮出更多。工作会议扩到 2 小时, 因为团队和值班经理在做越来越复杂的 prompt, 需要更多时间。一种新的 ad hoc 会议类型被引入: 30 分钟的 "边缘情况 clinic" 跟客户体验团队, 接下来两个月安排两次, 处理 eval set 浮出的具体边缘情况。

节奏不是静态的。它是一个被设计的东西, 随项目演化。项目结束时, 会议负载大致稳定, 但会议内容从早期周的设置和探索, 转到中期周的迭代和精炼, 转到后期周的上线准备和移交。总时间投入跟产出的价值匹配。

有意思的对照是同期跑的另一个 Qantas AI 项目。那个项目从不刻意设计节奏。**它累积会议**: 每日 standup、每周两次工作会议、每周 sponsor check-in、每周 steering committee、任何问题出现时的 ad hoc 会议。第二个月, 团队每周花 12 小时在会议里, 抱怨没时间做实际工作。第三个月, 几个会议在被跳过, 项目的纪律在侵蚀。

AI 交付的启示: 每周节奏是一个被设计的产物, 不是有机积累。设计有四个层级, 每个服务一个具体目的。节奏在月度边界 review 并修剪。总会议时间应该跟那些会议产出的价值成比例。大多数 AI 项目, 任其自然, 会积累太多会议。PM 的工作是刻意设计和修剪, 保护团队的时间, 用于真的推动项目前进的工作。
