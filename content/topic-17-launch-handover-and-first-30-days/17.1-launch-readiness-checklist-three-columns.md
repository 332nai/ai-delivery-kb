**Definition**

Launch readiness is the structured assessment of whether an AI system is ready to move from build to production use. The assessment covers three categories that are often confused with each other: technical readiness (does the system work), operational readiness (can the client run it), and commercial readiness (have the contractual and financial conditions been met). A system can be technically ready and operationally not ready, or operationally ready and commercially not ready. Launching when any one column is unfinished produces a predictable kind of post-launch crisis specific to that column.

**Why it matters in delivery**

The most common launch failure pattern is launching when the technical column is green and assuming the other two are also green. Technical readiness is the most visible: it has eval scores, demos, and engineering sign offs. Operational readiness and commercial readiness are less visible but no less important. A system that works technically but cannot be supported by the client's operations team becomes shelfware within weeks. A system that launches before the commercial framework is finalised creates payment disputes, support gaps, and contractual ambiguity that takes months to clean up.

The three column structure exists to force the team to consider all three explicitly. Each column has its own owner, its own evidence, and its own acceptance criteria. The launch is not ready until all three columns are signed off.

**How it works**

The checklist has roughly 8 to 12 items in each column. The exact items vary by project, but the table below covers the common ground.

| Column | Item | Acceptance evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Technical | Eval pass rate at or above target | Latest eval run, dated, signed off |
| Technical | Production environment deployed and tested | Smoke test results from production env |
| Technical | Monitoring and alerting configured | Test alert fired and acknowledged |
| Technical | Rollback procedure tested | Rollback drill conducted within last 2 weeks |
| Technical | Security and data flow review complete | Signed off by client security |
| Technical | Performance under expected load tested | Load test results matching production estimates |
| Operational | Operating team trained on the system | Training session held, attendance recorded |
| Operational | Runbook written and reviewed | Runbook signed off by named operating owner |
| Operational | On-call coverage assigned | Schedule for first 30 days agreed in writing |
| Operational | Incident response process tested | Tabletop exercise conducted |
| Operational | User-facing documentation complete | Docs reviewed by representative users |
| Operational | Feedback channel established | Channel created, monitoring assigned |
| Commercial | Production phase fee structure agreed | Signed amendment or new SOW |
| Commercial | Support and maintenance terms agreed | Terms written and signed |
| Commercial | Ownership of outputs and data clarified | Documented in writing |
| Commercial | Acceptance criteria for production phase agreed | Written and signed |
| Commercial | Communication and escalation contacts named | List shared with both sides |
| Commercial | Renewal or extension path defined | Documented even if not committed |

Items are marked complete only with evidence. A verbal "yes we are ready" does not count. The completeness of evidence is what makes the checklist useful.

**Running the readiness review**

The review is a single meeting, 90 minutes, with all three column owners present. The meeting structure:

1. Walk through each item in each column. Owner confirms or describes the gap.
2. Items with gaps are noted with owner and deadline.
3. Items with hard blockers (cannot be completed before launch) trigger a discussion: delay launch, launch with documented risk acceptance, or descope.
4. Output: signed off checklist, gap list with owners and deadlines, decision on launch date.

The single most important behaviour: do not let the meeting become a negotiation about the criteria. The criteria are agreed in advance. The meeting is about whether each criterion is met, not about whether the criterion was reasonable. If the team finds itself debating whether a criterion was fair, the readiness review has failed.

**Common gaps by column**

The most common gaps that surface in the readiness review, by column:

*Technical gaps.* The most common is monitoring being configured but not tested. Teams often deploy monitoring code and assume it works, without firing a test alert to confirm the alert reaches the right person. Always test the alert.

*Operational gaps.* The most common is operating team training being held but not absorbed. A 90 minute training session does not produce competence. Combine training with shadowing, written documentation, and an explicit period of co-running before the team is left alone.

*Commercial gaps.* The most common is the production fee structure being assumed rather than agreed. Teams often launch with the same fee that the build phase used, on the assumption that the client will agree later. This rarely works. Negotiate the production phase fees before launch.

**PM decision points**

- For each item on the checklist, do I have evidence (not opinion) that it is complete?
- Have I run the readiness review with all three column owners present, or have I only checked the technical column?
- For each gap, do I have an owner and a deadline, or is the gap floating?

**Watch outs**

- The temptation is to launch when technical is ready and treat operational and commercial as something to figure out in production. This is the most expensive shortcut in AI delivery. The first week of production is when the other two columns matter most.
- "We will train the team after launch" is almost always wrong. Operational readiness has to be ready at launch, not deferred.
- Commercial readiness slips quietly because nobody owns it on the delivery side. The PM should own this or explicitly delegate to someone who will.

**Takeaway: launch readiness is three columns, not one. Technical, operational, commercial. Each has 8 to 12 items, each with evidence-based acceptance. Run the readiness review with all three owners present. Do not launch when any column is incomplete unless the risk is explicitly accepted in writing.**

**Qantas story**

Imagine approaching the launch of a Qantas AI project, a tool for the customer service team that classifies and routes incoming complaints to the right resolution queue. The build has gone well. Eval scores are at target. The team is keen to launch.

The PM runs the readiness review with the technical lead, the operating sponsor, and the engagement partner all present. The technical column is mostly green. Eight of nine items are complete. One gap: the rollback procedure has been documented but not drilled. The team agrees to run a rollback drill the following day before launch.

The operational column reveals more gaps. The customer service team has been trained, but the training was a single 90 minute session two weeks ago, and the team has not used the system since. The runbook exists but has not been reviewed by the named operating owner. The on-call schedule for the first 30 days has not been finalised. The incident response process has been described but not tabletoped.

Each gap has a specific impact. Without recent training, the customer service team will not remember what they were taught. Without runbook review, the operating owner does not know what they are signing up for. Without on-call coverage, the first production issue will go unanswered. Without incident response testing, the team will not know how to respond when something goes wrong.

The team agrees to delay launch by one week. The week is used to run a refresher training session, walk the operating owner through the runbook, finalise the on-call schedule, and conduct a tabletop exercise. None of these tasks are large individually. Collectively they make the difference between a smooth launch and a chaotic first month.

The commercial column has its own gap. The production fee structure has been discussed but not agreed in writing. The team assumed the build phase fee would continue. The operating sponsor assumed there would be a reduced production fee. Neither side had committed to a number. The engagement partner takes responsibility for closing this conversation that week, before launch.

Launch happens one week later than originally planned. The first 30 days are uneventful. The customer service team uses the system confidently because the training was fresh. The runbook is referenced and updated by the operating owner during the first incident. The on-call response is fast because the schedule is clear. The commercial framework is in place, invoices are issued on the agreed terms, no payment disputes.

The interesting comparison is with a parallel Qantas AI project that launched on the originally scheduled date with the technical column green and the other two columns incomplete. That project had three operational issues in the first two weeks that nobody had practiced responding to, two commercial disputes about scope and fees in the first month, and a sponsor who lost confidence in the team. The post-launch recovery took eight weeks of additional work that was outside the original budget.

The lesson for AI delivery is that launch readiness is the single highest-leverage moment in the project. A few days of additional preparation prevent weeks of post-launch firefighting. The three column structure makes the preparation explicit. The discipline is to use it.

**定义**

上线 readiness 是一次结构化评估, 判断一个 AI 系统是否准备好从建造转入生产使用。评估覆盖三类经常被互相混淆的东西: 技术 readiness (系统能工作吗)、运营 readiness (客户能跑它吗)、商务 readiness (合同和财务条件满足了吗)。一个系统可以技术上 ready, 运营上不 ready, 或运营上 ready, 商务上不 ready。在任何一栏没完成时上线, 会产出那一栏特有的、可预测的上线后危机。

**为何在交付中重要**

最常见的上线失败 pattern, 是在技术那栏绿了之后就上线, 假设其他两栏也绿了。技术 readiness 最可见: 它有 eval 分数、demo、工程签字。运营 readiness 和商务 readiness 没那么可见, 但同样重要。一个技术上能工作、但客户运营团队没法支持的系统, 几周内就会变成搁置软件。一个在商务框架定稿之前上线的系统, 会制造付款纠纷、支持缺口、合同模糊, 要几个月才清理干净。

三栏结构存在的目的, 是逼团队明确考虑三件事。每栏有自己的 owner、自己的证据、自己的验收标准。所有三栏签字之前, 上线不算 ready。

**运作原理**

Checklist 每栏大约 8 到 12 项。具体项目按项目变化, 但下表覆盖常见地面。

| 栏目 | 项目 | 验收证据 |
|---|---|---|
| 技术 | Eval 通过率达到或超过目标 | 最新一次 eval 跑的结果, 带日期, 签过字 |
| 技术 | 生产环境已部署并测试 | 生产环境上的 smoke test 结果 |
| 技术 | 监控和告警已配置 | 测试告警触发并被确认收到 |
| 技术 | 回滚流程已测试 | 过去 2 周内做过一次回滚演练 |
| 技术 | 安全和数据流 review 完成 | 客户安全部门签字 |
| 技术 | 预期负载下的性能测试 | 负载测试结果匹配生产预估 |
| 运营 | 运营团队对系统培训过 | 培训 session 召开过, 出勤记录在 |
| 运营 | Runbook 写好并 review 过 | 由具名的运营 owner 签字 |
| 运营 | On-call 覆盖分配好 | 前 30 天的排班书面商定 |
| 运营 | 事件响应流程测试过 | 跑过一次桌面演练 |
| 运营 | 面向用户的文档完成 | 代表性用户 review 过 |
| 运营 | 反馈渠道建立 | 渠道创建, 监控分配 |
| 商务 | 生产阶段费率结构商定 | 签过字的合同修订或新 SOW |
| 商务 | 支持和维护条款商定 | 条款书面化并签字 |
| 商务 | 输出和数据的拥有权澄清 | 书面文档 |
| 商务 | 生产阶段的验收标准商定 | 书面化并签字 |
| 商务 | 沟通和升级联系人具名 | 清单给到双方 |
| 商务 | 续约或扩展路径定义 | 文档化, 即使还没承诺 |

每一项只有带证据才能标完成。口头的 "我们准备好了" 不算。证据的完整性是这个 checklist 有用的原因。

**跑 readiness review**

Review 是一次会议, 90 分钟, 三栏的 owner 都在场。会议结构:

1. 走过每栏的每一项。Owner 确认或描述缺口。
2. 有缺口的项目记录下 owner 和 deadline。
3. 有硬阻塞的项目 (上线前完成不了) 触发讨论: 推迟上线、带书面风险接受上线、或缩范围。
4. 产出: 签过字的 checklist、缺口清单带 owner 和 deadline、上线日期决定。

会议里最重要的单一行为: 不要让会议变成关于标准的谈判。标准是事先商定的。会议是关于每个标准是否被满足, 不是关于这个标准是否合理。如果团队发现自己在辩论某个标准公不公平, readiness review 已经失败了。

**每栏的常见缺口**

Readiness review 上最常浮出的缺口, 按栏分:

*技术缺口。* 最常见的是监控被配置但没测试过。团队经常部署监控代码, 假设它工作, 但没触发一次测试告警来确认告警到达对的人。永远测试告警。

*运营缺口。* 最常见的是运营团队培训过但没吸收。一次 90 分钟的培训不产出能力。把培训跟 shadowing、书面文档、以及在团队被单独留下之前的明确共跑期结合起来。

*商务缺口。* 最常见的是生产费率结构被假设而不是被商定。团队经常用建造阶段同样的费率上线, 假设客户以后会同意。这很少奏效。在上线前谈判生产阶段费率。

**PM 决策点**

- Checklist 上的每一项, 我有没有证据 (不是观点) 它完成了?
- 我有没有跟三栏 owner 都在场的情况下跑 readiness review, 还是只 check 了技术那栏?
- 每个缺口, 我有没有 owner 和 deadline, 还是缺口在悬置?

**注意事项**

- 诱惑是在技术 ready 时上线, 把运营和商务当作生产里搞清楚的事。这是 AI 交付里最贵的捷径。生产的第一周, 是另外两栏最重要的时候。
- "我们上线后再培训团队" 几乎总是错的。运营 readiness 必须在上线时 ready, 不是推迟。
- 商务 readiness 悄悄滑掉, 因为没人在交付侧拥有它。PM 应该拥有这个, 或者明确委托给一个会拥有的人。

**要点总结: 上线 readiness 是三栏, 不是一栏。技术、运营、商务。每栏 8 到 12 项, 每项有基于证据的验收。跟三栏 owner 都在场跑 readiness review。任何一栏不完整时不要上线, 除非风险被书面明确接受。**

**Qantas 案例**

设想你接近一个 Qantas AI 项目的上线, 一个给客服团队的工具, 把进来的投诉分类并路由到对的解决队列。建造跑得好。Eval 分数在目标上。团队迫不及待要上线。

PM 跑 readiness review, 技术负责人、运营 sponsor、engagement partner 都在场。技术栏大部分绿。九项里有八项完成。一个缺口: 回滚流程已经文档化但没演练过。团队同意第二天上线前跑一次回滚演练。

运营栏揭示更多缺口。客服团队培训过, 但培训是两周前的一次 90 分钟 session, 团队此后没用过系统。Runbook 存在但具名的运营 owner 没 review 过。前 30 天的 on-call 排班还没定下来。事件响应流程被描述过但没桌面演练过。

每个缺口有具体影响。没有近期培训, 客服团队不会记得他们学过什么。没有 runbook review, 运营 owner 不知道自己在签什么。没有 on-call 覆盖, 第一个生产 issue 会无人响应。没有事件响应测试, 团队不知道出问题时怎么响应。

团队同意推迟上线一周。这一周用来跑一次复习培训 session, 带运营 owner 走一遍 runbook, 定下 on-call 排班, 跑一次桌面演练。这些任务单独看都不大。合起来就是顺利上线和混乱第一个月的区别。

商务栏有自己的缺口。生产费率结构被讨论过但没书面商定。团队假设建造阶段费率会延续。运营 sponsor 假设会有降低的生产费率。没有一方在数字上承诺过。Engagement partner 承担责任在那一周内, 上线前, 关掉这个对话。

上线发生在比原计划晚一周。前 30 天平稳无事。客服团队自信地用系统, 因为培训新鲜。Runbook 在第一个事件中被运营 owner 引用和更新。On-call 响应快, 因为排班清楚。商务框架就位, invoice 按商定条款发出, 没有付款纠纷。

有意思的对照是一个并行的 Qantas AI 项目, 按原定日期、技术栏绿、其他两栏不完整就上线。那个项目前两周有三个运营 issue, 没人练习过怎么响应; 第一个月有两个关于范围和费率的商务纠纷; sponsor 失去了对团队的信心。上线后恢复花了八周额外工作, 在原始预算之外。

AI 交付的启示是, 上线 readiness 是项目里杠杆最高的单一时刻。几天的额外准备防止几周的上线后救火。三栏结构让准备明确。纪律是用它。
