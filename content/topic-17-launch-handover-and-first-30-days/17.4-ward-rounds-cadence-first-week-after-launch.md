**Definition**

Ward rounds is the borrowed medical term for a structured daily check on a newly-launched AI system in its first week of production. The metaphor comes from how hospital doctors round on recently-admitted patients: see each one every day, ask specific diagnostic questions, look for early signs of complications, and intervene before issues become serious. For an AI system, the equivalent is a 30 minute daily session in the first week of production where the team and operating team systematically check the system's health, surface any concerns, and decide on any interventions. Done well, it catches the issues that the launch readiness review missed.

**Why it matters in delivery**

The first week of production is when AI systems are most fragile. The system has been tested but not stress-tested under real production conditions. The operating team is new to using it. The user population is using it in ways the team did not anticipate. Edge cases are surfacing that the eval set did not include. The probability of an issue occurring is higher than at any other point in the system's life. The cost of catching an issue on day two versus day twelve is the difference between a quick fix and a recovery effort.

The ward rounds cadence creates the conditions for catching issues early. Without it, the team's attention is fragmented: the technical lead is watching dashboards, the operating sponsor is watching the operating team, the PM is watching everyone, but nobody is bringing the signals together. The daily session forces synthesis, which is where the leverage is.

**How it works**

Ward rounds is a 30 minute meeting each weekday morning in the first week of production. The attendees are the PM, the technical lead, the operating sponsor, and a rotating member of the operating team who used the system the previous day. The structure:

| Section | Duration | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Dashboard review | 10 minutes | Walk through the four monitoring signals from 17.3, note any deviation from baseline |
| Issue triage | 10 minutes | Surface any issues reported or observed in the last 24 hours, categorise by severity |
| User and operating team feedback | 5 minutes | The rotating operating team member shares observations from actual use |
| Action items | 5 minutes | Decide on any interventions, assign owners, set deadlines |

The meeting starts with the same agenda every day, even when there is little to discuss. The discipline of the cadence is more important than the content of any individual session.

**What good looks like on each day**

Each day of the first week tends to surface different categories of issue. Knowing what to expect makes it easier to spot deviations.

*Day 1.* The first real production day. The signals to watch: did anything break in the first hour, did users find the system, did the operating team encounter any handover issues. The most common day 1 issue is a routing or access problem: a user group that was supposed to have access does not, or a use case that was supposed to be in scope is not flowing through.

*Day 2.* The first day with overnight data. The signals to watch: did anything happen overnight, are usage patterns matching the projected volume, are any of the four monitoring signals trending in unexpected directions. Day 2 often reveals usage patterns the team did not expect.

*Day 3.* The first day where real usage has accumulated enough to evaluate quality. The signals to watch: user feedback, refusal patterns, output quality on a sample. Day 3 is when the first quality concerns typically surface.

*Day 4.* The first day where operating team observations are mature. The signals to watch: what the operating team is saying when not asked, what frictions they are experiencing, what they are quietly working around. Day 4 often surfaces operating issues that were invisible on day 1.

*Day 5.* The first day where week-over-week trend can start. The signals to watch: which issues from earlier in the week are resolved, which are recurring, which are getting worse. Day 5 is when the team decides whether to extend ward rounds into a second week.

The pattern is not strict, but it is a useful frame for what to expect each day.

**The rotating operating team member**

The rotating operating team member is the most underrated part of ward rounds. Bringing a different team member each day produces several benefits. First, the team member's perspective is fresh, not yet shaped by the delivery team's framing. Second, the member learns the operational rhythms of the system by participating, which builds capability in the operating team. Third, the operating team feels included rather than handed-to, which improves relationship through the first month.

A useful selection rule: invite the team member who used the system most the previous day, regardless of seniority. This connects the discussion to real usage rather than to managerial summary.

**When to extend ward rounds**

The default is to run ward rounds for five business days, then transition to a less intensive cadence (twice a week, then weekly) over the following weeks. Some signals suggest extending the daily cadence:

A significant issue is unresolved at end of week 1. Continue ward rounds until it is resolved.

User feedback is mixed in a way that needs daily synthesis. Continue for one more week.

The operating team is not yet confident running the system independently. Extend until they are.

Conversely, some signals suggest shortening: everything is smooth, all four signals are green, the operating team is independent. End ward rounds after day 3 or 4 and move to weekly.

**Transitioning out of ward rounds**

The transition is deliberate. At end of week 1, the team and operating sponsor decide on the next cadence: twice weekly, weekly, or as needed. The decision is documented. The team's role shifts from "watching daily" to "available when called". The operating team's role shifts from "supported by daily presence" to "owning the system with support on call".

The transition is the moment when handover becomes real. Done well, the operating team feels empowered. Done poorly, the operating team feels abandoned. The difference is in how explicit the team is about the change, what the new escalation path is, and how the team continues to be available without being daily-present.

**PM decision points**

- Have I scheduled ward rounds before launch, or am I planning to figure out the post-launch cadence afterwards?
- Is the rotating operating team member being invited, or are the same three people meeting every day?
- Am I making the transition out of ward rounds explicit, or letting the cadence fade?

**Watch outs**

- Ward rounds is not status reporting. The discipline is to surface issues, not to confirm that nothing is wrong. If every session ends with "everything is fine", either the meeting is too short or the team is suppressing concerns.
- The 30 minute limit matters. Longer than 30 minutes and the meeting becomes a working session that takes time from actual work. Keep it tight.
- Avoid using ward rounds to discuss longer-term roadmap items. Park those for separate meetings. Ward rounds is about the immediate week.

**Takeaway: ward rounds is a 30 minute daily meeting in the first week of production. The structure is dashboard review, issue triage, operating team feedback, action items. A rotating operating team member ensures real-usage perspective. Default to five days, then transition to a less intensive cadence deliberately. The cadence catches issues early when they are cheapest to fix.**

**Qantas story**

Imagine launching a Qantas AI project, a tool for the cargo customer service team that drafts responses to shipping enquiries. Launch happens on a Monday. The PM has scheduled ward rounds for each morning of the launch week, 9am for 30 minutes, with the PM, the technical lead, the operating sponsor (cargo customer service manager), and a rotating cargo customer service agent.

Monday ward rounds is uneventful. The system went live at 8am, has been running for an hour, no incidents. The agents have been using it. The signals are all baseline. Action items: confirm that all four customer service teams have access by end of day. The rotating agent is the agent who happened to be on the early shift.

Tuesday ward rounds reveals the first overnight signal. The dashboard shows a 12 percent refusal rate, which is above the 5 percent baseline. The PM and technical lead investigate during the meeting. The pattern: overnight enquiries from international customers are being refused because the prompts had not been tested with non-English business terminology. The fix is to expand the prompt to handle the most common non-English terms. Implementation is scoped for that day, deployed by 3pm. Refusal rate returns to baseline by Wednesday morning.

Wednesday ward rounds focuses on quality. With two days of usage, there is enough volume to sample. The rotating agent (a senior agent who used the system most heavily the previous day) brings a specific observation: the system is producing technically accurate responses but with a tone that is more formal than the cargo customer service team's voice. Customers are responding to drafts with phrases like "thank you for the prompt response" which suggests the system is being perceived as a different team than usual. The team agrees to adjust the prompt to match the team's house voice. Implementation that day, monitoring the change over the next 48 hours.

Thursday ward rounds surfaces an operational issue. The rotating agent (a junior agent) describes a workflow friction: when the system produces a draft, the agent has to copy it into their email tool to send to the customer, which is a multi-step manual process. The agents have been doing this 30 to 50 times a day. The friction is significant. The team agrees that a workflow integration is worth scoping for v2, but the immediate fix is to provide a one-click copy button, which the technical lead deploys before end of day Thursday.

Friday ward rounds reviews the week. Three issues have been surfaced and resolved (refusal rate, tone, copy friction). Two items are deferred to v2 planning. The signals are all healthy. The operating sponsor and the agents are confident running the system into the weekend.

The team agrees to transition to a twice-weekly cadence (Monday and Thursday) for week 2, then weekly for weeks 3 and 4. The transition is documented in writing with explicit acknowledgment of the change in support model.

The interesting comparison is with another Qantas AI project that did not run ward rounds. That project had a similar Monday launch. The Tuesday refusal rate spike was not noticed for four days because nobody was looking at the signal daily. The Wednesday tone issue would have been raised by customers escalating to the customer service manager, who would have raised it to the operating sponsor, by which time the operating sponsor would have framed it as "the AI system has a tone problem and is hurting our customer relationships". The Thursday workflow friction would have produced agent complaints in the second week that the operating sponsor would have framed as "the agents are unhappy with the system". Each of these would have produced a separate crisis conversation, each more politically loaded than the version that surfaced calmly in ward rounds.

The lesson for AI delivery is that the first week of production is the highest leverage period in the entire engagement. The ward rounds cadence is the structure that captures that leverage. The cost is two and a half hours of the PM's time over a week. The value is the difference between catching issues at day 2 versus day 12, and between a confident operating team and a fragile one. The discipline of running the cadence even when it feels unnecessary on day 1 is what produces the value by day 5.

**定义**

巡房是借用医学术语, 描述对刚上线的 AI 系统在生产第一周的结构化每日检查。这个比喻来自医院医生怎么查刚入院的病人: 每天看一次, 问特定的诊断问题, 找并发症的早期迹象, 在 issue 变严重之前介入。对 AI 系统, 等价物是生产第一周的每日 30 分钟 session, 团队和运营团队系统化地检查系统健康、浮出任何关切、决定任何干预。做得好, 它抓住上线 readiness review 错过的 issue。

**为何在交付中重要**

生产的第一周是 AI 系统最脆弱的时候。系统被测过, 但没在真实生产条件下压力测过。运营团队对它是新手。用户群体以团队没预期的方式用。eval set 没包含的边缘情况在浮出。一个 issue 发生的概率比系统生命中任何其他时刻都高。第二天抓住 issue 跟第十二天抓住的代价区别, 是快速修复跟恢复工作的区别。

巡房节奏创造抓住早期 issue 的条件。没有它, 团队的注意力是分散的: 技术负责人在看 dashboard, 运营 sponsor 在看运营团队, PM 在看所有人, 但没人在把信号聚合起来。每日 session 强制综合, 那是杠杆所在。

**运作原理**

巡房是生产第一周每个工作日早晨的 30 分钟会议。参加者是 PM、技术负责人、运营 sponsor、前一天用过系统的轮换运营团队成员。结构:

| 节 | 时长 | 发生什么 |
|---|---|---|
| Dashboard review | 10 分钟 | 走过 17.3 的四个监控信号, 注意任何偏离基线 |
| Issue triage | 10 分钟 | 浮出过去 24 小时报告或观察到的任何 issue, 按严重程度分类 |
| 用户和运营团队反馈 | 5 分钟 | 轮换的运营团队成员分享真实使用观察 |
| 行动项 | 5 分钟 | 决定任何干预, 分配 owner, 设 deadline |

每天用同样的议程开会, 哪怕没什么可讨论。节奏的纪律比任何单一 session 的内容更重要。

**每天好长什么样**

第一周每天倾向于浮出不同类别的 issue。知道预期什么, 让发现偏离更容易。

*第 1 天。* 第一个真实生产日。要看的信号: 第一小时有没有坏掉的、用户有没有找到系统、运营团队有没有遇到移交 issue。最常见的第 1 天 issue 是路由或访问问题: 本应有访问权的用户群没有、本应在范围内的 use case 没流进来。

*第 2 天。* 第一天带过夜数据。要看的信号: 过夜发生了什么、使用 pattern 是否匹配预测的量、四个监控信号有没有任何在预期外的方向上趋势。第 2 天经常揭示团队没预期到的使用 pattern。

*第 3 天。* 第一天真实使用累积到能评估质量的程度。要看的信号: 用户反馈、拒绝 pattern、采样的输出质量。第 3 天是第一个质量关切典型浮出的时候。

*第 4 天。* 第一天运营团队的观察成熟。要看的信号: 运营团队不被问时在说什么、他们经历什么摩擦、他们在安静地绕过什么。第 4 天经常浮出第 1 天看不见的运营 issue。

*第 5 天。* 第一天周对周趋势能开始的。要看的信号: 本周早些的 issue 哪些解决了、哪些重复、哪些变差。第 5 天是团队决定要不要把巡房延到第二周的时候。

Pattern 不严格, 但它是预期每天什么的有用框架。

**轮换的运营团队成员**

轮换的运营团队成员是巡房最被低估的部分。每天带不同的团队成员产出几个好处。第一, 团队成员的视角新鲜, 还没被交付团队的框架塑造。第二, 成员通过参与学到系统的运营节奏, 在运营团队建造能力。第三, 运营团队感觉被纳入而不是被移交给, 在第一个月改善关系。

一个有用的选择规则: 邀请前一天用系统最多的团队成员, 不管资历。这把讨论连到真实使用, 而不是管理层总结。

**何时延长巡房**

默认是巡房五个工作日, 然后接下来几周过渡到不那么密集的节奏 (一周两次、然后一周一次)。一些信号建议延长每日节奏:

一个显著 issue 在第 1 周末没解决。继续巡房直到它解决。

用户反馈以一种需要每日综合的方式混合。再继续一周。

运营团队还不自信独立跑系统。延长直到他们自信。

反过来, 一些信号建议缩短: 一切顺利、四个信号都绿、运营团队独立。第 3 或 4 天后结束巡房, 改成每周。

**过渡出巡房**

过渡是刻意的。第 1 周末, 团队和运营 sponsor 决定下一个节奏: 每周两次、每周一次、或按需。决定被文档化。团队的角色从"每天看"转向"被叫时在"。运营团队的角色从"被每日在场支持"转向"拥有系统, 带 on-call 支持"。

过渡是移交变真的时刻。做得好, 运营团队感觉被赋权。做得差, 运营团队感觉被抛弃。区别在于团队对变化多明确、新的升级路径是什么、团队怎么继续可用但不每日在场。

**PM 决策点**

- 我有没有在上线前安排巡房, 还是计划上线后再搞清楚上线后节奏?
- 轮换的运营团队成员有没有被邀请, 还是每天都是同样三个人开会?
- 我有没有让过渡出巡房明确, 还是让节奏自然淡化?

**注意事项**

- 巡房不是 status 汇报。纪律是浮出 issue, 不是确认没事。如果每次 session 都以"一切 fine"结束, 要么会议太短, 要么团队在压抑关切。
- 30 分钟限制重要。超过 30 分钟, 会议变成占用实际工作时间的工作 session。保持紧凑。
- 避免用巡房讨论长期路线图项目。把那些放到单独会议。巡房是关于眼下这周。

**要点总结: 巡房是生产第一周的每日 30 分钟会议。结构是 dashboard review、issue triage、运营团队反馈、行动项。轮换运营团队成员保证真实使用视角。默认五天, 然后刻意过渡到不那么密集的节奏。节奏在 issue 最便宜修的时候抓住它们。**

**Qantas 案例**

设想你上线一个 Qantas AI 项目, 一个给货运客服团队的工具, 起草对运输咨询的回应。上线在周一发生。PM 安排了上线那周每个早晨的巡房, 9 点开 30 分钟, PM、技术负责人、运营 sponsor (货运客服经理)、轮换的货运客服坐席参加。

周一巡房平静无事。系统早 8 点上线, 跑了一小时, 没事件。坐席在用。信号都在基线。行动项: 当天结束前确认所有四个客服团队有访问权。轮换的坐席是早班的那位。

周二巡房揭示第一个过夜信号。Dashboard 显示 12% 拒绝率, 高于 5% 基线。PM 和技术负责人在会议期间调查。Pattern: 来自国际客户的过夜咨询被拒, 因为 prompt 没在非英语商业术语上测过。修复是扩展 prompt 处理最常见的非英语术语。当天 scope, 下午 3 点部署。拒绝率周三早上回到基线。

周三巡房聚焦质量。两天使用, 量够采样了。轮换坐席 (前一天用系统最多的资深坐席) 带一个具体观察: 系统在产出技术上准确的回应, 但语气比货运客服团队的声音更正式。客户在用"感谢及时回应"这样的话回应草稿, 暗示系统被感知为跟平常不同的团队。团队同意调 prompt 匹配团队的内部声音。当天实施, 接下来 48 小时监控变化。

周四巡房浮出一个运营 issue。轮换坐席 (一位初级坐席) 描述工作流摩擦: 系统产出草稿时, 坐席得把它复制到邮件工具发给客户, 这是多步手工流程。坐席每天做 30 到 50 次。摩擦显著。团队同意工作流集成值得为 v2 scope, 但即时修复是提供一键复制按钮, 技术负责人在周四结束前部署。

周五巡房 review 一周。三个 issue 被浮出和解决 (拒绝率、语气、复制摩擦)。两项被推到 v2 规划。信号都健康。运营 sponsor 和坐席自信地把系统带入周末。

团队同意第 2 周过渡到一周两次节奏 (周一和周四), 然后第 3 和 4 周一周一次。过渡书面文档化, 明确承认支持模式的变化。

有意思的对照是另一个没跑巡房的 Qantas AI 项目。那个项目有类似的周一上线。周二的拒绝率飙升没被注意到四天, 因为没人每日看信号。周三的语气 issue 会被客户升级到客服经理, 经理升级到运营 sponsor, 那时运营 sponsor 会框架成"AI 系统有语气问题, 在伤客户关系"。周四的工作流摩擦会在第二周产出坐席投诉, 运营 sponsor 会框架成"坐席对系统不满"。每一个都会产出一场单独的危机对话, 比"在巡房里平静浮出"的版本更政治化。

AI 交付的启示是, 生产第一周是整个合作里杠杆最高的时期。巡房节奏是捕捉那个杠杆的结构。代价是 PM 一周两个半小时。价值是"第 2 天抓住 issue vs 第 12 天抓住"、以及"自信运营团队 vs 脆弱运营团队"的区别。在第 1 天感觉没必要时仍然跑节奏的纪律, 就是产出第 5 天价值的东西。
