**Definition**

The project retrospective is the final structured conversation between the delivery team and the client, typically held three to six weeks after launch when the system has stabilised. The retrospective serves three purposes simultaneously: it documents what was learned for both sides, it captures the project's outcomes for organisational memory, and it sets the tone for the next engagement. The third purpose is the most underrated. A retrospective run poorly leaves the client remembering the project as a series of problems that were eventually solved. A retrospective run well leaves the client remembering the project as a meaningful outcome that the team and the client achieved together. The framing matters, and the framing is mostly determined by how the retrospective is structured.

**Why it matters in delivery**

The retrospective is the last impression the client has of the engagement. Recency effects dominate in human memory: how the project ended will shape how it is remembered far more than how it ran day to day. A retrospective that focuses on bugs, friction, and what went wrong will be remembered as bug-heavy, even if the project was successful overall. A retrospective that focuses on outcomes, learning, and impact will be remembered as valuable, even if there were many issues along the way.

This is not about hiding problems. Honest retrospectives surface problems. But the structure of the conversation determines whether problems are framed as failures (which damage memory of the project) or as learnings (which become part of the project's value). Most teams default to the failure framing because it feels more humble. The cost is a client who is less likely to engage the team again, less likely to advocate for the team internally, less likely to remember the project's actual outcomes.

**How it works**

A well-structured retrospective has five sections, in this specific order. The order matters.

| Section | Duration | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Outcomes | 15 minutes | Establish what the project achieved, in business terms |
| Learning | 15 minutes | Capture what both sides learned that will inform future work |
| What went well | 10 minutes | Recognise the practices and decisions that produced the outcomes |
| What was hard | 10 minutes | Surface the challenges and how they were navigated |
| Looking forward | 10 minutes | Discuss the natural next steps, including possible follow-on work |

The order is deliberate. Starting with outcomes anchors the conversation in value. Moving to learning makes the conversation forward-looking. Recognising what went well before discussing what was hard frames the challenges as part of a successful journey, not as the journey itself. Looking forward leaves the conversation pointed at the next engagement, not at the rear view mirror.

The wrong order, common in poorly-run retrospectives, is: what went wrong, what went well, what we learned, what is next. This order anchors the conversation in problems, which colours everything that follows.

**The outcomes section**

The outcomes section opens the retrospective by establishing what the project achieved. The framing matters. The wrong framing is "the system is now live and producing X outputs per day". The right framing is the business outcome that the system enables, in terms the operating sponsor cares about.

For a customer service automation project, the wrong framing: "the system handles 60 percent of incoming queries automatically". The right framing: "the customer service team has been able to redirect 40 hours per week of routine work toward complex customer issues, while customer satisfaction scores have remained stable".

The right framing comes from the operating sponsor, not the delivery team. A useful pattern: ask the operating sponsor to open this section by describing the outcomes in their own words. The team adds specifics where helpful but lets the sponsor own the framing. This anchors the rest of the conversation in the sponsor's language and values.

**The learning section**

The learning section captures what both sides have learned. The framing is symmetrical: the team learned things, and so did the client. Each side names three to five learnings.

For the team, typical learnings: how this client's context differed from previous engagements, what worked technically that might apply to other clients, what was harder than expected, what the team would do differently next time.

For the client, typical learnings: what AI delivery actually requires of an organisation, what kinds of issues are normal versus exceptional, what infrastructure or capability gaps the client should address before the next AI project, what AI is and is not good at in their context.

The client's learnings often surprise the team. They reveal what the project was actually like from the client's side, which is usually different from what the team perceived. Capturing these honestly is one of the most valuable parts of the retrospective.

**The what went well section**

This section recognises the practices and decisions that produced the outcomes. The temptation is to skip this or rush through it because it can feel self-congratulatory. Resist the temptation. Naming what went well is what teaches the organisation what to repeat. Without this naming, successful practices are lost to memory.

Good prompts for this section: which specific decision do you think most affected the outcome? Which working practice would you want to keep in your next project? Which moment in the project do you remember as a turning point?

The answers should be specific. Not "the team was great" but "the decision in week 4 to descope the international customer segment and focus on domestic". Not "communication was good" but "the weekly written summary that the operating sponsor could forward to the executive committee without rewriting".

**The what was hard section**

This section names the challenges. The framing matters. The wrong framing is "what went wrong". The right framing is "what was hard, and how did we navigate it". The difference is whether the focus is on the problems or on the response to the problems.

The team's role in this section is to help the client articulate the challenges from their side, not to lead with the team's own complaints. The client's articulation of what was hard is more valuable to the team than the team's own articulation.

Common categories of hardness in AI projects: the data was harder than expected, the eval criteria took longer to agree than expected, the integration was more complex than initial scoping, the operating team had less capacity to engage than planned, the regulatory framework was less settled than assumed.

Each hardness is named, the response is described, and the implication for future work is captured. The result is a balanced picture of a project that achieved its outcomes through good work despite real challenges.

**The looking forward section**

The final section points at what is next. The right framing is not "are you happy with us" but "what are the natural next steps for this work, regardless of who does them". This positions the team as a thoughtful partner who is thinking about the client's roadmap, not as a vendor pitching for the next contract.

Most retrospectives end here with a natural conversation about possible follow-on work. The conversation does not need to be a sales pitch. The team has done the work, the outcomes are established, the relationship is in good shape. If follow-on work is appropriate, it usually comes up naturally.

The retrospective output is a one to two page memo that captures the key points from each section. This memo is shared with both sides and becomes part of the project's institutional record. The memo is more useful than a deck because it is read, not skimmed.

**PM decision points**

- Have I structured the retrospective with outcomes first, or am I defaulting to "what went well, what went badly"?
- Am I asking the operating sponsor to open with their framing of outcomes, or am I leading?
- Am I producing a memo that captures the conversation, or letting the retrospective be ephemeral?

**Watch outs**

- The retrospective should be honest. Honest does not mean negative. It means accurate. Frame challenges as challenges, not as failures.
- Avoid making the retrospective a celebration. A celebration tone undermines the honest learning. A balanced tone serves both sides better.
- The team's contribution to the retrospective should be light. The client's voice should dominate. The team has been talking for the whole project. The retrospective is the moment to listen.

**Takeaway: the project retrospective is the last impression the client has of the engagement. Structure it with outcomes first, then learning, then what went well, then what was hard, then looking forward. Let the operating sponsor anchor the outcomes framing. Capture the conversation in a memo. The structure is what makes the client remember the project's value rather than its bug list.**

**Qantas story**

Imagine reaching the retrospective for a Qantas AI project, an internal tool for the cargo customer service team. The project ran 16 weeks, launched 6 weeks ago, has been running smoothly with the operating team now self-sufficient. The retrospective is scheduled for 90 minutes, with the PM, the engagement partner, the operating sponsor (cargo customer service manager), the executive sponsor (head of cargo operations), and one senior agent representing the user community.

The team is tempted to structure the retrospective around the project's challenges. There were three significant ones: the data access took 7 weeks instead of the planned 4 (mentioned in 14.3), the launch was delayed by one week (mentioned in 17.1), and the first week of production surfaced three issues that needed fixing (mentioned in 17.4). A bug-focused retrospective would walk through each of these.

The PM resists this structure and instead leads with the five section format.

The outcomes section opens with the operating sponsor describing what the system has achieved. Her framing: "the cargo customer service team has been able to respond to enquiries an average of 35 percent faster, the team has shifted from reactive to proactive on complex enquiries because they have time, and we have started taking enquiries from market segments we previously had to decline because we did not have capacity". The executive sponsor adds: "the team's morale has noticeably improved because the routine work is faster, which lets them focus on the work they find meaningful". These outcomes are specific, business-relevant, and owned by the client.

The learning section captures three learnings from each side. The team learned: that cargo customer service has unique terminology that needed dedicated prompt engineering, that the operating team's involvement in eval design was more valuable than the team had expected, and that the runbook investment paid back within the first month. The client learned: that AI delivery requires more upfront data preparation than they had expected, that running an AI project alongside the line work requires dedicated team capacity that they should plan for in future projects, and that the value of the system was less about automation and more about giving the team more time for judgment work.

The what went well section names specific practices. The operating sponsor names two: "your weekly written summary made it easy for me to keep the executive committee informed without rewriting" and "the change request memos kept us honest about scope without slowing us down". The senior agent names: "the workshops where you brought us in early meant the system actually fits how we work, not how someone from outside thought we worked". The team adds: "the partnership on the runbook in the final weeks was probably the single most valuable investment of the project".

The what was hard section names challenges honestly without dwelling. The data access timeline was hard, and the navigation involved escalating to the right executive and adjusting the build schedule. The launch delay was hard, and the navigation involved being honest about the readiness gaps. The first week issues were hard, and the navigation involved daily ward rounds. Each hardness is followed by the response, which is what made the project successful.

The looking forward section opens naturally. The operating sponsor describes the next obvious step: extending the system to handle international cargo enquiries, which was descoped from v1. The executive sponsor mentions that other Qantas teams have asked about the project and may be interested in similar tools. The conversation about possible follow-on work happens without anyone needing to pitch.

The retrospective ends at 75 minutes. The PM produces a two page memo within 48 hours, capturing each section. The memo is shared with both sides. The operating sponsor forwards it to her own team and to the executive committee. The executive sponsor references it in his quarterly business review.

The interesting comparison is with a different Qantas AI project where the retrospective was structured as "what went well, what went badly, what we learned, what is next". That retrospective spent the first 30 minutes discussing the project's challenges, which set the tone for the entire conversation. The outcomes were mentioned but did not anchor the discussion. The learnings were less rich because the conversation had become defensive. The follow-on conversation was strained because the executive sponsor left the meeting thinking primarily about what had gone wrong. No follow-on work materialised from that project, despite the actual outcomes being similar to the cargo project.

The lesson for AI delivery is that the retrospective is the moment where the client's memory of the project is consolidated. Structure determines what is remembered. Leading with outcomes anchors memory in value. Treating challenges as things that were navigated rather than as failures preserves the project's success in memory. Producing a memo locks the framing in writing. The hour and a half of the retrospective, run well, is one of the highest leverage hours of the entire engagement. The hour and a half, run poorly, can undo months of good work in how the project is remembered.

**定义**

项目复盘会是交付团队和客户之间最后一次结构化对话, 典型在上线后三到六周, 系统稳定后举行。复盘会同时服务三个目的: 给双方文档化学到的东西、为组织记忆捕捉项目结果、为下次合作定基调。第三个目的最被低估。开得差的复盘会让客户记住项目是一系列最终被解决的问题。开得好的复盘会让客户记住项目是团队和客户一起取得的有意义结果。框架很重要, 而框架主要由复盘会怎么结构化决定。

**为何在交付中重要**

复盘会是客户对合作的最后印象。近因效应在人类记忆里占主导: 项目怎么结束塑造它怎么被记住的程度, 远超过它日复一日怎么跑。聚焦 bug、摩擦、什么出错的复盘会, 会被记成 bug 多, 哪怕项目整体成功。聚焦结果、学习、影响的复盘会, 会被记成有价值, 哪怕沿路有很多 issue。

这不是关于隐藏问题。诚实的复盘会浮出问题。但对话的结构决定问题被框架成失败 (损害项目记忆) 还是学习 (变成项目价值的一部分)。大多数团队默认失败框架, 因为感觉更谦虚。代价是更不可能再 engage 团队、更不可能在内部为团队倡导、更不可能记得项目实际结果的客户。

**运作原理**

一份结构良好的复盘会有五节, 按这个具体顺序。顺序重要。

| 节 | 时长 | 目的 |
|---|---|---|
| 结果 | 15 分钟 | 建立项目用业务语言达成了什么 |
| 学习 | 15 分钟 | 捕捉双方学到的会告知未来工作的东西 |
| 跑得好的 | 10 分钟 | 认可产出结果的实践和决定 |
| 难的 | 10 分钟 | 浮出挑战以及怎么导航 |
| 向前看 | 10 分钟 | 讨论自然的下一步, 包括可能的后续工作 |

顺序是刻意的。从结果开始把对话锚在价值上。移到学习让对话向前看。在讨论难的之前认可跑得好的, 把挑战框架成成功旅程的一部分, 不是旅程本身。向前看让对话指向下次合作, 不是后视镜。

错的顺序, 在跑得差的复盘会里常见, 是: 什么出错、什么跑得好、我们学到什么、下一步什么。这个顺序把对话锚在问题上, 给后面一切染色。

**结果节**

结果节开场, 建立项目达成了什么。框架重要。错的框架是"系统现在上线了, 每天产出 X 输出"。对的框架是系统使能的业务结果, 用运营 sponsor 在意的术语。

对一个客服自动化项目, 错的框架: "系统自动处理 60% 进来的查询"。对的框架: "客服团队每周能把 40 小时常规工作重定向到复杂客户 issue, 同时客户满意度评分保持稳定"。

对的框架来自运营 sponsor, 不是交付团队。一个有用的 pattern: 请运营 sponsor 用自己的话开这一节、描述结果。团队在有帮助时加具体, 但让 sponsor 拥有框架。这把对话其余部分锚在 sponsor 的语言和价值上。

**学习节**

学习节捕捉双方学到了什么。框架对称: 团队学到了, 客户也学到了。每边命名三到五个学习。

对团队, 典型学习: 这个客户的上下文跟之前合作怎么不同、什么技术上奏效可能适用于其他客户、什么比预期难、团队下次会不同地做什么。

对客户, 典型学习: AI 交付实际要求组织什么、什么 issue 算正常、什么算例外、客户在下个 AI 项目之前应该处理什么基础设施或能力缺口、AI 在他们的上下文里擅长和不擅长什么。

客户的学习经常让团队意外。它们揭示项目从客户那边实际是什么样的, 通常跟团队感知的不同。诚实捕捉这些是复盘会最有价值的部分之一。

**跑得好节**

这一节认可产出结果的实践和决定。诱惑是跳过或匆忙过, 因为它感觉自我恭维。抵抗诱惑。命名跑得好的, 是教组织重复什么的方法。没有这种命名, 成功实践被记忆遗忘。

这一节的好提问: 哪个具体决定你认为最影响结果? 哪个工作实践你想在下个项目保留? 项目里哪个时刻你记得是转折点?

答案应该具体。不是"团队很棒"而是"第 4 周降范围、聚焦国内的决定"。不是"沟通好"而是"运营 sponsor 不用重写就能转发给执委会的每周书面总结"。

**难的节**

这一节命名挑战。框架重要。错的框架是"什么出错"。对的框架是"什么难、我们怎么导航"。区别在于聚焦在问题上还是聚焦在对问题的响应上。

团队在这一节的角色是帮客户从他们那边表达挑战, 不是先提团队自己的抱怨。客户对什么难的表达, 对团队比团队自己的表达更有价值。

AI 项目难的常见类别: 数据比预期难、商定 eval 标准比预期久、集成比初始 scoping 复杂、运营团队 engage 的容量比计划少、监管框架比假设的不稳定。

每个难处被命名, 响应被描述, 对未来工作的含义被捕捉。结果是一幅平衡的图: 一个通过好工作、尽管有真实挑战、达成结果的项目。

**向前看节**

最后一节指向下一步。对的框架不是"你对我们满意吗"而是"这项工作自然的下一步是什么, 不管谁做"。这把团队定位成思考客户路线图的深思熟虑的伙伴, 不是为下个合同推销的供应商。

大多数复盘会自然地结束于关于可能后续工作的对话。对话不需要是销售推销。团队做了工作、结果建立、关系状态好。如果后续工作合适, 通常自然冒出来。

复盘会产出是一到两页 memo, 捕捉每节的关键点。这份 memo 跟双方共享, 变成项目机构记录的一部分。Memo 比 deck 有用, 因为它被读, 不是被扫。

**PM 决策点**

- 我有没有用结果优先的结构, 还是默认"什么跑得好、什么跑得差"?
- 我有没有请运营 sponsor 用他们的结果框架开场, 还是我在主导?
- 我有没有产出捕捉对话的 memo, 还是让复盘会蜉蝣?

**注意事项**

- 复盘会应该诚实。诚实不意味着负面。它意味着准确。把挑战框架成挑战, 不是失败。
- 避免让复盘会变成庆祝会。庆祝语气损害诚实学习。平衡语气对双方更好。
- 团队对复盘会的贡献应该轻。客户的声音应该主导。团队在整个项目里一直在说话。复盘会是听的时刻。

**要点总结: 项目复盘会是客户对合作的最后印象。用"结果优先, 然后学习, 然后跑得好, 然后难的, 然后向前看"结构化。让运营 sponsor 锚结果框架。用 memo 捕捉对话。结构是让客户记住项目价值而不是 bug 列表的东西。**

**Qantas 案例**

设想你到了一个 Qantas AI 项目的复盘会, 一个给货运客服团队的内部工具。项目跑了 16 周, 6 周前上线, 一直跑得顺, 运营团队现在自给自足。复盘会安排 90 分钟, PM、engagement partner、运营 sponsor (货运客服经理)、executive sponsor (货运运营负责人)、一位代表用户社区的资深坐席参加。

团队被诱惑围绕项目挑战结构化复盘会。有三个显著的挑战: 数据访问花了 7 周而不是计划的 4 周 (14.3 提过), 上线晚一周 (17.1 提过), 生产第一周浮出三个需要修的 issue (17.4 提过)。Bug 聚焦的复盘会会走过每一个。

PM 抵抗这个结构, 改用五节格式。

结果节开场, 运营 sponsor 描述系统达成了什么。她的框架: "货运客服团队对咨询的响应平均快了 35%, 团队从被动转向主动应对复杂咨询, 因为他们有时间, 我们开始接来自之前因为没容量不得不拒绝的市场段的咨询"。Executive sponsor 加: "团队士气明显改善, 因为常规工作更快, 让他们聚焦在他们觉得有意义的工作上"。这些结果具体、跟业务相关、由客户拥有。

学习节每边捕捉三个学习。团队学到: 货运客服有需要专门 prompt 工程的独特术语、运营团队对 eval 设计的参与比团队预期更有价值、runbook 投资在第一个月内就回本。客户学到: AI 交付需要比他们预期更多的前期数据准备、跟线上工作并行跑 AI 项目需要专门的团队容量, 他们未来项目应该规划、系统的价值更少是关于自动化、更多是给团队更多判断工作的时间。

跑得好节命名具体实践。运营 sponsor 命名两个: "你的每周书面总结让我不用重写就容易把执委会知会" 和 "change request memo 让我们对范围诚实, 没有慢化我们"。资深坐席命名: "你早期把我们带进来的 workshop 让系统实际符合我们怎么工作, 不是外面的人以为我们怎么工作"。团队加: "最后几周在 runbook 上的伙伴关系, 可能是项目最有价值的单一投资"。

难的节诚实命名挑战, 不沉湎。数据访问时间线难, 导航涉及升级到对的高管和调整建造日程。上线延迟难, 导航涉及对 readiness 缺口诚实。第一周 issue 难, 导航涉及每日巡房。每个难处后面跟响应, 那才是让项目成功的东西。

向前看节自然打开。运营 sponsor 描述明显的下一步: 扩展系统处理国际货运咨询, v1 里被降的。Executive sponsor 提到 Qantas 其他团队问过项目, 可能对类似工具感兴趣。关于可能后续工作的对话不需要任何人推销就发生。

复盘会在 75 分钟结束。PM 在 48 小时内产出两页 memo, 捕捉每一节。Memo 跟双方共享。运营 sponsor 转发给自己的团队和执委会。Executive sponsor 在他的季度业务 review 里引用。

有意思的对照是另一个 Qantas AI 项目, 复盘会按"跑得好、跑得差、学到的、下一步"结构化。那个复盘会前 30 分钟讨论项目的挑战, 给整个对话定调。结果被提到, 但没锚讨论。学习更不丰富, 因为对话变得防御。后续对话紧张, 因为 executive sponsor 离开会议时主要在想什么出错了。没有从那个项目产生后续工作, 尽管实际结果跟货运项目相似。

AI 交付的启示是, 复盘会是客户对项目的记忆被合并的时刻。结构决定什么被记住。从结果开始把记忆锚在价值上。把挑战当作被导航的事而不是失败, 在记忆里保留项目的成功。产出 memo 把框架锁定在文字里。复盘会的一个半小时, 跑得好, 是整个合作里杠杆最高的几小时之一。一个半小时, 跑得差, 能在"项目怎么被记住"上撤销几个月的好工作。
