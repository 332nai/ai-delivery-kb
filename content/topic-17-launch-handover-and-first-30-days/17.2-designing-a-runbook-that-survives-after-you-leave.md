**Definition**

A runbook is the document that allows the client's operating team to diagnose and respond to common problems with the AI system after the delivery team has left. It is distinct from technical documentation (which describes how the system is built) and from user documentation (which describes how to use the system). The runbook is the operational playbook: what to do when the system misbehaves, who to call, what to check, what to try, when to escalate. A good runbook is the difference between a system that survives handover and a system that collapses the first time something goes wrong.

**Why it matters in delivery**

The most common post-handover failure is not the AI system itself, it is the operating team's inability to respond when something unexpected happens. The system was built and tested. The team was trained. But the first time the model starts producing strange outputs at 11pm on a Friday, the operating team does not know what to check, who to call, or whether to roll back. Without a runbook, they call the delivery team, which is no longer engaged, or they let the issue continue until business hours, by which point the impact has spread.

The runbook fixes this by giving the operating team a structured starting point. It does not need to anticipate every possible issue. It needs to anticipate the most common ones, document the diagnostic steps, and provide a clear escalation path for everything else. Done well, it produces operating teams that are confident handling issues themselves, which is what handover is supposed to achieve.

**How it works**

A useful runbook has four sections. Anything more is over-engineered, anything less misses critical content.

| Section | What it contains |
|---|---|
| System overview | One page summary of what the system does, the major components, the data it uses, the model it calls, the external dependencies |
| Common issues and responses | 10 to 20 issues that the team and the operating team have anticipated, each with symptoms, diagnostic steps, fixes, and escalation criteria |
| Escalation contacts and process | Who to call for what kind of issue, with phone numbers, working hours, and response time expectations |
| Reference and links | Where to find logs, monitoring dashboards, source code, vendor support portals, related runbooks |

The most important section is "common issues". Each issue is a structured entry with five fields:

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Symptom | How the operating team will notice the issue (user complaint, alert, dashboard metric) |
| Likely causes | Two to four candidate causes, ordered by frequency |
| Diagnostic steps | What to check, in what order, with specific commands or links |
| Fixes to try | What to do once the cause is identified |
| When to escalate | Specific conditions that require calling the delivery team or vendor |

A worked example: an entry for "model returning empty responses".

Symptom: users report the assistant is returning blank or extremely short responses. Dashboard shows refusal rate spiking.

Likely causes: (1) content filter triggered by unusual user input, (2) model provider experiencing degraded service, (3) prompt was changed and now triggers refusal patterns, (4) rate limiting on the model API.

Diagnostic steps: (1) check the monitoring dashboard for the refusal rate spike, note the start time; (2) check the model provider's status page; (3) check the prompt version deployed in production, compare to last known good version; (4) check the rate limit metrics on the model API.

Fixes to try: if cause 1, capture the specific user inputs and disable that input class temporarily; if cause 2, the issue will likely resolve as the provider recovers, monitor; if cause 3, roll back to the previous prompt version; if cause 4, increase the rate limit or implement queueing.

When to escalate: if the refusal rate stays above 10 percent for more than 30 minutes after attempting fixes, escalate to the delivery team. If the issue began after a deployment, roll back first then escalate.

This level of specificity is what makes the runbook useful. Vague advice ("check the logs") is not useful. Specific advice with named dashboards, commands, and thresholds is.

**Writing the runbook**

The runbook is written by the delivery team and the operating team together, not by the delivery team alone. The reason: a runbook written by the delivery team alone reflects what the delivery team thinks the operating team will encounter, which is often different from what the operating team actually encounters. Joint writing forces the team to think from the operating perspective.

A useful method: hold three or four runbook workshops in the last two weeks before launch. Each workshop focuses on a category of issues: model behaviour, infrastructure, integration, user experience. In each workshop, the operating team describes what they would expect to do if a certain problem occurred, and the delivery team fills in the technical specifics. The output is a draft entry for each issue. The drafts are refined between workshops.

The runbook should be tested. Run one or two tabletop exercises before launch where the team simulates an issue and the operating team uses the runbook to respond. The exercise reveals gaps: entries that are not clear, escalation paths that do not work, references that are missing. Fix the gaps before launch.

**Maintaining the runbook**

The runbook is a living document. The first 30 days after launch will surface issues that the runbook does not cover. Each new issue should produce a new entry. The delivery team's job in the first 30 days is to support the operating team in adding these entries, not to handle the issues themselves. By the end of 30 days, the runbook should be comprehensive enough that the operating team is mostly self-sufficient.

A useful pattern: every incident, large or small, ends with the question "is this in the runbook, and if not, should it be?" Most incidents add an entry. Over time the runbook becomes the institutional memory of the system.

**PM decision points**

- Is the runbook a real working document that the operating team will use, or is it a deliverable nobody reads?
- Has the runbook been written jointly with the operating team, or by the delivery team alone?
- Has the runbook been tabletop-tested before launch?

**Watch outs**

- A runbook written entirely by engineers is often too technical and assumes too much. Operating teams need procedures, not architecture.
- The escalation section is critical and often weak. Without clear escalation, operating teams either escalate everything (overwhelming the delivery team) or nothing (letting issues fester).
- Avoid copying a generic runbook template. Specifics are the value. A 5 page custom runbook beats a 50 page generic one.

**Takeaway: a good runbook has four sections: system overview, common issues with structured entries, escalation contacts, references. Write it jointly with the operating team. Tabletop test it before launch. Update it during the first 30 days. The runbook is what makes handover real.**

**Qantas story**

Imagine approaching launch of a Qantas AI project, a tool for the airline operations team that helps them draft initial customer communications during disruptions. The system is technically ready. The team is keen to handover.

The PM raises the runbook conversation in week 12 of a 14 week project. The technical lead has prepared a 40 page document covering the architecture, the data flows, the model parameters, and the deployment scripts. It is comprehensive. It is also useless to the operating team.

The PM redirects. The 40 page document becomes a technical appendix. The actual runbook is rewritten as a 12 page document with the four standard sections. The "common issues" section is built through three workshops with the airline operations team.

The workshops surface issues the delivery team had not anticipated. The operations team is worried about what happens if a model output gets published to a customer in error, what happens if the system goes down during a major disruption when they need it most, what happens if the model starts producing outputs in a language the operations team does not speak. None of these had been in the original 40 page document. All become runbook entries.

The published runbook has 14 issue entries. Each has the five field structure. Each is reviewed by an operations team member before the runbook is signed off.

The tabletop test is held three days before launch. The PM describes a scenario: at 2am Sunday, the airline experiences a major weather disruption affecting 30 flights, the AI system begins producing customer communications that mention specific weather conditions that have not been externally verified by the meteorology bureau, and the customer experience team flags the issue. What does the operations team do?

The operations team uses the runbook. They find the entry for "system producing unverified claims", they follow the diagnostic steps, they identify the cause (the prompt does not require external verification for weather claims), they apply the documented fix (switch to the conservative prompt variant that uses only neutral language), they verify the fix works in monitoring, and they document the incident. The tabletop takes 22 minutes. The runbook works.

In production, the first 30 days surface seven new issues that the runbook did not cover. Each becomes a new entry. The PM stays lightly engaged during this period to help the operations team write the new entries to the same standard as the original ones. By day 30, the runbook has 21 entries and the operations team has handled five real incidents independently without calling the delivery team.

The interesting comparison is with a parallel Qantas AI project where the runbook was 40 pages of technical documentation. When the first production incident occurred, the operations team did not know which page to look at, called the delivery team, and the delivery team's engagement was already winding down. The incident took six hours to resolve. By the third incident, the operations team had stopped trying to use the runbook and was escalating everything. The system became fragile, the relationship with operations deteriorated, and the project was eventually classified as a partial success.

The lesson for AI delivery is that the runbook is one of the most important artifacts of the entire project, often underinvested in because it is unglamorous. A good runbook takes 10 to 20 hours to produce. It is the difference between a system that the operating team owns and a system that the operating team merely received. The investment is small, the leverage is enormous.

**定义**

Runbook 是让客户的运营团队在交付团队撤之后, 能诊断和响应 AI 系统常见问题的文档。它跟技术文档 (描述系统怎么建) 不同, 也跟用户文档 (描述怎么用系统) 不同。Runbook 是运营 playbook: 系统出毛病时做什么、找谁、查什么、试什么、何时升级。好的 runbook 是一个系统"挺过移交"还是"第一次出毛病就崩"的区别。

**为何在交付中重要**

最常见的移交后失败不是 AI 系统本身, 是运营团队没法响应意外。系统建好了, 测过了。团队培训过了。但模型周五晚上 11 点开始产出奇怪输出时, 运营团队不知道查什么、找谁、要不要回滚。没有 runbook, 他们要么打电话给已经不 engaged 的交付团队, 要么让 issue 持续到工作时间, 那时影响已经扩散。

Runbook 修复这点, 给运营团队一个结构化的起点。它不需要预测每个可能的 issue。它需要预测最常见的, 文档化诊断步骤, 对其他一切提供清晰的升级路径。做得好, 它产出"自信地自己处理 issue"的运营团队, 这就是移交本应实现的。

**运作原理**

一份有用的 runbook 有四节。多了就过度工程化, 少了漏关键内容。

| 节 | 包含什么 |
|---|---|
| 系统概览 | 一页总结: 系统做什么、主要组件、用什么数据、调什么模型、外部依赖 |
| 常见 issue 和响应 | 10 到 20 个团队和运营团队预期到的 issue, 每个带症状、诊断步骤、修复、升级标准 |
| 升级联系人和流程 | 什么 issue 找谁, 带电话、工作时间、响应时间预期 |
| 参考和链接 | 在哪找日志、监控 dashboard、源码、供应商支持门户、相关 runbook |

最重要的一节是"常见 issue"。每个 issue 是一个结构化条目, 五个字段:

| 字段 | 内容 |
|---|---|
| 症状 | 运营团队怎么注意到这个 issue (用户投诉、告警、dashboard 指标) |
| 可能原因 | 两到四个候选原因, 按频率排序 |
| 诊断步骤 | 查什么, 什么顺序, 带具体命令或链接 |
| 试什么修复 | 一旦原因识别, 做什么 |
| 何时升级 | 触发"打电话给交付团队或供应商"的具体条件 |

一个例子: "模型返回空回应" 这一条。

症状: 用户反馈助手返回空白或极短回应。Dashboard 显示拒绝率飙升。

可能原因: (1) 内容过滤被异常用户输入触发, (2) 模型厂商服务降级, (3) prompt 改过, 现在触发拒绝 pattern, (4) 模型 API 限流。

诊断步骤: (1) 看监控 dashboard 上的拒绝率飙升, 记下起始时间; (2) 看模型厂商的状态页; (3) 看生产里部署的 prompt 版本, 跟最后已知好版本对比; (4) 看模型 API 上的限流指标。

试什么修复: 如果是原因 1, 抓住具体用户输入, 临时禁用那类输入; 如果是原因 2, issue 会随厂商恢复而解决, 监控就好; 如果是原因 3, 回滚到上一个 prompt 版本; 如果是原因 4, 提高限流或实现队列。

何时升级: 尝试修复后, 拒绝率仍然在 10% 以上超过 30 分钟, 升级到交付团队。如果 issue 在一次部署后开始, 先回滚再升级。

这种程度的具体性是让 runbook 有用的原因。模糊建议 ("查日志") 没用。带 dashboard 名、命令、阈值的具体建议有用。

**写 runbook**

Runbook 是交付团队和运营团队一起写, 不是交付团队单独写。原因: 交付团队单独写的 runbook 反映交付团队认为运营团队会遇到什么, 经常跟运营团队实际遇到的不一样。联合写逼团队从运营视角思考。

一个有用的方法: 上线前最后两周开三到四次 runbook workshop。每个 workshop 聚焦一类 issue: 模型行为、基础设施、集成、用户体验。每个 workshop 上, 运营团队描述如果某个问题出现他们会预期怎么做, 交付团队填技术细节。产出是每个 issue 的草稿条目。草稿在 workshop 之间精炼。

Runbook 应该被测试。上线前跑一两次桌面演练, 团队模拟一个 issue, 运营团队用 runbook 响应。演练揭示缺口: 不清晰的条目、不工作的升级路径、缺失的引用。上线前修缺口。

**维护 runbook**

Runbook 是活文档。上线后前 30 天会浮出 runbook 没覆盖的 issue。每个新 issue 应该产出一个新条目。交付团队前 30 天的工作是支持运营团队添加这些条目, 不是自己处理 issue。30 天结束时, runbook 应该全面到运营团队大部分自给自足。

一个有用的 pattern: 每个事件, 不管大小, 结束时问 "这个在 runbook 里吗, 如果不在, 该不该在?" 大多数事件加一个条目。随时间, runbook 变成系统的机构记忆。

**PM 决策点**

- Runbook 是运营团队会用的真实工作文档, 还是没人读的交付物?
- Runbook 有没有跟运营团队联合写, 还是交付团队单独写?
- Runbook 在上线前桌面测试过吗?

**注意事项**

- 完全由工程师写的 runbook 经常太技术、假设太多。运营团队需要流程, 不是架构。
- 升级那节关键但经常薄弱。没有清晰升级, 运营团队要么升级一切 (压垮交付团队), 要么不升级 (让 issue 发酵)。
- 避免复制通用 runbook 模板。具体性才是价值。一份 5 页的定制 runbook 胜过 50 页通用的。

**要点总结: 好的 runbook 有四节: 系统概览、带结构化条目的常见 issue、升级联系人、参考。跟运营团队联合写。上线前桌面测试。前 30 天更新。Runbook 是让移交变真的东西。**

**Qantas 案例**

设想你接近一个 Qantas AI 项目的上线, 一个给航司运营团队的工具, 帮他们在中断期间起草初始客户沟通。系统技术上 ready。团队迫不及待要移交。

PM 在 14 周项目的第 12 周提出 runbook 对话。技术负责人准备了一份 40 页文档, 覆盖架构、数据流、模型参数、部署脚本。它全面。它对运营团队也没用。

PM 重定向。40 页文档变成技术附录。实际 runbook 重写成一份 12 页文档, 用四节标准结构。"常见 issue" 那节通过三次跟航司运营团队的 workshop 建造。

Workshop 浮出交付团队没预期到的 issue。运营团队担心: 一个模型输出被错误地发布给客户怎么办、系统在他们最需要的重大中断期间宕机怎么办、模型开始产出运营团队不懂的语言的输出怎么办。这些都不在原 40 页文档里。全部变成 runbook 条目。

发布的 runbook 有 14 个 issue 条目。每个有五字段结构。每个在 runbook 签字之前被运营团队成员 review。

桌面测试在上线前三天举行。PM 描述一个场景: 周日凌晨 2 点, 航司经历一次重大天气中断, 影响 30 个航班, AI 系统开始产出提到具体天气状况的客户沟通, 那些天气状况没被气象局外部验证过, 客户体验团队标记了这个 issue。运营团队做什么?

运营团队用 runbook。他们找到"系统产出未经验证的声明"条目, 跟随诊断步骤, 识别原因 (prompt 没要求天气声明的外部验证), 应用文档化的修复 (切换到只用中性语言的保守 prompt 变体), 在监控里验证修复奏效, 文档化事件。桌面演练用了 22 分钟。Runbook 工作。

生产里, 前 30 天浮出 7 个 runbook 没覆盖的新 issue。每个变成新条目。PM 在这期间保持轻度 engaged, 帮运营团队把新条目写到跟原始条目同样的标准。第 30 天, runbook 有 21 个条目, 运营团队独立处理过 5 个真实事件, 没打电话给交付团队。

有意思的对照是一个并行的 Qantas AI 项目, runbook 是 40 页技术文档。第一个生产事件发生时, 运营团队不知道看哪一页, 打电话给交付团队, 交付团队的 engagement 已经在收尾。事件花 6 小时解决。到第三个事件, 运营团队放弃用 runbook, 升级一切。系统变脆弱, 跟运营的关系恶化, 项目最终被分类为部分成功。

AI 交付的启示是, runbook 是整个项目最重要的产物之一, 经常因为不光鲜而投资不足。一份好的 runbook 花 10 到 20 小时产出。它是"运营团队拥有的系统"和"运营团队只是收到的系统"的区别。投资小, 杠杆大。
