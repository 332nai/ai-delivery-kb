# Taking over a project mid-flight: when the original team wasn't expecting you

You've been put on a project that's already running. But this isn't like T18.2 (new team day one) — you're not stepping into a vacant role. You're replacing someone.

The triggers are varied:

- The previous PM couldn't hold it (left, burnt out, was replaced)
- The client has lost confidence and the partner or your new manager parachutes you in to firefight
- The project didn't need a PM at your seniority, but something went wrong and the staffing was upgraded
- The previous PM is still there, but the partner has decided "this project needs another set of eyes"

Whichever it is, the shared feature is: **the existing team didn't ask for you, and most of them know your arrival means something isn't right**.

You're walking into three things at once:

1. You can't disparage the previous PM or even publicly comment (even if the project genuinely was their fault)
2. You can't make sudden big moves (trust hasn't been built, big moves look like a takeover)
3. You still have to produce a judgement faster than you would on a normal first week (because they sent you in for a reason)

These three are inherently in tension — you can't move fast and you have to move fast; you can't step on the previous PM and you have to fix the previous PM's mess. Below: how to handle.

---

## Decision 1 — Aligning with the person who put you in, before you start

The most overlooked step: **before you walk in, spend 30 minutes with whoever parachuted you in, and ask a few uncomfortable questions**.

**What to ask**

> "Does the client know you put me in? If they know, what was their reaction?"

This shapes how you behave in the first few days:
- Client knows and asked for it: client expects a lot of you, will engage, but the tolerance for error is low
- Client knows but didn't ask: client may be quietly resentful, feels you were imposed
- Client doesn't know: your first few days have to be partly spent on "letting the client know and accept this", not on fixing the project

> "Is the previous PM still on the team? What does the transition look like?"

- Still on the team: your position gets awkward — who calls the shots?
- Not on the team but still at the company: can you talk to them?
- Already left: you have to rebuild the project history with no handover

> "How fast do you want a read from me? Who are you going to share that read with?"

This is one most people don't ask, but it's crucial. If your read will end up in front of the partner who'll take it to the client, the wording of the read is very different from "internal only".

> "If I conclude the direction is wrong, how do you want me to handle that in front of the client?"

This forces your manager to commit. They might say "you frame it", "align with me first", or "don't say it directly to the client". Each answer matters for what you say later.

**The last question — most people don't dare ask it**

> "Honestly — what do you think the previous PM's main problem was? What were the one or two things they got wrong?"

Ask it straight. Your manager will probably soften the answer, but whatever version they give you is the "official diagnosis" you'll be working from. You need to know that diagnosis, because it's the baseline you'll be aligning against later.

---

## Decision 2 — Day one walking in: what to say and what not to say

At your first team standup you'll be introduced. The team likely already knows you're here to take over or reinforce. The air will be a little tense.

**Most common mistake: explaining why you're here**

Bad opening:

> "Hi everyone, I know this transition might come as a surprise, I'm not here to disrupt what you're doing, just to help unblock things …"

The problem: you're explaining something nobody has challenged yet — and proactively explaining like that makes the team think "ah, so they are here to disrupt".

**Right move: don't explain, cut to substance**

> "Hi, I'm [name]. Over the next week I want to do two things — one-on-ones with each of you, and catch up on the project. I'm not going to be asking 'why did you do it this way' style questions this week, I just want to catch up enough to talk to you."

That does several things: you didn't explain (your presence doesn't need defence), you gave a concrete week-one plan, and you **explicitly said you won't be challenging existing decisions** — which is exactly what the team needs to hear.

If someone asks "what are you here to do?", answer directly:

> "The company thinks the project needs another set of eyes — that's what I am. Short term I look, mid term we figure out how we split things."

"Another set of eyes" is a neutral phrase — doesn't disparage the previous PM, doesn't oversell yourself.

---

## Decision 3 — Handling the shadow of the previous PM

This is the hardest part of taking over mid-flight. Even if the previous PM did badly, you absolutely cannot say so in front of the team, the client, or in email. The reason isn't moral — it's that it makes the team feel unsafe: today you speak ill of the previous PM behind their back, tomorrow when I leave, what will you say about me.

**When the team starts complaining about the previous PM**

This happens 90% of the time. In one-on-ones, engineers or analysts will say "the previous person didn't get it" / "they never read our updates" / "the state of this project is on them".

Wrong responses:

- Agreeing — "yeah I heard the same" (you're done)
- Defending the previous PM — "you can't say that, they might have had reasons" (the team feels unheard)
- Changing the subject — "let's look at where we go next" (too obviously dodging)

Right response:

> "OK, noted, no comment. The question I want to ask is — is there anything on this team that everyone is tiptoeing around, that actually needs someone to call out squarely?"

That does three things: you don't comment on the previous PM (neutral), you reframe the conversation from "past person" to "current issues", and you give them a meaningful topic.

**When the client complains about the previous PM**

More dangerous. The sponsor or lead may tell you "the previous PM really didn't get it".

Wrong responses:

- Agreeing (you're catastrophically done — the client will remember what you said)
- Defending the previous PM hard (you look like you're covering for a failure, the client may conclude you're cut from the same cloth)

Right response:

> "When I came onto this project, I only saw the SOW and the last few status updates — I don't plan to second guess what happened before, I wasn't there. What I want to do over the next two weeks is get the current ground truth clear, and align with you on the way forward."

That does three things: you refuse to second guess (the high-status move), you shift focus from past to present, and you implicitly promise a forward-looking action.

**If the client keeps pushing — "but really, what do you think the previous PM did wrong?"**

Deflect harder:

> "I can't fairly judge someone I haven't worked with. What I can tell you is what I'm planning to do next — [one or two lines on your approach]."

Refuse to judge + give a replacement answer = standard deflect from people who do this well.

---

## Decision 4 — How to do "the diagnostic" in week one

The manager who parachuted you in expects a diagnosis faster than a normal new PM. But diagnosing wrong is worse than diagnosing slow. How do you balance?

**Week one is diagnostic, not decision**

Keep these two things clearly separate (in your own head, and in what you report to your manager):

- Diagnostic: describing what the problem is, how serious, why it happened
- Decision: what we should do, when, who does it

In week one you only diagnose, you don't decide. If your manager pushes "should we cut scope?", say:

> "I have a leaning, but I don't want to commit before I've looked at [the things I haven't looked at yet]. I can give you a recommendation with conviction by Monday."

"A recommendation with conviction" is the kind of language seniors respond to — you're not stalling, you're calibrating the quality of your judgement.

**Four lenses for diagnostic**

Run through these four every time you take over a project in week one:

| Lens | What to look at | What to ask |
|---|---|---|
| **Scope** | SOW vs what's actually being built vs what the client expects | Are these three the same? Where's the gap? |
| **Quality** | Actual deliverable quality — look at the demo, the code, the deck | Would this hold up in a senior review? |
| **People** | Who's pulling the weight, who's coasting, who might leave | Is anyone critical about to walk? |
| **Trust** | The client's confidence in the project right now (ask the sponsor directly) | When did the client last proactively contact the PM? |

The fourth lens — Trust — is most often missed. Client sentiment is its own dimension. A project can have correct scope, correct quality, correct people — but the client's heart has gone cold, and at that point fixing the project and fixing the relationship are two different things, the second one being harder.

How do you measure client trust? The simplest signal: when was the last time the client proactively pinged the PM? If it was two weeks ago, the client has effectively given up on the project.

**Format for the diagnostic output**

End of week one, give your manager a one-page diagnostic — **not in front of the client**:

> **Week 1 Diagnostic**
>
> **Scope:**
> - [One line — biggest gap between SOW / actual / client expectation]
>
> **Quality:**
> - [One line — would current deliverables hold up in a senior or client review]
>
> **People:**
> - [One line — team state, especially any critical-person risk]
>
> **Trust:**
> - [One line — client sentiment right now]
>
> **My recommended next move:**
> - [One or two concrete actions — not sweeping changes]
> - [Timeline]
>
> **What I want you to weigh in on:**
> - [One or two things I'm not sure about]

The last two sections are the point. The manager isn't really reading the diagnostic — the manager is reading whether you **proactively frame the next move and proactively ask for input**.

---

## Decision 5 — Small moves in week two

You've diagnosed. You're probably seeing five to ten things that need fixing. But you can't roll out five to ten changes in week two — the team will push back, the client will get alarmed.

**Week two: only one or two visible small fixes**

Criteria for picking:

- The team will immediately feel "life is a bit easier"
- It doesn't need any client approval
- The effect is demonstrable within a week

Typical candidates:

- Cancel one useless recurring meeting
- Simplify one status-update process
- Rewrite a backlog so priorities are clear
- Clear out a client question that's been sitting in the inbox for two weeks

Don't pick:

- Architecture changes
- Scope changes (that's week three or later)
- Team responsibility changes (that needs earlier alignment)

**When you make this small move, let the team see it's for them**

Don't talk it up to your manager. In one-on-ones, mention it lightly:

> "I looked at it last week and figured [that weekly meeting] wasn't earning its time. I checked with [client side] and we cancelled it. If you find later we actually do need it, we'll add it back."

That last bit — "we'll add it back" — is important. You're not here to overhaul. You're here to adjust.

---

## Decision 6 — Restarting client trust

If the client has already gone cold on the project, fixing the project itself isn't enough. You have to put dedicated effort into fixing how the client feels.

**Move 1: directly acknowledge the awkwardness**

Get the sponsor alone, and **proactively** open the conversation:

> "Want to share my read on this project — honestly, looking through it, I think there are a few places over the past few months where we haven't delivered to your expectation. I can't undo what's happened, but I'd like you to tell me directly what you're most frustrated by, and we can work out how to adjust."

That does several things:

- You bring it up first (instead of letting the client bring it up) — proactive acknowledgement makes the client instantly less defensive
- You say "I can't undo it" — honest, and frames you as forward-looking
- You invite the client to vent their frustration — they feel heard

**Move 2: engineer a small visible win in week 2-3**

Client trust isn't restored by a conversation, they need to see evidence. Within 2-3 weeks of taking over, **deliberately engineer one visible small win** — a deliverable delivered a day early, a stuck issue resolved, a piece of analysis the sponsor has been waiting on.

Don't wait for it to "happen naturally". Plan it and make sure it happens.

**Move 3: rewrite the first status update**

The first status update you send the client after taking over — **completely rewrite the format**, don't use the previous PM's template. The reason isn't that the format matters; it's that it's a clear signal: things are different now.

You don't need to say "I rewrote the format" in the status update. The client will feel it when they see it.

---

## Quick do / don't

**Do**

- Before joining, spend 30 minutes with your manager on "why me", "does the client know", "what happened to the previous PM"
- Don't explain why you're there on day one
- Week one is diagnostic, not decision
- Scan with the four lenses: Scope / Quality / People / Trust
- End of week one: one-page diagnostic to your manager + ask them to weigh in on 1-2 items
- Week two: 1-2 small, visible fixes that don't need client approval
- Proactively acknowledge past gaps with the client — without second-guessing the previous PM

**Don't**

- Don't try to explain why you're here
- Don't agree with team or client complaints about the previous PM (even if you internally agree)
- Don't make big moves or challenge existing decisions in week one
- Don't second-guess specific decisions of the previous PM in front of the client
- Don't tell the client "the old way was wrong" before your manager has signed off on that framing
- Don't wait for things to "happen naturally" — engineer a small win for the client

---

---

Zooming out — the most counter-intuitive thing about taking over a project is: **you were sent in because things are urgent, but the more urgent you act, the more likely you fail**.

The urgency is what your manager feels, what the client feels, maybe what you've internalised. But teams don't like urgent PMs. Clients don't like vendors who look panicky. Internally you can feel urgent. **Externally you must look calm.**

PMs who successfully take over share one trait: they walk in and immediately everyone gets the feeling "this person knows what they're doing", then quietly, in small unobtrusive moves, fix a few things over the next two or three weeks, and then somewhere around week 4-6 the team realises "actually the project isn't that bad anymore".

There's no hero moment. No dramatic turnaround. Just steady, calm, deliberate execution. That's one of the highest forms this work can take.

# 中途接手项目:原 team 不期待你

你被派进一个已经在跑的项目。但跟 T18.2 (新团队第一天) 不一样 — 这次你不是替补一个 vacant 的位置,你是替换某个人。

可能的触发因素有几种:

- 前一个 PM 撑不住了 (离职、burn out、被换掉)
- 客户对项目失去信心,合伙人或你的新老板把你 parachute 进去救火
- 项目本来不需要你这个 level,但出了什么事,临时升级人手
- 别的 PM 还在,但合伙人觉得"应该再多一双眼睛"

不管哪一种,这个场景共同的特点是:**原来的 team 没主动要你来,而且他们多半知道你来意味着事情已经不正常了**。

你进去面对的有三件事同时压在头上:

1. 你不能贬低或公开评论前任 (哪怕项目确实是前任搞砸的)
2. 你不能立刻搞大动作 (信任还没建立,大动作会让 team 觉得你来是颠覆的)
3. 你还必须在比第一周更短的时间内拿出判断 (因为派你来就是因为要快)

这三件事本来就矛盾 — 既不能动手太快又要快出判断,既不能踩前任又要 fix 前任的烂摊子。下面拆开讲怎么处理。

---

## 决策点 1 — 进项目之前的对齐 (跟你 manager / 派你来的人)

最容易被忽略的一步: **进项目之前,先跟把你 parachute 进来的人花 30 分钟,把几个不舒服的问题问清楚**。

**该问的问题**

> "你把我派进来这件事,客户那边知道吗?知道的话他们 reaction 是什么?"

这个问题的答案极大影响你前几天怎么表现:
- 客户知道而且要求的: 你进去时客户对你期望很高,会主动 engage,但容错率低
- 客户知道但不是他们要求的: 客户可能会有点不爽,觉得你是被强加的
- 客户不知道: 你前几天要花精力在"让客户知道并接受你"这件事上,而不是 fix project

> "前一个 PM 现在还在 team 吗?他的 transition 怎么办?"

- 还在 team: 你的位置变得很尴尬,谁说了算?
- 不在 team 但还在公司: 你能找他聊?
- 已经离开: 你要在没有 handover 的情况下重建项目历史

> "你希望我多久之内给你一个 read?这个 read 你会拿去跟谁讲?"

这一题很多人不问,但极其值钱。如果你的 read 会被合伙人拿到客户面前讲,你 read 的措辞跟"只在内部用"完全不一样。

> "如果我看下来觉得项目方向有大问题,你期待我在客户面前怎么 handle?"

这个问题逼你 manager 提前表态。他可能说"你 frame 一下"、"你跟我先 align 再说"、"你不要直接讲给客户"。每种答案对你后面的措辞都至关重要。

**该问的最后一个问题 (大部分人不敢问)**

> "Honestly,你觉得前一个 PM 主要的问题是什么?他做错的是哪一两件事?"

直接问。你 manager 多半会避重就轻,但他给的那个版本就是你 manager 的"官方诊断"。你心里要知道这个官方诊断,因为这是你后面 align 的 baseline。

---

## 决策点 2 — 第一天进去,讲什么不讲什么

第一天 team standup 上,你已经被引入。Team 多半已经知道你是来接手或者来 reinforce 的。空气会有点尴尬。

**最容易犯的错: 试图解释自己为什么来**

错的开场:

> "嗨大家,我知道这个 transition 大家心里可能有点 surprise,我来这里不是为了 disrupt 你们已经在做的事,只是希望帮 unblock 一些 …"

错在哪: 你在解释一个还没被质疑的事 — 这种主动解释,反而让 team 想"嗯所以是来 disrupt 的"。

**对的开场: 不解释,直接切到 substance**

> "嗨,我是 [name]。我接下来这一周想做两件事 — 跟你们每个人 1-on-1,以及 catch up 项目的进展。这周我不会问'为什么这样做'类型的问题,我只想 catch up 到能跟你们对话的程度。"

这段话做了几件事: 你没解释 (你的存在不需要解释),你给了一个 concrete 的第一周 plan,你**明确说不会 challenge 已有的决定** — 这是 team 最需要听到的一句话。

如果有人问"你来是干嘛的?",直接答:

> "公司这边觉得这个项目应该多一双眼睛 — 我就是那双眼睛。短期我先看,中期我们再看怎么分工。"

"多一双眼睛"这个说法是中性的,没贬低前任,也没夸大自己。

---

## 决策点 3 — 怎么处理前任的影子

这是中途接手最难的事。哪怕前任做得不好,你在 team 面前 / 客户面前 / 邮件里,**绝对不能说前任的坏话**。理由不是道德层面 — 理由是这会让 team 感到不安全:今天你在背后说前任,明天我离开,你会怎么说我。

**Team 主动吐槽前任怎么办**

90% 的情况会发生。1-on-1 里 engineer 或 analyst 会说"前任那个人根本不懂"、"他从来不读我们的 update"、"项目搞成这样就是他的问题"。

错的回应:

- 附和 "对啊,我也听说了" (你完了)
- 替前任辩护 "不能这么说,他可能有难处" (team 觉得你不听他们的)
- 转移话题 "我们看看现在怎么往前走" (太显眼地在躲)

对的回应:

> "嗯,这个我知道了,不评价。我想问的是 — 你觉得现在 team 里有什么东西是大家都在小心翼翼绕过去的、其实需要有人 squarely 说出来?"

这个回应做了三件事: 你不评价前任 (中性),你 reframe 了对话从"过去的人"到"现在的事",你给了对方一个有意义的话题。

**客户主动吐槽前任怎么办**

更危险。客户那边的 sponsor 或 lead 可能会跟你说"前一个 PM 真的不行"。

错的回应:

- 附和 (你彻底完蛋,因为客户会记住你说过)
- 替前任硬辩护 (你看起来在掩护一个失败,客户会觉得你也是)

对的回应:

> "我接到这个项目的时候,只看了 SOW 和最近几次 status — 我不打算 second guess 之前发生过什么,因为我没在那个时候在场。我接下来这两周想做的是,把现在的 ground truth 看清楚,然后跟你 align 接下来怎么走。"

这段话做了三件事: 你拒绝 second guess (这是高级 PM 的姿态),你强调现在 vs 过去 (转移焦点),你 implicitly 承诺会有一个 forward-looking action (给客户一个未来感)。

**如果客户继续问"那你觉得前一个 PM 哪里做得不对"**

直接 deflect:

> "我没法公正地评价一个我没合作过的人。但我可以告诉你我接下来打算怎么做 — [一两句话讲你的 approach]。"

不评价 + 给一个替代答案,是 deflect 高手的标准动作。

---

## 决策点 4 — 第一周的"诊断"怎么做

被派进来救火的 PM,manager 期待你比一般新 PM 更快做出诊断。但诊断错了比慢做诊断更危险。怎么平衡?

**第一周做"诊断",不做"决策"**

把这两件事在脑子里 (和给 manager 的汇报里) 明确分开:

- 诊断: 描述问题在哪、严重程度、为什么会出现
- 决策: 我们要做什么、什么时候做、谁来做

第一周只做诊断,不做决策。如果 manager 问你"那我们要不要砍 scope",你说:

> "我有一个倾向,但不想在我看完 [还没看的几个东西] 之前给你 commit。我下周一可以给你一个有 conviction 的 recommendation。"

"有 conviction 的 recommendation"这种语言在 senior 那里很值钱 — 你不是在拖延,你是在 calibrate 自己的判断质量。

**诊断的 4 个 lens**

每个项目接手第一周,沿着这 4 个 lens 扫一遍:

| Lens | 你要看的 | 你要问的 |
|---|---|---|
| **Scope** | SOW vs 实际在做的事 vs 客户期待 | 这三者是同一个吗?差在哪? |
| **Quality** | Deliverable 的实际质量 — 看 demo、看代码、看 deck | 这些东西经得起 senior review 吗? |
| **People** | Team 里谁在拉车、谁在划水、谁要走 | 有没有关键人即将离开? |
| **Trust** | 客户对项目当前的信心 (问 sponsor 直接问) | 客户已经多少天没主动联系 PM 了? |

第四个 lens — Trust — 最容易被忽略。客户的"sentiment"是一个独立的 dimension。一个项目可能 scope 对、quality 对、people 对,但客户那边已经心凉了 — 这时候 fix 项目和 fix 客户感受是两件不同的事,后者更难。

怎么测客户 trust?最简单的指标: 客户最近一次主动 ping PM 是什么时候?如果是两周前,客户已经不指望项目了。

**诊断的输出格式**

第一周末给 manager 一份不超过一页的诊断,**不在客户面前讲**:

> **Week 1 Diagnostic**
>
> **Scope:**
> - [一句话 — SOW vs 实际 vs 客户期待之间最大的 gap]
>
> **Quality:**
> - [一句话 — 当前 deliverable 经不经得起 senior / client review]
>
> **People:**
> - [一句话 — team 现状,尤其有没有关键人风险]
>
> **Trust:**
> - [一句话 — 客户对项目的当前 sentiment]
>
> **My recommended next move:**
> - [1-2 个具体动作 — 不是大刀阔斧]
> - [Timeline]
>
> **What I want you to weigh in on:**
> - [1-2 个我自己拿不定主意的事]

最后两段是关键。Manager 不是要看你诊断,manager 是要看你**会不会主动 frame next move、会不会主动找他要 input**。

---

## 决策点 5 — 第一周之后的"小动作"

你诊断完了,可能看到 5-10 个要 fix 的事。但你不能第二周就发动 5-10 个改变 — team 会反弹,客户会 alarm。

**第二周只做 1-2 个 visible 的小改动**

挑的标准:

- 这件事改了之后,team 立刻感觉"日子好过了"
- 这件事不需要客户那边任何 approval
- 这件事改了之后,你能在 1 周内 demonstrate 效果

候选典型动作:

- 砍掉一个 useless 的 recurring meeting
- 简化一个 status update 流程
- 重写一份 backlog 让 priority 清晰
- 把卡在 inbox 里两周的客户问题清掉

不要选:

- 改技术架构
- 改 scope (这是第三周以后的事)
- 改 team 的分工 (这要更早 align)

**你做这个小动作的时候,要让 team 看见这是为他们做的**

不要在 manager 面前邀功。在 team 1-on-1 里轻描淡写:

> "我上周看下来,觉得 [那个 weekly meeting] 没什么用,我跟 [客户那边] 沟通了一下取消掉了。如果你们后面发现还是需要,我们再加回来。"

最后那句"再加回来"很重要 — 你不是来颠覆的,你是来调的。

---

## 决策点 6 — 怎么跟客户重新启动信任

如果客户那边对项目已经心凉了,你单独靠 fix 项目本身不够。你要专门花精力 fix 客户的感受。

**第一招: 直接 acknowledge 当前的尴尬**

跟 sponsor 单聊,**主动**起一个话题:

> "我想跟你说一下我对这个项目的 read — 老实讲我看下来,我觉得过去几个月有几个地方我们 deliver 得没达到你的期待。我没办法 undo 过去发生的事,但我希望你跟我直接讲一下你现在最 frustrated 的是什么,然后我们看怎么调。"

这段话做了几件事:

- 你主动承认 (而不是等客户提) — 主动承认让客户瞬间放松
- 你说"我没办法 undo" — 这是诚实,也是 frame 你的角色 (我是 forward-looking 的)
- 你 invite 客户讲 frustration — 让客户感觉被听到

**第二招: 在第 2-3 周给客户一个明显的、小的"win"**

客户的信任不是一个解释就能恢复的,他们要看到 evidence。在你接手后 2-3 周内,**人为 engineer 一个可见的小 win** — 一个 deliverable 提前一天交、一个之前卡住的问题被解决、一份 sponsor 等了很久的 analysis 出来。

不要等"自然发生" — 主动 plan 一个,确保发生。

**第三招: 把第一份 status update 重写**

接手后第一份给客户的 status update,**彻底重写格式**,不要沿用前任的模板。理由不是格式有多重要,理由是这是一个明显的 signal: 事情不一样了。

你不需要在 status update 里说"我重写了格式"。客户看到不一样的东西,自己会感受到。

---

## 一些 do / don't 速记

**Do**

- 进项目前花 30 分钟跟你 manager 把"为什么是你""客户知不知道""前任怎么了"这几个问题问清楚
- 第一天进去不解释自己为什么来
- 第一周做诊断,不做决策
- 用 Scope / Quality / People / Trust 四个 lens 扫一遍
- 第一周末给 manager 一份一页诊断 + 让他 weigh in 1-2 件事
- 第二周做 1-2 个小、可见、不需要客户 approval 的 fix
- 跟客户主动 acknowledge 过去的 gap,但不要 second guess 前任

**Don't**

- 不要试图 explain 自己为什么来
- 不要附和 team 或客户对前任的吐槽 (哪怕你心里同意)
- 不要在第一周做大动作或挑战已有决定
- 不要在客户面前 second guess 前任的 specific 决定
- 不要在 manager 没明确表态前,擅自跟客户讲"以前做错了"
- 不要等"自然发生" — 主动 engineer 一个小 win 给客户看

## 话术速查:17 条中途接手项目直接抄走用的句子

### 进项目前 — 跟 manager 对齐

**1. 问客户知不知情**
> "你把我派进来这件事,客户那边知道吗?知道的话他们 reaction 是什么?"

**2. 问前任去向**
> "前一个 PM 现在还在 team 吗?他的 transition 怎么办?如果不在了,有没有人能帮我重建项目历史?"

**3. 问 read 的时间和受众**
> "你希望我多久之内给你一个 read?这个 read 你会拿去跟谁讲?"

**4. 问对外 frame**
> "如果我看下来觉得项目方向有大问题,你期待我在客户面前怎么 handle?"

**5. 问 manager 的"官方诊断"**
> "Honestly,你觉得前一个 PM 主要的问题是什么?他做错的是哪一两件事?"

### 第一天进 team

**6. 第一天开场 (不解释为什么来)**
> "我是 [name]。我接下来这一周想做两件事 — 跟你们每个人 1-on-1,以及 catch up 项目的进展。这周我不会问'为什么这样做'类型的问题,我只想 catch up 到能跟你们对话的程度。"

**7. 被问"你来是干嘛的"**
> "公司这边觉得这个项目应该多一双眼睛 — 我就是那双眼睛。短期我先看,中期我们再看怎么分工。"

### 处理前任阴影

**8. Team 吐槽前任 (不附和不辩护)**
> "嗯,这个我知道了,不评价。我想问的是 — 你觉得现在 team 里有什么东西是大家都在小心翼翼绕过去的、其实需要有人 squarely 说出来?"

**9. 客户吐槽前任 (deflect)**
> "我接到这个项目的时候,只看了 SOW 和最近几次 status — 我不打算 second guess 之前发生过什么,因为我没在那个时候在场。我接下来这两周想做的是,把现在的 ground truth 看清楚,然后跟你 align 接下来怎么走。"

**10. 客户继续追问前任 (硬 deflect)**
> "我没法公正地评价一个我没合作过的人。但我可以告诉你我接下来打算怎么做 — [一两句话讲你的 approach]。"

### 诊断 vs 决策

**11. Manager 催决策 (你想再看一周)**
> "我有一个倾向,但不想在我看完 [还没看的几个东西] 之前给你 commit。我下周一可以给你一个有 conviction 的 recommendation。"

### 跟客户 reset 信任

**12. Sponsor 单聊 — 主动 acknowledge**
> "我想跟你说一下我对这个项目的 read — 老实讲我看下来,我觉得过去几个月有几个地方我们 deliver 得没达到你的期待。我没办法 undo 过去发生的事,但我希望你跟我直接讲一下你现在最 frustrated 的是什么,然后我们看怎么调。"

**13. 给客户讲接下来 2 周的 plan**
> "我接下来两周想做的不是大改,是先把 [一两件具体的小事] 整好,让你看到我们能 deliver 又一次小的 win。大的方向调整,我想再看一两周再跟你提。"

### 小动作 / 小 win

**14. 取消一个 useless 的会**
> "我上周看下来,觉得 [那个 weekly meeting] 没什么用,我跟 [客户那边] 沟通了一下取消掉了。如果你们后面发现还是需要,我们再加回来。"

**15. 给 team 解释你的小改动 (不邀功)**
> "我改了 [那个 status template]。原来的 template 让大家每周花太多时间填,新的我试着精简了一下。用一两周看效果,如果不顺再改。"

### 给 manager 的诊断

**16. Week 1 诊断邮件开头 (frame)**
> "下面是我接手第一周的 read — 不是 final 的判断,主要想让你 see what I see、然后我们 align 一下接下来的方向。"

**17. 主动 invite manager weigh in**
> "我自己有几件事拿不准主意,想请你 weigh in: [1] [一句话问题] [2] [一句话问题]。这两个我都有 default 想法,但希望你给一个 sanity check。"

---

往大了说,中途接手项目最反直觉的地方是: **你被派进来是因为情况紧迫,但你越是表现得紧迫,你越容易失败**。

紧迫感是 manager 给你的、客户给你的、可能你自己也内化了的。但 team 不喜欢紧迫的 PM,客户也不喜欢看起来 panic 的 vendor。你内心可以紧迫,但**外表必须 calm**。

中途接手成功的 PM,共同特点是: 进去之后第一周让所有人都觉得"this person knows what they're doing",然后第二三周悄悄地、小幅地、不引人注意地 fix 几件事,然后过 4-6 周突然大家发现"项目好像没那么糟了"。

整个过程没有 hero moment,没有戏剧性的 turnaround。Just steady, calm, deliberate execution。这是这一行最高级的状态之一。
