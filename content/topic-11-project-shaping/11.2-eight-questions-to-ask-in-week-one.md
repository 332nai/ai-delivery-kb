**Definition**

The first week of an AI project is the only week where it is socially free to ask basic, sometimes naive questions. After that, you are expected to know. There is a short list of questions which, if answered well, save the project months, and if answered poorly or evasively, tell you exactly where the risk lives.

**Why it matters in delivery**

Most failed AI projects fail on questions that could have been asked in week one. Who actually approves go live. Where the data really lives. What the success measure really is. These are not advanced questions. They are early questions that get postponed because the team is busy looking competent. The cost of asking in week one is a slightly awkward five minutes. The cost of asking in week eight is the project.

**How it works**

Below are the eight questions, in the order to ask them, and what to listen for in the answer.

| # | Question | What a good answer sounds like | What a bad answer sounds like |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Who is the single person who can say yes to launch? | One named person with the authority | "It will go to committee" |
| 2 | What does success look like in one sentence, no jargon? | Concrete, measurable, owned | "Transform our operations" |
| 3 | What is the worst that could happen if this goes wrong in production? | Specific scenario, who gets hurt, who finds out | "We do not expect issues" |
| 4 | What data exists today, where, and who controls access? | Names of systems and people | "We have lots of data" |
| 5 | What has been tried before in this space, and why did it stop? | Honest history with lessons | "Nothing, you are the first" |
| 6 | Who in the organisation would benefit from this project failing? | Named functions or individuals | "Everyone is aligned" |
| 7 | If we hit the timeline but miss the quality target, what happens? | A real fallback or descope plan | "We will hit both" |
| 8 | What is the next thing on this team's plate after we deliver? | Clear successor, ownership defined | Silence, or "we will see" |

You will not ask all eight in one meeting. Spread them across the kickoff, the first one to one with the sponsor, and the first technical session. Some are asked of executives, some of the operating team, some of the IT or data owner.

A few principles for how to ask:

*Ask the question, then stop talking.* Silence after an uncomfortable question is the most valuable diagnostic tool you have. People fill silence with the truth.

*Ask the same question to two different people.* The variance in the answers is more informative than either answer alone.

*Write the answers down in the project log on day one.* Three months later, the team will benefit from seeing what was said and by whom.

**Why each question matters**

A short justification for each, since the team will want one:

- **Q1** stops the project from being stuck in a swamp of consensus seeking. Identify the actual decider and prioritise their understanding of progress.
- **Q2** forces success to be measurable. If the client cannot say it in one sentence without jargon, neither can the team.
- **Q3** surfaces the real risk appetite. A client who has not imagined the failure mode is a client who will panic when it happens.
- **Q4** reveals whether data is a 2 week problem or a 16 week problem. This determines schedule realism more than any other factor.
- **Q5** uncovers political landmines. "We tried this two years ago" usually comes with the people who were on the losing side still in the building.
- **Q6** is rarely asked, but every project has internal dissenters. Knowing who they are early lets you engage them rather than be ambushed by them.
- **Q7** tests whether the client has thought about trade offs. If not, the team will be the one forced to face the trade off in week ten.
- **Q8** reveals whether this project is a real strategic priority or an isolated experiment. If nothing follows it, the urgency is theatre.

**PM decision points**

- Have I designed an agenda for week one that creates space for these questions, or only for status updates?
- For each question, who is the right person to ask, and in what setting?
- Am I prepared to act on uncomfortable answers, or am I asking just to look diligent?

**Watch outs**

- Do not ask all eight in a row. It feels like an interrogation. Interleave them with technical conversation and listening.
- Some clients answer with what they think you want to hear. Triangulate by asking the same question to peers, not just superiors.
- The answers you do not get are also data. If the sponsor will not answer Q1 or Q6, the project has a governance problem before it has a technical problem.

**Takeaway: there are eight questions that, in week one, are still free to ask. By week four they are expensive. By week eight they are unaskable. Ask them while you still can.**

**Qantas story**

Imagine running the first week of an AI project at Qantas to build an internal pricing analytics agent for the revenue management team. The team is talented, the SOW is signed, and the partner expects momentum. There is pressure to "get on with it".

Asking question one ("who can say yes to launch") in the first sponsor meeting surfaces something useful. The sponsor names a Head of Revenue Management, but adds that "anything customer facing also needs the Chief Customer Officer to be comfortable". The project was scoped as internal, but the answer suggests the boundary between internal and customer facing is not as clean as the SOW assumes. That is a 30 second answer with weeks of implication.

Asking question four ("what data exists, where, and who controls access") in the first technical session reveals that pricing data lives in three systems, two of which are owned by the IT data platform team and one of which is owned by a small revenue management analytics squad that runs its own SQL on its own server. The squad lead is on long leave for the next three weeks. The schedule cannot survive that without a workaround.

Asking question five ("what has been tried before") to a senior analyst in a coffee chat turns up that two years ago a similar project was attempted with a different consulting firm, did not finish, and the partner relationship soured. Some of the same Qantas stakeholders are still in the room. Knowing this changes how the team frames the first steering committee. Not differently in substance, but differently in tone.

The questions themselves are not advanced. Anyone could ask them. The discipline is asking them in week one, while the social cost of asking is at its lowest, and writing the answers down so the team can refer back to them when things get harder later.

The lesson for AI delivery is that the early weeks of a project are an information asymmetry problem, not a technical problem. The client knows things the team does not, and the team needs to extract that knowledge fast and cheaply. These eight questions are simply the most efficient extraction protocol the author has found.

**定义**

AI 项目的第一周是**唯一一个**问基础甚至显得幼稚的问题在社交上还免费的窗口。之后, 大家就默认你应该懂了。有一份很短的问题清单, 如果答得好能省下几个月时间, 如果答得差或者闪烁其词, 它会准确告诉你**风险藏在哪里**。

**为何在交付中重要**

绝大多数失败的 AI 项目, 输在第一周本来就该问的问题上。**谁真的能拍板上线**、**数据到底在哪**、**成功标准究竟是什么**。这些不是高深的问题。这些是被推迟的早期问题, 因为团队忙着**显得很能干**。第一周问这些问题, 代价是五分钟的小尴尬。第八周问, 代价是整个项目。

**运作原理**

下面是八个问题, 按建议顺序排列, 以及好答案和坏答案分别长什么样。

| # | 问题 | 好答案听起来像 | 坏答案听起来像 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 哪一个具体的人能拍板说"上线"? | 一个有授权的具体人名 | "要走委员会" |
| 2 | 用一句话, 不用 jargon, 描述成功长什么样? | 具体、可衡量、有 owner | "转型我们的运营" |
| 3 | 这个东西在生产上出岔子, **最坏会怎样**? | 具体场景、谁受伤、谁会知道 | "我们不预期会出问题" |
| 4 | 今天有什么数据、在哪、谁控制访问权限? | 具体的系统名和人名 | "我们数据很多" |
| 5 | 这个方向以前有人试过吗, 为什么停了? | 诚实的历史 + 教训 | "没有, 你们是第一个" |
| 6 | 公司里**谁会从这个项目失败中受益**? | 具体的部门或人名 | "大家立场都一致" |
| 7 | 时间线能保证, 但质量目标差一点, 怎么办? | 真正的 fallback 或缩范围方案 | "两个都能做到" |
| 8 | 我们交付完了之后, 这个团队下一步做什么? | 明确的后续 owner | 沉默, 或者"再看" |

你不会在一次会议里问完所有八个。**分散开**: kickoff、第一次和 sponsor 的 1:1、第一次技术会议。有些问高管, 有些问执行团队, 有些问 IT 或数据负责人。

几条问的姿势:

*问完, 然后闭嘴。* 在一个让人不舒服的问题之后, **沉默是最好的诊断工具**。人会用真话填充沉默。

*同一个问题问两个不同的人。* 两个答案之间的差异比任何一个答案本身信息量都大。

*第一天就把答案记在项目日志里。* 三个月之后, 团队会感谢自己当时记下了**谁说了什么**。

**为什么每个问题重要**

每条都配一个简短理由, 因为团队会想要一个理由:

- **Q1** 把项目从"共识沼泽"里拉出来。找到真正的决策者, 把他们对进度的理解放在首位。
- **Q2** 强制把"成功"变得可衡量。如果客户没法用一句没有 jargon 的话讲清楚, 团队也讲不清楚。
- **Q3** 暴露真正的风险偏好。**一个没想过失败长什么样的客户, 失败发生时一定会慌**。
- **Q4** 揭示数据是个两周问题还是十六周问题。这一条决定时间线的现实性, 比其他任何因素都强。
- **Q5** 挖出政治地雷。"我们两年前试过"通常意味着**当时输的那一方还在这栋楼里**。
- **Q6** 很少有人问, 但每个项目都有内部反对者。**早点知道他们是谁**, 你可以提前接触, 而不是被他们伏击。
- **Q7** 测试客户有没有思考过 trade off。如果没有, 第十周被迫面对 trade off 的就是团队。
- **Q8** 揭示这个项目到底是真战略优先级, 还是孤立实验。**如果后面什么都没有, 紧迫感就是表演**。

**PM 决策点**

- 我设计的第一周议程, 是给这些问题留了空间, 还是只留给 status update?
- 每个问题, 谁是合适的对话对象, 在什么场合问?
- 我准备好对**不舒服的答案**采取行动了吗, 还是问只是为了显得尽职?

**注意事项**

- 不要一口气连问八个。**会像审讯**。穿插在技术对话和倾听之间问。
- 有些客户会说他们以为你想听的话。**交叉验证**: 同样的问题问同级别的同事, 而不是只问上级。
- **没拿到的答案也是数据**。如果 sponsor 不愿意回答 Q1 或 Q6, 这个项目在出现技术问题之前, 已经有治理问题了。

**要点总结: 有八个问题在第一周问还是免费的。到第四周变贵。到第八周已经问不出口。**趁还能问, 把它们问了。****

**Qantas 案例**

设想你在 Qantas 跑一个 AI 项目的第一周, 给收益管理团队做一个内部定价分析 agent。团队很能干, SOW 已经签了, partner 期待"势头"。压力是"赶紧动起来"。

在第一次 sponsor 会议上问出 Q1 ("谁能拍板上线"), 浮出一个有用的东西。Sponsor 提到一位 Head of Revenue Management, 但补了一句"任何**面向客户**的东西也需要 Chief Customer Officer 觉得放心"。项目被定位成内部的, 但这个答案说明**内部和面向客户的边界没有 SOW 假设的那么清晰**。这是一个 30 秒的答案, 但有数周的影响。

第一次技术会议问 Q4 ("数据在哪、谁控制") , 发现定价数据散在三个系统里, 两个归 IT 数据平台团队管, 一个归收益管理团队自己一个小分析小组管 (他们在自己的服务器上跑自己的 SQL)。这个小组的负责人**接下来三周休长假**。时间线在没有变通方案的情况下扛不住这个。

跟一位资深分析师约咖啡时问 Q5 ("以前有人试过吗"), 得到的信息是两年前同方向有过一个项目、跟另一家咨询公司合作、没做完、合作关系搞坏了, **当时 Qantas 这边的几位 stakeholder 还在房间里**。知道这件事会改变团队对第一次 steering committee 的措辞: 不是改变内容, 而是改变语气。

这些问题本身没什么高深。**谁都能问**。难的是**在第一周问**, 在问的社交成本还最低的时候问, 并且把答案记下来, 让团队在后面变难的时候有个回看的参照。

AI 交付的启示: 项目早期是一个**信息不对称问题, 不是技术问题**。客户知道一些团队不知道的事, 团队要快、便宜地把那些知识挖出来。这八个问题, 不过是作者目前找到的最高效的提取协议而已。
