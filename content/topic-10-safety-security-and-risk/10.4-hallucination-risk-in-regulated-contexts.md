**Definition**

Hallucination risk is acute in regulated domains because the cost of a confident wrong answer is uniquely high. A made up case citation in a legal brief, a fabricated drug interaction in a clinical setting, or an invented tax rule in a financial filing can trigger malpractice claims, patient harm, regulatory action, and reputational damage. The model's "I do not know" signal is unreliable, so the burden is on the system design.

**Why it matters in delivery**

Regulated clients (law firms, banks, hospitals, government) have a much lower tolerance for hallucination than the general market. Their reviewers, partners, and compliance teams will reject any system that produces unsourced or unverifiable output. PMs working in these sectors must design for verifiability from the start. The product is not "an AI that answers questions," it is "an AI whose answers can be audited."

**How it works**

| Domain | High risk hallucination types | Standard mitigations |
|---|---|---|
| Legal | Fake case citations, invented statutes, wrong jurisdiction | RAG with strict source grounding, citation required, human review |
| Medical | Wrong drug doses, fabricated drug interactions, invented diagnoses | RAG over verified medical references, refuse if not in source, mandatory clinician review |
| Financial | Made up regulations, wrong calculations, invented tax codes | Tool use for calculations, RAG over current regulations, human review |
| Insurance | Wrong policy terms, fabricated exclusions | RAG over actual policy docs, citations required |
| Government | Wrong policy, fabricated procedures, outdated information | RAG over current policy, date checks, human review |

The pattern across all these: RAG with strict grounding, mandatory citations, human in the loop for high stakes outputs, refusal when retrieval returns nothing, and audit logs of every output.

**PM decision points**

- What is the cost of a single hallucination in this domain, and what is the acceptable rate?
- Is every output traceable to a source, and can a reviewer verify in seconds?
- Where is the human review checkpoint, and is it actually being used or rubber stamped?

**Watch outs**

- Confidence in fluency, regulated audiences mistake good prose for good content, the model is good at the former
- Workflow shortcuts, humans in the loop often stop reviewing after the first 100 good outputs, design for sustained vigilance
- Liability framing, document who is responsible for the final output, the AI is never the responsible party

**Takeaway: in regulated domains, the product is auditability, not just answers. Design for verification on every output.**

**Qantas story**

Hallucination in regulated contexts is acute for an airline because aviation is one of the most regulated industries in the world, with safety, consumer protection, and operational rules that have legal weight.

Consider a hypothetical Qantas AI assistant for cabin crew helping with procedural questions during turnarounds. A crew member asks about the correct procedure for a passenger who has a specific medical condition and requires a particular kind of supplemental oxygen. The model has plausible training data about aviation medical procedures, oxygen equipment, and passenger handling. It can produce a confident, fluent, and entirely wrong answer in three seconds.

The wrong answer in this context has real consequences. A passenger who is given the wrong oxygen handling procedure could suffer serious health effects. The crew member who relied on the AI is in a difficult position. Qantas faces regulatory exposure under the relevant aviation safety regulations. The brand impact is significant.

The design pattern that works in regulated contexts is auditability. The AI is not allowed to answer procedural questions from its own knowledge. It must retrieve from current, verified Qantas operational manuals. If retrieval returns nothing relevant or returns ambiguous content, the model is instructed to refuse and direct the crew member to the appropriate procedural reference, not to attempt a fluent answer. Every output that involves a procedural commitment must include the source document the answer came from. The crew member can click through to verify the source in seconds.

The audit trail is equally important. Every interaction is logged with the exact retrieved content, the exact model output, and any clarifications. If a procedural error occurs and there is a question about whether the AI contributed to it, the trail provides the evidence. Without the trail, the AI is a black box and the airline cannot defend its use.

The human review pattern matters too. For high stakes procedural questions, the AI is positioned as a draft or a starting point, not as the final word. The crew member is trained to verify against the source document before acting. The expectation is not that the AI is always right, but that the AI plus human review is reliably right.

The regulated context also imposes documentation requirements. Aviation safety regulators expect to see evidence that automated tools used in safety contexts have been tested, validated, and have appropriate oversight. The eval dataset, the change management process, and the incident response procedures all become regulatory artefacts, not just engineering hygiene.

For AI delivery, the broader lesson is that in regulated domains, the product is not "an AI that answers questions." The product is "an AI whose answers can be audited, verified, and traced to authoritative sources." Designing for this from the start is different from designing for general consumer use. Clients in regulated industries (banking, healthcare, government, aviation, energy) understand this immediately because they live with regulation every day. The job is to translate the regulation into AI design principles, which most engineering teams have not encountered before.

**定义**

在受监管场景下,幻觉风险意味着 LLM 给出错误信息这件事不仅仅是质量问题,而是合规问题。在法律、医疗、金融、以及其他受监管领域,AI 的错误能制造真实的法律责任、监管暴露、患者或客户伤害。这里的代价跟普通场景比是数量级的差别。

**为何在交付中重要**

受监管行业是企业 AI 里最大、最有价值的客户群。但它们也正是"幻觉风险让产品最难交付"的地方。理解高风险场景如何改变设计取舍的 PM,能在这些行业里把产品发出去。不理解的人,大多停在 demo 阶段。

**运作原理**

| 行业 | 高风险幻觉的例子 | 监管 / 责任暴露 |
|---|---|---|
| 法律 | 引用一个不存在的案例、编造法规 | 律师执照风险、客户伤害 |
| 医疗 | 推荐错误剂量、漏掉禁忌症 | 患者伤害、医疗事故 |
| 金融 | 给错税务建议、错误描述产品功能 | 监管罚款、客户损失 |
| 航空 | 关于安全程序、重量限制的错误信息 | 安全暴露、监管违规 |
| 政府 | 错误引用政策、资格规则 | 公民伤害、政治暴露 |
| 教育 | 错误描述学位要求 | 学生伤害 |

设计含义:

- 接地是不可商量的: 缺少检索源时,系统不应该生成响应
- 引用是必须的: 用户必须能看到答案的来源,以便核实
- 合适的不确定性表达: 不清楚的时候,模型必须说不清楚,不能瞎猜
- 人工审查关卡: 高风险输出永远不应该没经审核就发到客户面前
- 责任分配: AI 错误归谁负责,这件事要在 AI 系统运营方和客户之间写清楚

**PM 决策点**

- 这个用例的真实风险等级是什么? (从"小麻烦"到"威胁生命")
- 哪些设计选择是风险等级强制要求的? (RAG、拒答行为、人工审查关卡)
- 责任在哪里? AI 错了归谁负责?这件事不在合同里写清楚,出事再争就晚了。

**注意事项**

- 受监管行业的客户经常被销售团队"AI 最棒"的承诺烧过,要做好他们持保守姿态的预期。
- 监管要求经常滞后于 AI 能力,客户的法务团队可能不知道怎么把现有规则套到 AI 上。
- 在受监管场景里,偏保守。"AI 起草、人工签字" 几乎永远是第一步该用的 pattern。

**要点总结: 在受监管场景里,幻觉是合规问题,不是质量问题。架构要设计成"没有人工批准,AI 永远不会直接走到受监管决策"。**

**Qantas 案例**

Qantas 运营在几个受监管场景里,每一个高风险幻觉都需要专门的架构。

设想一个看起来简单的客户安全信息问题: "我能不能把 5000mAh 的锂电池放在手提行李里?" 航空当局 (澳洲是 CASA) 指定的真实答案是具体的,取决于电池容量、数量、保护措施。如果 AI 给了错的答案 (比如本该禁止的时候允许),风险是真实的: 锂电池在飞机上起火,众所周知是危险的。

这里的设计回应必须保守。面向客户的 AI 助手应该: (1) 检索经过批准的危险品文档里实际的 CASA 规则;(2) 引用它检索到的具体条款;(3) 在规则模糊、或者没有明确答案被检出来的时候,表达不确定性;(4) 引导客户去 Qantas 网站的官方危险品资源 (而不是只依赖 AI 答案);(5) 对边缘情况,建议直接联系 Qantas。

医疗场景下,类似的动态。"我带着 X 医疗状况能飞吗?" 是一个比表面看起来风险高得多的问题。Qantas 对各类医疗状况有特定政策,通常基于 IATA 或 ICAO 指南。AI 应该: 检索特定政策、引用它、在状况新奇或边缘时建议走医疗放行流程、不要直接告诉客户带着某种医疗状况飞是安全的。

金融场景下,这种情况不太常见但相关。"这次 Frequent Flyer 兑换我要交什么税?" 是细微的 (取决于司法管辖区、兑换类型、客户身份)。AI 的答案应该是一般性的,并引导客户去找专业建议,不要直接给具体的税务建议。

所有这些受监管场景下的更大设计原则:

1. **绑定到源**。AI 永远不应该"凭知识"生成响应。每个响应都应该来源于检索到的内容 (政策、程序、规则)。如果没有可检索的内容,AI 应该拒答并转交。

2. **包含引用**。客户 (或审核它的坐席) 应该能看到 AI 答案的出处。这允许核实、也建立信任。

3. **明确拒答**。AI 不确定的时候、或者风险很高的时候,它应该升级到人工。"我需要把这个转给 Qantas 团队成员处理" 是比"一个错答案"好得多的输出。

4. **合适的不确定性表达**。当答案只有部分清楚的时候,AI 应该说"基于 [来源] 看起来是 X,但请核实,因为 [原因]"。绝对确定性应该留给绝对清楚的事情。

5. **高风险走人工审查**。如果 AI 在受监管领域生成建议 (医疗放行、安全、监管合规),永远不要直接发到客户。AI 起草,人工审查并签字。

往大了说,这件事给 AI 交付的启示是: 受监管场景需要的架构,把"客户问题的安全答案"放在"体验的便利"之上。客户经常会反问"我们为什么不能直接把 AI 答案发出去?"。正确的回应是教育: 在受监管场景里,AI 的错误不是质量问题,是合规问题,代价必须被尊重。能清晰走完这条逻辑的交付者,做出来的是受监管客户真的能上线的系统。讲不清的人,做出来的是永远停在 demo 阶段的 demo。
