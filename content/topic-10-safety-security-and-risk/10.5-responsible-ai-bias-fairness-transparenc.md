**Definition**

Responsible AI covers the practices and disclosures that demonstrate an AI system is fair, transparent, and accountable. In enterprise procurement, this translates to specific document and process requirements: model cards, bias testing, explainability, disclosure to end users, human oversight, and incident response. The principles are universal, the procurement reality is paperwork.

**Why it matters in delivery**

Responsible AI is increasingly a hard gate in enterprise procurement, especially in finance, government, healthcare, and HR adjacent use cases. PMs who can speak the language of model cards, impact assessments, and bias evaluation pass procurement gates quickly. Those who treat it as a soft topic to address later cause weeks of delay or get vetoed. The procurement reality is often dull, but it is real.

**How it works**

| Procurement ask | What it really means | How to satisfy it |
|---|---|---|
| Model card | Documentation of model capabilities, limitations, training data, intended use | Use provider model cards plus a system specific addendum |
| Bias and fairness testing | Evidence the system does not systematically disadvantage protected groups | Stratified eval set across demographics, document results |
| Explainability | Can a reviewer understand why the system produced a given output | Show retrieved sources, tool calls, prompt logic, not "model interpretability" in the deep ML sense |
| Human oversight | Humans must be able to review, override, and intervene | Approval steps for high stakes outputs, audit logs, ability to disable |
| Disclosure | End users know they are interacting with AI | Clear labelling in UI, AI generated content marked |
| Incident response | Process for when AI causes harm | Defined escalation, takedown ability, root cause review |
| AI impact assessment | Risk assessment specific to this use case | Documented analysis of potential harms and mitigations |
| Regulatory alignment | Compliance with EU AI Act, Australian AI Ethics Principles, sectoral rules | Map system to regulatory categories, document accordingly |

**PM decision points**

- What responsible AI documentation will procurement actually require, and when?
- Has the team tested for bias across relevant groups, or just average accuracy?
- Is there a documented incident response process, with named owners?

**Watch outs**

- Bias testing only finds what you measure for, define the protected attributes early
- "The provider is responsible for fairness" is wrong, the deployer is accountable for outcomes
- Disclosure rules vary by jurisdiction, EU AI Act and US state laws are different from each other

**Takeaway: responsible AI in enterprise is procurement language for "show your work." Build the documentation as you build the system, not at the end.**

**Qantas story**

Responsible AI in the Qantas context combines regulatory obligation, brand commitment, and operational discipline. The Australian government has published AI ethics principles. The aviation regulator has views on AI use in safety contexts. Qantas's own brand stands for trust and reliability. All of these intersect in any AI deployment.

Consider a hypothetical Qantas AI capability for personalised customer offers. The system reads each member's account, travel history, and preferences, and generates tailored offers (route specific promotions, status match opportunities, dining experiences, partner offers). The capability is commercially attractive and customers would generally welcome the personalisation. The responsible AI considerations are real.

Bias and fairness are not obvious in customer offer personalisation, but they exist. If the AI systematically generates better offers for one demographic than another (because the training data reflected historical patterns), the outcome could be discriminatory in ways that have legal and brand consequences. The discipline is to define protected attributes (age, location, family status, accessibility needs) and test offer quality across each group on a stratified eval set. If the AI systematically underdelivers for some groups, that is a fairness issue regardless of intent.

Transparency is the other dimension. Customers should know when they are seeing AI generated content. Disclosure can be subtle (an icon, a tooltip, a settings page) but it should be there. The EU AI Act and emerging Australian regulation both push toward more disclosure, and the brand consideration of "do we want customers to feel surprised when they learn this was AI" usually points the same way.

Human oversight applies to high impact decisions. The personalised offer system that suggests offers is fine without per offer human review. A system that automatically applies status matches or refunds would need human review before any commitment is sent to the customer. The principle is that decisions with material customer impact have a human in the loop.

The procurement consideration is real too. Increasingly, enterprise clients buying AI services from Qantas (corporate accounts, partner airlines, agency networks) ask for responsible AI documentation. Model cards for the underlying models. Bias testing reports for the specific Qantas use case. Incident response procedures. Audit trails. The team that does this work proactively can satisfy procurement requests in days. The team that does it reactively spends weeks producing documentation under pressure.

The Australian regulatory environment is also evolving. The federal government has published AI ethics principles, the privacy commissioner has views on AI uses of personal information, and sector specific regulators (aviation, financial services, healthcare) are developing their own positions. A responsible AI practice that maps current obligations and tracks emerging ones avoids being caught by regulatory change.

For AI delivery, the lesson is that responsible AI in enterprise contexts is procurement language for "show your work." It is not primarily about philosophy or ethics, though those matter. It is about documentation, testing, oversight, and accountability that satisfy procurement reviews, regulatory expectations, and brand standards. The team that builds these artefacts as they build the system has them when they are needed. The team that treats them as an afterthought finds out the hard way that they are gates, not nice to haves. Clients respect a practitioner who understands this and frames responsible AI as part of the engineering discipline, not as a separate workstream that someone else owns.

**定义**

负责任 AI 框架 (有时缩写成 RAI) 是一套企业用来确保 AI 系统公平、无偏见、透明、安全、可问责的政策、流程、工具。它既包括硬约束 (合规要求) 也包括软规范 (品牌一致性、客户信任)。RAI 是 AI 在企业里"被信任并被采用"的核心。

**为何在交付中重要**

每家大型企业现在要么已经有、要么正在建立 RAI 政策。理解它的 PM 能从需求确定阶段就构建出合规的 AI 系统。不理解的人会在评审阶段碰到延迟,因为 RAI 团队会把没充分处理的考虑打回来。

**运作原理**

| 维度 | 它的意思 | 实践中长什么样 |
|---|---|---|
| 公平性 | AI 不应该不公平地对特定群体不利 | 跨人口结构 (年龄、性别、族裔) 测试输出,看响应是否不平等 |
| 偏见 | 训练数据的偏见不应该渗透到决策里 | 偏见审计、偏见缓解技术 |
| 透明度 | 用户应该知道自己在跟 AI 交互 | 明确披露,系统行为可解释 |
| 问责制 | AI 出问题的时候,有人拥有它 | 明确所有权、失败响应程序 |
| 隐私 | 用户数据被尊重和保护 | (前面讨论过) |
| 安全性 | 系统不能造成伤害 | 拒答行为、内容审核 |
| 可解释性 | 决策可以被解释出来 | 至少要有粗略、透明的推理过程 |
| 人工监督 | 人类保留有意义的控制 | 高风险决策走人工审批关卡 |

实践中,大部分企业 RAI 框架结合了:

1. **政策**: 关于哪些用例可以、哪些用例不能用 AI 的明确规则
2. **审查关卡**: 在需求确定、实施、发布阶段的检查点
3. **工具**: 偏见检测、公平性测试、内容审核工具
4. **培训**: 团队内部关于 RAI 的教育
5. **治理**: 审查 AI 投资的内部委员会

**PM 决策点**

- 这个用例是否涉及高风险公平性问题 (招聘、信贷、福利)?这是第一道筛。
- 我们怎么向用户披露这是 AI 交互?
- AI 错误的问责归谁?响应程序定义清楚了吗?

**注意事项**

- RAI 框架相对新,公司经常还在建设期,要预期它会演化。
- 客户的 RAI 政策可能比你的更保守,要先了解他们具体的框架。
- "AI 伦理" 是 RAI 的子集,但范围更窄,不要把这两个搞混。

**要点总结: RAI 不是事后再说。早期参与 RAI 框架、为合规做设计、把它当作客户信任的来源。**

**Qantas 案例**

设想 Qantas 的 RAI 框架应用到客服 AI 助手。Qantas 有内部 RAI 政策 (跟所有大型澳洲企业一样)。这个政策应用到多个维度。

公平性: 客服 AI 不应该基于姓名、推断的人口属性、或其他特征,对客户有不同对待。这需要测试。一组对照查询,跨越人口构成不同的客户姓名,应该收到对等的服务质量。这是一个真实的 eval 任务,集成进回归测试。

透明度: 客户应该知道自己在跟 AI、不是人类交互。AI 助手自我介绍是"Qantas 助手,一个 AI 助手"。客户可以随时要求转人工。这种披露要符合澳洲消费者保护标准。

问责制: 当 AI 错误地告诉客户某件事,谁拥有这个错?Qantas 拥有。从客户视角看,AI 的错就是 Qantas 的错。这意味着 Qantas 内部要有人拥有 AI 系统、并对错误做出响应。这个所有权要被命名 (比如客服运营负责人加上 AI 平台负责人共同拥有)。响应程序要被定义清楚。客户怎么升级 AI 错误?它们怎么被记录、调查、补救?

人工监督: AI 不被允许在没有人类签字的情况下做某些决策。补偿超过阈值。订单变更超过价值阈值。任何涉及监管暴露的事情 (比如医疗放行)。每一个都有定义好的人工审查关卡。

偏见: AI 可能继承训练数据里的偏见。比如 LLM 在某些人口结构的语言表达上可能比其他的处理得更好。Qantas 的 RAI 审查要求测试这一点、缓解识别出来的偏见、对系统性问题持续监控。

内容审核: AI 不应该产出不合适的内容 (脏话、歧视性语言、不专业语气)。这一部分靠 LLM 训练 (现代模型对这种拒答得很好)、一部分靠后处理过滤、一部分靠 prompt 设计。

可解释性: 当 AI 做出建议 (比如应用补偿、拒绝请求),它应该提供理由。这帮客服坐席审查 AI 的工作,也让客户的申诉成为可能。

更微妙的一个 RAI 关注是"AI 实际在做什么决策?"。客服 AI 是真在做决策,还是只在起草响应?在 Qantas 这种场景里,真正的决策 (批准补偿、升级、调整等等) 是人类做的。AI 起草。但当客户从"AI 渠道"收到响应时,他们体验到的是"Qantas 的决定",不是"AI 起草人类批准的草稿"。这给问责制制造了微妙: 流程是人类驱动的,但客户体验是 AI 驱动的。

往大了说,这件事给 AI 交付的启示是: RAI 框架是受监管客户里开发工作必要的一部分。早期参与的交付者 (在需求确定阶段就跟 RAI 团队开会,而不是到评审阶段才去) 把方案设计成合规的,避免延迟。在需求确定里试图绕开 RAI 关注的人,在评审关卡上撞墙,这经常把项目延迟好几个月。Qantas 和大部分大型澳洲企业现在都严肃对待 RAI。能说出"这是我们如何思考公平性、透明度、问责制,这是我们的具体设计选择"的交付者,会胜过那些把 RAI 当成"打勾合规框"的人。
