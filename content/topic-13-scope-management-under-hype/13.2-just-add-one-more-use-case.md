**Definition**

A specific pattern of scope creep, common enough to deserve its own playbook, is the request that arrives as "just add one more use case, it should not be hard since you have already built the platform". The request is almost always made casually, often in the middle of an unrelated conversation, by a senior stakeholder. The team's response in that moment determines whether the next four weeks are spent building or arguing.

**Why it matters in delivery**

This phrasing has three rhetorical effects that put the team at a disadvantage. First, "just" minimises the work. Second, "one more" frames the addition as small relative to what already exists. Third, "should not be hard" applies social pressure: agreeing is the easy path, disagreeing requires explanation. The request is engineered, often unconsciously, to be hard to refuse.

The reality is that most "one more use case" requests are, in fact, hard. Each new use case typically requires new evals, new prompt work, new data, new edge case handling, new sign offs from the operating team, and new production monitoring. The marginal cost is rarely small. The PM's job is to engage with the request seriously without being steamrolled by its phrasing.

**How it works**

The response has three parts, delivered in a specific order. Skipping any of them produces a worse outcome.

*Part 1: Take the request seriously.* The opening move is not pushback, it is engagement. "That is an interesting idea, let me think through what it would involve. Can you tell me a bit more about what you have in mind?" This achieves two things: it signals respect for the stakeholder, and it buys time to think.

The follow up question almost always reveals useful information. The stakeholder usually has not thought through the details, and articulating them often surfaces complexity that was not visible. Sometimes the request collapses on its own once it has to be specified.

*Part 2: Walk through what would actually be involved.* The key move is to make the implicit cost visible. The cost framework has roughly six components, each of which applies to most new use cases.

| Cost component | Typical effort |
|---|---|
| Specifying the new use case (input-output table, edge cases) | 1 to 2 weeks |
| Adding eval rows and rebuilding the eval set | 0.5 to 1 week |
| Prompt engineering or agent reconfiguration | 1 to 3 weeks |
| Data access and integration if new sources needed | 2 to 8 weeks, highly variable |
| Operating team sign off and training | 1 to 2 weeks |
| Production monitoring and rollback plan | 0.5 to 1 week |

When totalled, even a "small" new use case is usually 4 to 12 weeks of incremental work. The total is rarely small. Showing the breakdown is how the request stops being characterised as easy.

*Part 3: Offer paths forward.* The team is not refusing. The team is showing the trade off. There are usually four paths, and the stakeholder should be invited to pick.

The four paths are: add the use case and extend the timeline accordingly; add the use case and drop something else of equivalent effort; add the use case as a v2 item with appropriate planning; or absorb a smaller version of the request that captures most of the value at a fraction of the effort. Each is a real option. The stakeholder picks.

What this approach achieves is the conversion of a soft social request into a structured decision. The stakeholder is no longer asking "can you do this", they are choosing between trade offs. That is a different and healthier conversation.

**Phrasing matters**

The exact words matter. A few framings that tend to work, and a few that tend not to.

Tends to work: "Let me think through what would be involved so we can decide together how to handle it." Tends not to work: "That is out of scope, sorry."

Tends to work: "This is actually four pieces of work, let me show you." Tends not to work: "It is harder than it looks."

Tends to work: "We have a couple of options on how to fit this in." Tends not to work: "We do not have time for that."

The pattern: framing that invites collaboration on a real problem works. Framing that puts the team on the side of refusal does not.

**PM decision points**

- When a casual scope request arrives, do I default to engagement and questions, or to instinctive resistance?
- Have I prepared a cost framework I can walk through quickly, or do I have to invent the explanation each time?
- Am I offering paths forward, or am I leaving the stakeholder with only "yes" or "no"?

**Watch outs**

- "It should not be hard" is rarely true, but saying it out loud will not change the stakeholder's mind. Showing the breakdown changes their mind.
- The stakeholder may be testing whether the team has thought through the implications. A confident, structured response is reassuring even if the answer is "this would extend the timeline by six weeks".
- If the stakeholder is the executive sponsor, escalate the conversation internally first (see 13.5). Do not commit on the spot to anything that has commercial or timeline implications.

**Takeaway: "just add one more use case" is engineered to be hard to refuse. The response is engagement first, structured cost breakdown second, paths forward third. Convert a soft social request into a structured decision, and the stakeholder usually picks a reasonable path on their own.**

**Qantas story**

Imagine being deep in the build phase of a Qantas AI project, an agent for the customer service team that handles policy lookups across baggage, refunds, and frequent flyer. The team is in week six of a ten week build. The first sponsor review of the early build went well. In the corridor after the meeting, the executive sponsor catches the PM and says, casually, "this looks great. Should be easy to extend it to also handle disruption communications, right? Same kind of thing, just a different content area."

The temptation to nod and say "we will look into it" is strong. The temptation to immediately list reasons it cannot be done is also strong. Both lose.

The PM takes the request seriously. "That is interesting, I want to make sure I understand what you have in mind. Are you thinking about the agent generating customer notifications for disruption events, or helping internal staff find disruption policies, or something else?" The sponsor clarifies they were thinking of both, vaguely.

The PM books a 30 minute follow up for the next day. In that meeting, the PM walks the sponsor through what the addition would involve. Disruption communications has a different operating team, which means new operating team sign off, new eval set development, new edge case workshop. Disruption content is generated under time pressure and is regulated, which means additional compliance review and a different review workflow. Disruption data lives in a different system, which means new integration. The total incremental scope is roughly six to eight weeks.

The PM then offers paths. Path one: extend the project by six to eight weeks, requiring commercial renegotiation. Path two: descope something from the current ten week plan to make room, with the trade off that one of the current capabilities will be delivered in v2 instead. Path three: include disruption communications in a separate v2 phase, with v1 launching on the current scope on schedule. Path four: absorb a smaller version, specifically a disruption policy lookup capability (which uses similar mechanics to the existing baggage and refund lookups), but not disruption communications generation, which is meaningfully different.

The sponsor reflects. The original instinct was that the addition would be small. The breakdown made the actual size visible. The sponsor picks path four: include the smaller version of disruption policy lookup in the v1 scope, with a one week extension, and put disruption communications generation on the v2 roadmap.

The result is a decision that captures roughly 30 percent of the value the sponsor wanted, at roughly 10 percent of the incremental cost, with no commercial renegotiation. The sponsor leaves feeling listened to and accommodated. The team protects the v1 timeline. Both parties feel good about the outcome.

The interesting counterfactual is what would have happened if the PM had nodded and said "we will look into it". The team would have spent a week in fragmented internal debate about whether to do it, then come back to the sponsor with an awkward "it turns out this is more work than expected", which would have damaged credibility. Or worse, the team would have absorbed it, missed the timeline, and the sponsor would have been disappointed at launch.

The lesson for AI delivery is that the response to "just add one more use case" is structured engagement, not reflexive yes or reflexive no. The structure has three parts: take the request seriously, walk through the actual cost, offer paths forward. Done well, the conversation ends with the stakeholder making a thoughtful choice, often the choice the team would have recommended, but reached by their own judgment rather than imposed by the team. That is a much more durable outcome.

**定义**

一种特定的 scope creep pattern, 常见到值得有自己的 playbook, 是这样到达的请求: "再加一个 use case 吧, 应该不难, 平台都建好了"。这个请求几乎总是被随口说出来的, 经常是在无关对话的中间, 由一位高级 stakeholder 说出来。团队在那一刻的回应, 决定了接下来四周是花在建造上还是花在争吵上。

**为何在交付中重要**

这种措辞**有三个让团队处于不利位置的修辞效果**。**第一**, "再加" 把工作量最小化。**第二**, "一个" 把添加框架成相对于已有的东西很小。**第三**, "应该不难" 施加社交压力: 同意是容易的路, 不同意需要解释。这个请求被设计 (经常是无意识地) 成难以拒绝。

**现实是**, 大多数 "再加一个 use case" 的请求, 实际上很难。每个新 use case 通常需要新的 evals、新的 prompt 工作、新的数据、新的边缘情况处理、运营团队的新签字、生产监控。边际成本很少是小的。PM 的工作是认真 engage 请求, 同时不被它的措辞推平。

**运作原理**

回应**分三部分**, 按特定顺序说出来。跳过任何一部分都产出更差的结果。

*第一部分: 认真对待请求。* **起手动作不是 push back, 是 engage**。**"这想法有意思, 我想想看会涉及什么。能多说一点你想的是什么吗?"** **这做到两件事**: **它向 stakeholder 传递尊重, 也争取时间思考**。

追问几乎总是揭示有用的信息。Stakeholder 通常没想清楚细节, 被迫表达细节时经常浮出之前看不见的复杂度。有时候请求在不得不被规定时自己崩了。

*第二部分: 走一遍实际涉及什么。* **关键动作是把隐含成本变得可见**。**成本框架大致有六个组件**, **每个都适用于大多数新 use case**。

| 成本组件 | 典型工作量 |
|---|---|
| 规定新 use case (输入-输出表、边缘情况) | 1 到 2 周 |
| 加 eval 行、重建 eval set | 0.5 到 1 周 |
| Prompt 工程或 agent 重新配置 | 1 到 3 周 |
| 数据访问和集成 (如果需要新来源) | 2 到 8 周, 高度可变 |
| 运营团队签字和培训 | 1 到 2 周 |
| 生产监控和回滚计划 | 0.5 到 1 周 |

**加总起来**, 哪怕一个 "小" 的新 use case, 通常是 4 到 12 周的增量工作。总数很少是小的。展示这个分解, 是让请求停止被描述成 "容易" 的方法。

*第三部分: 提供前进的路径。* **团队不是在拒绝**。**团队在展示 trade off**。**通常有四条路径**, **应该邀请 stakeholder 选**。

**四条路径是**: 加入这个 use case 并相应延长时间线; 加入这个 use case 并放弃一个工作量相当的东西; 把它作为 v2 项, 带合适的规划; 吸收一个更小的版本, 用很小一部分工作量捕获大部分价值。每一条都是真选项。Stakeholder 选。

这种方式做到的事情, 是把一个软的社交请求转换成结构化的决策。Stakeholder 不再问 "你能做吗", 他们在 trade off 之间选。那是一场不同的、也更健康的对话。

**措辞很重要**

具体用词很重要。几个倾向有效的措辞, 和几个倾向无效的措辞。

**倾向有效**: "让我想想会涉及什么, 我们一起决定怎么处理。" **倾向无效**: "那超出范围, 抱歉。"

**倾向有效**: "这其实是四块工作, 让我给你看一下。" **倾向无效**: "比看起来难。"

**倾向有效**: "我们有几个把它装进去的选项。" **倾向无效**: "我们没时间做那个。"

Pattern: 邀请协作处理真问题的措辞有效。把团队定位在拒绝那一侧的措辞无效。

**PM 决策点**

- 当一个随口的范围请求到达, 我**默认 engage 和提问**, 还是**本能抵抗**?
- 我**有没有准备好一个能快速走一遍的成本框架**, 还是每次都得现场发明解释?
- 我**有没有提供前进的路径**, 还是只给 stakeholder 留下 "是" 或 "不是"?

**注意事项**

- **"应该不难" 很少是真的**, 但**口头说出来不会改变 stakeholder 的想法**。**展示分解才改变**。
- **Stakeholder 可能在测试团队有没有想清楚后果**。**一个有信心的、结构化的回应是让人安心的**, **哪怕答案是 "这会把时间线延长六周"**。
- **如果 stakeholder 是 executive sponsor**, **先在内部升级对话** (见 13.5)。**不要当场承诺任何有商业或时间线影响的事**。

**要点总结: "再加一个 use case" 被设计成难以拒绝。回应是 engage 在先、结构化成本分解在中、前进路径在后。把一个软的社交请求转换成结构化决策, stakeholder 通常会自己选一条合理的路径。**

**Qantas 案例**

设想你在一个 Qantas AI 项目建造阶段的深处, 给客服团队的 agent, 处理跨行李、退款、frequent flyer 的政策查询。团队在十周建造的第六周。第一次 sponsor review 跑得不错。**会后走廊里**, 执行 sponsor 截住 PM, 随口说, "这看起来很棒。也很容易扩展到处理中断通讯吧?同一类东西, 只是不同内容领域。"

点头说 "我们看看" 的诱惑很强。立刻列举做不到的理由的诱惑也很强。**两个都输**。

PM **认真对待请求**。"这想法有意思, 我想确保我理解你想的是什么。你想的是 agent 生成中断事件的客户通知, 还是帮内部员工查中断政策, 还是别的?" Sponsor 澄清说他想的是两个都要, 模糊地。

PM 预订第二天 30 分钟的跟进。**那场会议里**, PM 带 sponsor 走一遍这次添加会涉及什么。中断通讯有一个不同的运营团队, 这意味着新的运营团队签字、新的 eval set 开发、新的边缘情况 workshop。中断内容是在时间压力下生成的, 而且受监管, 这意味着额外的合规审查和不同的审阅工作流。中断数据住在不同的系统里, 这意味着新集成。总增量范围大约六到八周。

PM 然后提供路径。**路径一**: 把项目延长六到八周, **需要商业重谈**。**路径二**: 从当前十周计划里 descope 一些东西腾空间, trade off 是当前能力之一会改在 v2 交付。**路径三**: 把中断通讯放在一个单独的 v2 阶段, v1 按当前范围按时上线。**路径四**: 吸收一个更小的版本, 具体是中断政策查询能力 (它跟现有的行李和退款查询用相似的机制), 但不包括中断通讯生成 (那是有意义不同的)。

Sponsor 反思。原始本能是这个添加会很小。分解让实际大小变得可见。Sponsor 选路径四: 在 v1 范围里加入更小版本的中断政策查询, **带一周延期**, 把中断通讯生成放到 v2 路线图上。

结果是一个捕获了 sponsor 想要的大约 30% 价值、用大约 10% 的增量成本、没有商业重谈的决定。Sponsor 离开时感觉被听到、被满足。团队保护了 v1 时间线。双方对结果都感觉好。

有意思的反事实是, 如果 PM 当时点头说 "我们看看", 会发生什么。团队会花一周在分散的内部辩论上, 然后回到 sponsor 那里, 带着尴尬的 "结果发现这比预期的工作多", 这会损害可信度。**或者更糟**, 团队会吸收它、错过时间线、sponsor 在上线时失望。

AI 交付的启示: 对 "再加一个 use case" 的回应是结构化 engagement, 不是反射性的是或反射性的不。**结构有三部分**: 认真对待请求、走一遍实际成本、提供前进的路径。**做好了**, 对话以 stakeholder 做一个深思熟虑的选择结束, 经常是团队本来会推荐的选择, 但是经由他们自己的判断到达, 而不是被团队强加。那是一个更耐用得多的结果。
