**Definition**

A change request process is the workflow by which a verbal or informal scope change request becomes a documented decision: written description, impact assessment, agreed disposition (accepted, deferred, declined), signed off by both sides. In AI projects, change requests are especially valuable because the work is inherently fluid and the temptation to "just absorb it" is high. The skill is running this process in a way that protects the project without making the client feel they are being bureaucratic-ed at.

**Why it matters in delivery**

Verbal requests have two properties that make them dangerous. First, they are deniable: a request made in a corridor can be forgotten, misremembered, or selectively recalled later when the impact becomes visible. Second, they accumulate without anyone tracking the total: each verbal addition feels small, but ten of them collectively change the project meaningfully. A change request process converts both problems into a documented audit trail.

The reason the process is hard to introduce is that it can feel cold. A senior client who casually asks for an addition does not want to receive a four-page form in response. The PM's job is to make the formalisation feel like a service to the client (clarity, no surprises, agreed expectations) rather than a barrier (paperwork, friction, distrust). Done well, the process becomes invisible. Done poorly, the client feels nickel-and-dimed.

**How it works**

A useful change request artifact is one page, with five fields. Anything longer is bureaucratic. Anything shorter loses the audit trail.

| Field | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Request | One paragraph plain English description of what is being asked for. Written by the team, confirmed by the requestor. |
| Source | Who asked, when, in what context. "Sarah Chen, marketing director, in the steering committee meeting on 14 March." |
| Impact | Effort, timeline, cost. A few bullets, not a essay. "Approximately 3 to 4 weeks incremental work. Timeline extension or descope of equivalent. No change in fee at current size." |
| Recommendation | The team's view, with reasoning. "Recommend deferring to v2 because the timeline impact would push critical capabilities past the agreed launch date." |
| Disposition | Accepted, deferred to v2, declined. Signed off by the agreed decision maker. |

The artifact is short, friendly, and clear. It is not a contract amendment. It is a memo. The form matters less than the practice of producing one for every meaningful scope discussion.

**How to introduce the process without friction**

The framing for the client is critical. The wrong framing is "we have a change request process you have to follow". The right framing is "we want to make sure nothing gets lost or forgotten, so we will write up each change discussion as a short memo for both of us to sign off on. This protects both sides from misremembering later."

Most clients, when this is framed as protective rather than restrictive, will agree. The few who push back are usually clients who specifically want the ability to deny later that they asked for something, and that is itself useful information.

A useful tactic is to volunteer to do the writing. The team produces the change request artifact, sends it for the client's review, the client either confirms or edits. The client does not have to fill out a form. The team does that work. This removes most of the friction.

**When to trigger the process**

Not every casual question needs a change request. The trigger threshold is: any request that, if absorbed, would change the team's planning. Asking "could the agent also work in Mandarin" might not need a change request if the response is "we discussed this, it is in the v2 list". The change request is needed if the response is "let me think about whether to bring it forward into v1".

In practice, three triggers cover most cases:

1. *The request involves more than a day of incremental work.* Anything below that, just do it and note it in the weekly update.
2. *The request involves a capability that is in the v1 will-not-do list.* The list was signed off, so revisiting it is by definition a documented decision.
3. *The request comes from a stakeholder above the operating sponsor.* High level requests carry political weight, so the documentation is protection for everyone.

Below these thresholds, the team has discretion to absorb minor requests without formalisation. Above them, formalise.

**Common failure modes**

A few patterns that defeat the process if not watched for.

*Change request as decoration.* The team writes up requests but does not get them signed off, so they live in a document folder and have no force. Always close the loop with explicit disposition.

*Change request as defence.* The team uses the process to slow everything down and refuse most requests. The process becomes a barrier rather than a clarifier. Watch for the team's tone in change request memos: if every memo recommends "decline", something is wrong.

*Change request as theatre.* The team writes up requests after the fact, to make a decision look documented. This is worse than not having a process, because it pretends to rigor without the substance.

The healthy practice is: requests are documented at the time they arise, with a recommendation made in good faith, with the client's input invited, and with the final disposition signed off promptly.

**PM decision points**

- Have I introduced the change request process early in the project, or am I trying to introduce it after the first big request?
- Am I doing the writing for the client, or am I asking the client to fill in forms?
- Is the disposition for each request being signed off and stored, or are change requests piling up without resolution?

**Watch outs**

- Do not let change requests substitute for the conversation. The artifact captures the decision, but the decision happens in the room. Have the conversation first, write the memo second.
- Avoid the word "change request" in client-facing language if it feels heavy. "Decision memo" or "scope note" works equally well and feels less bureaucratic.
- The process is for both directions. The team should also write change requests when the team needs to renegotiate scope due to discoveries during build. Symmetry is important.

**Takeaway: a one page change request memo, written by the team and signed off by both sides, is the cheapest scope hygiene available. The skill is making it feel like a service to the client, not a barrier. Volunteer to do the writing, frame it as protection, and close every memo with a clear disposition.**

**Qantas story**

Imagine being in week five of a Qantas AI project, an agent for the corporate sales team that drafts proposal sections based on past wins. The project has been progressing well. The corporate sales director has been engaged, supportive, and casual. Every couple of weeks, the director sends a Slack message with an idea: "could the agent also pull pricing from past proposals?", "what about cross-referencing customer feedback?", "could it also draft the cover letter?". Each idea is well-intentioned. None are part of the agreed v1 scope.

The temptation is to either absorb the small ones quietly or to start formally pushing back. Both create problems. Absorbing creates a hidden scope drift that becomes visible when the timeline slips. Pushing back creates friction with a stakeholder who is otherwise helpful.

The PM introduces a change request process, in a low key way. In the next weekly check in, the PM says to the director: "you have had some great ideas in our recent conversations. I want to make sure none of them get lost, so I am going to start writing each one up as a short memo, with my take on whether it fits v1 or v2 or something else. I will send them to you to sign off. That way we have a shared record and we can come back to any of them later." The director agrees readily.

The next time an idea arrives via Slack, the PM writes a one paragraph memo: the request as the PM understood it, the impact (rough effort estimate), the recommendation (in this case, deferral to v2 because the work would conflict with the launch date), and a request for the director's sign off. The director reads it, confirms the recommendation, and the request goes onto the v2 backlog. Total elapsed time: less than 30 minutes of work, including the writing.

Over the next four weeks, the director sends four more ideas. Each gets a one paragraph memo. Three are deferred to v2. One is accepted as a minor in-scope clarification. The memos are filed in a shared folder. The director, far from feeling bureaucratic-ed at, appears to enjoy the clarity. In one steering committee, the director references the memos as evidence that "the team is on top of things".

The interesting test comes in week eight, when the regional VP joins a steering committee and asks why a particular capability is not being built. The director responds, "good question, we discussed that in week five, here is the memo, we agreed to defer it to v2 because of the timeline impact. Should we revisit?" The conversation lasts three minutes. The VP nods and moves on. Without the memo, that conversation could have taken half an hour, exposed a misalignment between the director and the team, and damaged credibility with the VP.

At project end, the team produces a closing report. The change request memos are listed as an annex. There are 11 of them across the project. Seven deferred to v2, two accepted as minor in-scope clarifications, one declined as out of scope, one absorbed into a small scope adjustment. The director signs off on the closing report. The relationship is in better shape than it would have been if the team had managed the same requests informally, because every decision is documented and shared.

The lesson for AI delivery is that the change request process is not bureaucracy when it is run with discipline. It is the lightest possible hygiene that converts verbal requests into documented decisions, protecting both sides from later misalignment. The PM's job is to introduce it in a way that feels like a service, to do the writing themselves, and to close the loop on each memo. Done this way, the process becomes nearly invisible, and the project benefits from clarity that informal management cannot provide.

**定义**

Change request 流程是**把口头或非正式的范围变更请求, 转换成有文档的决策**的工作流: 书面描述、影响评估、商定处置 (接受、推迟、拒绝)、双方签字。AI 项目里, change request 尤其有价值, 因为**工作本身是流动的, "吸收掉算了" 的诱惑很高**。技艺是把这个流程跑得保护项目, 同时不让客户感觉被官僚化对待。

**为何在交付中重要**

口头请求**有两个让它们危险的属性**。**第一**, 它们是可否认的: 走廊里说的请求, 可以被遗忘、记错、或在影响显现时被选择性回忆。**第二**, 它们累积起来而没人追踪总量: 每一次口头添加感觉很小, 但十次合起来有意义地改变项目。Change request 流程把两个问题都转换成有文档的审计轨迹。

这个流程难以引入的原因, 是它会让人觉得冷。一位随口要求加东西的资深客户, 不希望收到一份四页表格作为回应。PM 的工作是把形式化做得感觉像是给客户的服务 (清晰、没意外、商定的预期) **而不是障碍** (纸面工作、摩擦、不信任)。做得好, 这个流程变成隐形的。做得差, 客户感觉被锱铢必较。

**运作原理**

一份有用的 change request 产物是一页, **五个字段**。比这长就是官僚。比这短就丢了审计轨迹。

| 字段 | 写什么 |
|---|---|
| 请求 | 一段大白话描述被要求的东西。团队写, 请求方确认。 |
| 来源 | 谁问的, 什么时候, 什么场合。"Sarah Chen, 营销总监, 3 月 14 日 steering committee 会上。" |
| 影响 | 工作量、时间线、成本。几个 bullet, 不是 essay。"大约 3 到 4 周的增量工作。时间线延期, 或等量 descope。当前规模下费率不变。" |
| 建议 | 团队的看法, 带理由。"建议推到 v2, 因为时间线影响会把关键能力推到商定上线日之后。" |
| 处置 | 接受、推到 v2、拒绝。由商定的决策者签字。 |

这个产物短、友好、清晰。它不是合同修订。它是 memo。形式没那么重要, 重要的是 "为每一次有意义的范围讨论产出一份" 这个实践。

**怎么无摩擦地引入这个流程**

对客户的措辞至关重要。错的措辞是 "我们有一个 change request 流程你得遵守"。对的措辞是 "我们想确保什么都不丢、不忘, 所以我会把每一次变更讨论写成一份短 memo, 双方签字。这保护两边不被以后记错伤害。"

**大多数客户**, 当这件事被框架成保护性而非限制性时, 会同意。少数 push back 的客户, 通常是 specifically 想要 "以后能否认自己提过" 的能力, 而那本身就是有用的信息。

一个有用的策略是主动承担写的工作。团队产出 change request 产物, 发给客户审阅, 客户要么确认要么修改。**客户不必填表**。团队做那份工作。这消除了大部分摩擦。

**什么时候触发这个流程**

不是每一个随口的问题都需要 change request。**触发阈值是**: 任何会改变团队规划的请求, 如果被吸收。问 "agent 能不能也用中文", 如果回应是 "我们讨论过, 在 v2 清单上", 可能不需要 change request。Change request 在回应是 "让我想想要不要把它带前到 v1" 时才需要。

实操中, 三个触发器覆盖大多数情况:

1. *请求涉及超过一天的增量工作*。**低于这个的, 直接做, 在周报里 note 一下**。
2. *请求涉及一个在 v1 不做清单里的能力*。**那份清单签过字, 重新讨论它按定义就是一个文档化的决策**。
3. *请求来自高于运营 sponsor 的 stakeholder*。**高层请求带政治分量**, **文档化对所有人都是保护**。

低于这些阈值, 团队有酌情权吸收小请求, 不形式化。高于阈值, 形式化。

**常见失败模式**

几个 pattern, 没注意就会让流程失效。

*Change request 作为装饰*。**团队写起来, 但不让它们被签字**, **它们住在文档夹里, 没有效力**。**永远闭环到明确的处置**。

*Change request 作为防御*。**团队用这个流程来慢化一切、拒绝大多数请求**。**流程变成障碍而非澄清器**。**注意团队在 change request memo 里的语气**: **如果每一份 memo 都建议 "拒绝", 哪里出问题了**。

*Change request 作为戏剧*。**团队事后写起来, 让一个决定看起来有文档**。**这比没有流程更糟**, 因为**它假装有严谨, 没有实质**。

**健康的实践是**: 请求在出现时被文档化, 带善意做出的建议, 邀请客户的输入, 最终处置及时被签字。

**PM 决策点**

- 我**有没有在项目早期引入 change request 流程**, 还是**在第一个大请求出现后才尝试引入**?
- 我**有没有替客户做写的工作**, 还是**让客户填表**?
- 每个请求的处置**有没有被签字和存档**, 还是 change request 在堆积没解决?

**注意事项**

- **不要让 change request 替代对话**。**产物捕捉决策, 但决策在房间里发生**。**先对话, 再写 memo**。
- **避免在面向客户的语言中用 "change request" 这个词, 如果它感觉重**。**"决策 memo" 或 "范围说明" 同样有效, 感觉不那么官僚**。
- **这个流程是双向的**。**当团队因为建造期间的发现需要重新谈判范围时, 团队也应该写 change request**。**对称性很重要**。

**要点总结: 一页 change request memo, 团队写、双方签, 是市面上最便宜的范围卫生。技艺是让它感觉像给客户的服务, 不是障碍。主动承担写的工作, 把它框架成保护, 每份 memo 闭环到明确处置。**

**Qantas 案例**

设想你在一个 Qantas AI 项目第五周, 给企业销售团队的 agent, 基于过往胜单起草提案章节。**项目进展不错**。企业销售总监 engaged、支持、随和。每隔几周, 总监发一条 Slack 消息, 带一个想法: "agent 能不能也从过往提案抽定价?"、"那交叉引用客户反馈呢?"、"它能不能也起草 cover letter?"。每个想法都出于好意。没有一个在商定的 v1 范围里。

诱惑是要么悄悄吸收小的, 要么开始正式 push back。两个都制造问题。吸收制造隐形的 scope 漂移, 在时间线滑动时变得可见。Push back 跟一个本来很帮忙的 stakeholder 制造摩擦。

PM 以一种低调的方式引入 change request 流程。下一次周会上, PM 跟总监说: "你最近的对话里有一些很棒的想法。我想确保没有一个丢掉, 所以我会开始把每一个写成短 memo, 带我对它适合 v1、v2、还是别的的看法。我会发给你签字。这样我们有一份共享记录, 以后任何一个我们都能回头看。" **总监欣然同意**。

下一次想法通过 Slack 到达时, PM 写一段 memo: PM 理解的请求、影响 (粗略工作量估计)、建议 (这次是推到 v2, 因为工作会跟上线日冲突)、**请求总监签字**。总监读它、确认建议、请求进入 v2 backlog。**总耗时**: 不到 30 分钟工作, 包括写。

接下来四周, 总监发了四个更多的想法。每个收到一段 memo。三个推到 v2。一个被接受作为小的范围内澄清。Memo 存在共享文件夹里。总监远没有感觉被官僚化, 似乎享受这种清晰。有一次 steering committee 上, 总监引用 memo 作为 "团队 on top of things" 的证据。

有意思的测试出现在第八周, 当区域 VP 加入一次 steering committee 并问为什么某个能力没在建。**总监回应**, "好问题, 我们第五周讨论过, 这是 memo, 我们因为时间线影响同意推到 v2。要不要重审?" 对话持续三分钟。VP 点头继续往下走。没有那份 memo, 那场对话可能用半小时, 暴露总监跟团队之间的错位, 损害团队在 VP 面前的可信度。

项目结束时, 团队产出一份收尾报告。Change request memo 作为附录列出。项目期间有 11 份。七份推到 v2, 两份作为小的范围内澄清接受, 一份作为超范围拒绝, 一份吸收成小的范围调整。总监给收尾报告签字。关系的状态比团队非正式管理同样请求的情况要好, 因为**每一个决策都有文档、被共享**。

AI 交付的启示: 当 change request 流程被有纪律地跑时, 它不是官僚。它是最轻量的可能的卫生, 把口头请求转换成有文档的决策, 保护双方免于以后的错位。PM 的工作是用感觉像服务的方式引入它、自己做写的工作、闭环每一份 memo。这样做, 流程变得几乎隐形, 项目得益于非正式管理无法提供的清晰。
