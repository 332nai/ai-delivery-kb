# Explaining AI project costs to a CFO

A CFO and an engineer don't share a language. This isn't because the CFO isn't smart. Most CFOs are smarter than most engineers, because they make bigger decisions on less information. It's that their head is filled with a different set of words and a different set of things they care about.

The single most common mistake when talking AI projects to a CFO: the PM opens with "we're using GPT-4o, 128k context window, about 500k API calls per month, X dollars per thousand tokens..." — three sentences in, the CFO has tuned out. **It's not that they can't follow. It's that they're not hearing what they came to hear.**

When a CFO hears any cost, their brain is automatically sorting three questions:

1. Is this cost **predictable**?
2. Does this cost **scale**? (unit economics)
3. Is this **CapEx** or **OpEx**? (accounting and cashflow implications)

Talk AI costs in this order. Not in the order of "what tech we're using".

---

## Decision 1 — How to open

The CFO usually gives you a short window — 15 to 30 minutes, the same magnitude as the T18.3 exec meeting. Your first sentence can't be technical. It needs to directly answer at least one of those three questions in their head.

**Wrong opener**

> "Our AI system is mainly built on OpenAI's GPT-4o, paired with a RAG pipeline, vector DB is Pinecone, current monthly API call volume is around..."

By this point the CFO is already wondering "did this person mistake me for an engineer".

**Right opener**

> "Quick framing before I get into numbers — the cost structure of an AI project doesn't look like traditional software. Software cost is upfront CapEx plus maintenance. AI cost is **ongoing, usage-based OpEx**. So as I take you through the numbers, switch your mental model from CapEx to OpEx."

What this opener does:

- You go straight to what the CFO cares about (CapEx vs OpEx)
- You give them a mental model (traditional software vs AI)
- You haven't used a single piece of jargon
- You've given them an instruction ("switch to OpEx mode") — CFOs like people who hand them a framework rather than asking them to assemble one

If the CFO is an AI skeptic, add one line:

> "There's a flip side to that OpEx model worth flagging. The upside is you don't carry the CapEx risk of a traditional IT investment — if the project fails, you can stop the bleed quickly. The downside is once it scales, the cost grows continuously, and you need to model it into forecast from day one."

Volunteering the downside is an advanced move. CFOs have heard vendors pitch upside their whole career — when you preempt with downside, they immediately put you in a different bucket.

---

## Decision 2 — Break AI cost into pieces a CFO can model

The CFO doesn't need to know what a token, context window, or embedding is. They need to know **what makes up this cost, what fraction each piece is, what's fixed, what's variable**.

**The pieces (in the order a CFO cares about)**

| Cost piece | Nature | What the CFO cares about |
|---|---|---|
| **Model API calls** | Variable, scales with usage | Unit cost? Negotiable at volume? |
| **Data / infrastructure** (vector DB, storage, compute) | Semi-fixed, baseline + variable | Baseline cost? Marginal cost curve? |
| **Third-party tools / SaaS** (eval platforms, observability, orchestration) | Mostly fixed | Multi-year contracts? Cancellable? |
| **People** (PM, engineer, analyst, ops) | Fixed but the largest line | What share of total spend? |
| **Hidden costs** (compliance, security, re-training) | Unpredictable, easy to underestimate | What buffer % of budget? |

When walking through, **emphasise people and hidden costs**. Most PMs only talk to CFOs about API spend, but in real projects these two lines combined often hit 60-70% of total. If a CFO only hears about API spend, they think the project is cheap. The first budget overrun and your credibility is gone.

**Example walkthrough (an internal AI project with AUD 1M annual budget)**

> "Let me break the annualised cost down for you. Model API calls, around 80k — under 10%. This scales with usage; if we roll out company-wide, this goes to 250-300k. Second piece, infrastructure and data, around 120k, mostly fixed, doesn't grow much with scale. Third, third-party tools, around 100k, multi-year contracts. Fourth, and the largest, people — around 600k, which is 4-5 FTE fully loaded. Last, I've set aside 100k as a buffer for compliance and unexpected items. I'd recommend not cutting this — I'll explain why."

Why this walkthrough works:

- The CFO sees the structure in one second (where the weight sits)
- You've handed them a scale assumption (80k → 250-300k)
- You've flagged the buffer and framed it as "don't cut" (preempting the negotiation)
- 100% in CFO vocabulary (FTE, annualised, scale)

---

## Decision 3 — Why AI cost can't be forecasted like software

This is the first deeper question a CFO will ask after the breakdown: **"Why can't you give me a clean forecast the way I get for traditional software?"**

Most PMs don't answer this well, because they haven't fully worked it out in their own head.

**The version that lands**

> "Three sources of forecasting uncertainty in AI cost — let me walk you through each.

> First, **unit cost itself is falling fast**. A task that cost X a year ago costs X over 3 today, and the trend isn't stopping any time soon. So my forecast for the model API piece, 12 months out, is most likely lower than current — I just don't know by how much.

> Second, **the usage curve isn't linear**. Once an AI project gains internal traction, usage grows virally — not user count times average usage, it's user count times new use cases each user discovers. That's why we need a buffer.

> Third, **model version cycles trigger re-engineering cost**. Every 6 to 12 months a new model comes out. We have to decide whether to switch — switching has migration cost, not switching has competitive cost. This piece is hardest to forecast.

> Putting it together — what I'm giving you isn't a point estimate, it's a range. I'm confident on the midpoint, less confident on the edges. For budgeting, I'd recommend locking budget at the 60th percentile of the range with 30% flex, rather than at the midpoint."

After this, the CFO will usually be quiet for a few seconds, then nod. Reasons:

- You acknowledged uncertainty (CFOs hate people who fake certainty)
- You broke the uncertainty into three pieces, each with a cause
- You gave them a specific budgeting recommendation (60th percentile + 30% flex)
- "60th percentile" is finance vocabulary — it lands directly

---

## Decision 4 — How to talk about ROI / value

After cost, the CFO will ask: "What does this project save or earn?"

**The most common mistake: a soft number**

Wrong: "We estimate this project will save the company 2 million."

The CFO immediately asks: "How did you get that?" You can't answer, and your credibility is gone.

**The right approach**

Don't hand over a single number. Hand over **a formula + assumptions + sensitivity**.

> "What I'm giving you isn't a number, it's a formula — you tell me which assumption to challenge and we adjust.

> Formula: AI handles a case in 1/N the time of a human; the company handles M cases per year; the fully loaded cost per case is X. So the value AI substitutes is M × X × (1 - 1/N) × adoption rate.

> Key assumptions:
> - N = 5 (measured from our PoC)
> - M = 100,000 / year (your number)
> - X = AUD 80 / case (FTE-loaded)
> - Adoption rate = 60% (conservative — not every case suits AI)

> That gives 3.84 million annualised value.

> But here's the sensitivity: if adoption only hits 30%, value drops to 1.92 million. If N is actually 3 (not 5), value drops another 30%. So the floor is roughly 1 million, the ceiling is 3.8 to 4 million, the median estimate is around 2.5 million."

Why this works:

- You haven't committed to a number (no risk on you)
- You've exposed every assumption (the CFO can stress-test them)
- You've given floor / ceiling / median (the three numbers a CFO uses for decisions)
- The conversation has shifted from "you tell me what we save" to "let's work out what we save together" — a huge relationship shift

**On ROI / payback period**

CFOs love these two numbers. If you've worked them out, say them:

> "On the floor estimate, payback is 14 months. On the median, 8 months. On the ceiling, 5 months. So even in the most pessimistic scenario, two-year IRR is still positive."

If you're not sure of the calculation, **don't fake it**. Say:

> "ROI and IRR I'd want to work out with your finance team, not pull a number out myself — I don't have your cost of capital or internal hurdle rate. Can we get someone from your finance side in a follow-up?"

Pass the ball back, and pass it back professionally.

## Decision 5 — The most common CFO pushbacks, and how to answer

**Pushback 1: "Why not just buy a SaaS tool instead of building this in-house?"**

> "Fair question. We did consider the SaaS path — the trade-off is: SaaS total cost is probably 30-50% higher but implementation is much shorter; in-house is cheaper but has a 6-12 month ramp. Which fits depends on two factors: one, whether our use case has differentiation (if it does, in-house is worth it); two, whether this capability is strategic (if it's strategic, you don't want it outsourced to a SaaS vendor). My recommendation is [a specific path], but I'd want you to weigh in on that."

Hand the decision to the CFO rather than defending a fixed answer.

**Pushback 2: "AI prices are falling — can we wait a year and start later?"**

A classic CFO question. Answer this wrong and you've lost the project.

> "Prices are falling, but the cost of waiting is real. Let me run the numbers: if we start this year and the system is live in 12 months, that year is worth, say, 2 million in value. If we wait a year and start in 2027, we don't see output until then — the opportunity cost of waiting is that 2 million. Even if AI unit cost drops 50%, model API is under 10% of our project cost — a 50% drop saves us 40k, nowhere near the opportunity cost."

This uses the CFO's own logic (NPV, opportunity cost) to push back on the CFO. They'll respect it.

**Pushback 3: "Why does this project need 4-5 FTE?"**

The standard CFO move to cut budget.

> "I understand the instinct. Headcount is the most under-estimated line in an AI project — people assume AI is a tool, but actually it's a full stack of PM + engineer + data + ops + business interface. Industry benchmark: Anthropic and OpenAI's own deployment teams run at a 1:3 to 1:5 ratio against clients — at our scale, 4-5 people is lean. But if budget is genuinely tight, I can give you a 3-person plan — you'd need to accept that the 6-month timeline becomes 9-12 months, and we'd need to cut [a specific scope item]."

Give a reduced version, but frame the cost of cutting (longer timeline + scope cut). Let the CFO decide whether it's worth it. **Never agree to cut headcount without naming the trade-off.**

**Pushback 4: "What's our exposure if this project fails?"**

The hardest question, because the CFO is evaluating downside. **Don't minimise**, but frame it as bounded.

> "Failure has a few modes. One — technical infeasibility. We'd catch this at the PoC stage; exposure is around the PoC budget, roughly 1.5 million. Two — built but adoption doesn't take. Exposure is PoC + production investment, around 4-5 million. Three — reputational or governance issues. Financial loss might be small but reputational loss is hard to quantify. I'd suggest we set a kill criteria at the PoC stage — if [a specific metric] isn't hit, we stop. That way worst case our exposure is the PoC piece, not the full project."

Proactively proposing a kill criteria is an advanced move. When the CFO sees you set your own stop-loss, their trust in you goes up immediately.

---

## A short do / don't

**Do**

- Open with CapEx vs OpEx framing
- Break cost into 5 pieces — people and hidden cost are non-negotiable to mention
- Give a range with sensitivity, not a single number
- Give floor / ceiling / median when talking value
- Proactively propose kill criteria
- Let the CFO challenge assumptions instead of defending a fixed number

**Don't**

- Don't start with tech details (model / token / context window — instant credibility loss)
- Don't give a soft ROI number ("we'll save around 2 million")
- Don't minimise downside — the CFO stops trusting you the moment you do
- Don't defend a fixed proposal — give trade-offs and let them decide
- Don't fake what you don't know — if you can't do IRR, say so and ask for finance
- Don't try to please CFO and CTO in the same sentence — when you're with the CFO, 100% CFO vocabulary

---

## Phrasebook: 16 lines to use directly with a CFO

### Opening frame

**1. First sentence — CapEx vs OpEx**
> "Quick framing — the cost structure of an AI project doesn't look like traditional software. Software is upfront CapEx plus maintenance. AI is ongoing, usage-based OpEx. As I walk through numbers, switch your mental model to OpEx mode."

**2. With a skeptical CFO, volunteer the downside**
> "There's a flip side to that OpEx model worth flagging. Upside: you don't carry traditional IT CapEx risk — failure lets you stop the bleed fast. Downside: once it scales, this cost grows continuously, and needs to be modelled into forecast from day one."

### Breaking down cost

**3. Five-piece breakdown (example)**
> "Annualised: model API around 80k, infrastructure 120k, third-party tools 100k, people 600k, compliance and buffer 100k. The weight sits on people — that's the most under-estimated line in most AI budgets."

**4. Preempt the buffer cut**
> "I've set aside 100k as buffer for compliance and unexpected items. I'd recommend not cutting it — governance and re-training are real cost in AI projects, just less visible."

### Explaining uncertainty

**5. Three sources of uncertainty**
> "AI cost can't be point-estimated like traditional software, for three reasons: unit cost is falling fast, the usage curve isn't linear, and model version cycles trigger re-engineering cost. What I'm giving you is a range, not a number."

**6. Lock at the 60th percentile**
> "For budgeting, I'd recommend locking at the 60th percentile of the range with 30% flex, rather than at the midpoint. That way worst case doesn't surprise you."

### Talking value

**7. A formula not a number**
> "What I'm giving you isn't a number, it's a formula — you challenge any assumption, we adjust. Formula is [M × X × (1 - 1/N) × adoption]. Key assumptions are [...]."

**8. Floor / ceiling / median**
> "Floor is around 1 million, ceiling 3.8 million, median estimate 2.5 million. Even on the floor, two-year IRR is positive."

**9. If you can't do IRR, say so**
> "ROI and IRR I'd want to work out with your finance team, not pull a number myself — I don't have your cost of capital or hurdle rate. Can we loop someone in from your finance side?"

### Pushback responses

**10. "Why not buy a SaaS instead?"**
> "Considered. Trade-off: SaaS is 30-50% more expensive total but faster, in-house is cheaper but has a 6-12 month ramp. Right answer depends on two factors: differentiation and strategic importance. My recommendation is [path], but I'd want you to weigh in."

**11. "Why not wait a year?"**
> "Cost of waiting is real. If we start now, 12 months out we have a system worth 2 million annualised. Wait a year and we don't see that until 2027 — opportunity cost is 2 million. Even if unit AI cost falls 50%, model API is under 10% of our project — a 50% drop saves us 40k, far less than opportunity cost."

**12. "Why so many people?"**
> "Headcount is the most under-estimated line in AI. Industry benchmark is 1:3 to 1:5 deployment team to client team — at our scale, 4-5 people is lean. If budget is genuinely tight, I can give you a 3-person plan, but timeline goes from 6 months to 9-12, and [specific scope item] gets cut. You decide whether it's worth it."

**13. "What's exposure if it fails?"**
> "Three failure modes: technical infeasibility (caught at PoC, 1.5M exposure), built but no adoption (4-5M), reputational (hard to quantify). I'd set a kill criteria at PoC stage — if [metric] isn't hit, we stop. Worst-case exposure is the PoC piece, not the full project."

### Proactive proposals

**14. Proactively propose kill criteria**
> "I'd recommend we align a kill criteria at PoC stage — if [specific metric] isn't hit by [time], we stop. That caps your downside."

**15. Proactively propose budget cadence**
> "I'd recommend releasing budget on a quarterly review basis rather than approved annually — gives you a re-evaluation point each quarter, and gives me a quarterly checkpoint to demonstrate value."

### When you don't know

**16. CFO asks a finance question you can't answer**
> "I can't give you an evidence-based answer on that right now — I'd want to work it out with your finance team so the number is one you trust. Let me set up a follow-up with [specific role]."

---

Stepping back: the CFO isn't your adversary. The CFO is one of the most important allies you have on this project. Whether they approve budget decides whether the project runs, for how long, and how far it scales.

But the CFO's vocabulary doesn't match yours. They don't need to become you — you need to switch into their language. Specifically: **CapEx vs OpEx, unit economics, predictability, ROI, payback, IRR, kill criteria, sensitivity** — these words need to come out of your mouth as naturally as they come out of theirs.

The moment they feel "this PM speaks my language", everything you ask for afterwards — budget, headcount, escalation cover — gets easier. In AI delivery, PMs who can hold this conversation are a minority. Demonstrate it once and you'll be remembered.

# 跟 CFO 解释 AI 项目的成本结构

CFO 跟 engineer 完全不在一个语言系统里。这不是说 CFO 不聪明 — 大部分 CFO 比 engineer 更聪明,因为他们要在信息更少的情况下做更大的决定。问题是他们的脑子里装的是另一套术语和另一套关心的事。

跟 CFO 讲 AI 项目最常见的错: PM 一开口就讲"我们用的是 GPT-4o,context window 128k,大概每月调用量 50 万次,每千 token 多少钱…" — 这种讲法 CFO 听不到三句话就开始走神。**不是他听不懂,是他听不到他想听的东西**。

CFO 听任何 cost 的时候,脑子里在自动 sort 三件事:

1. 这个成本是**可预测**的吗 (predictability)
2. 这个成本能 **scale** 吗 (unit economics)
3. 这个成本算 **CapEx** 还是 **OpEx** (会计 / 现金流影响)

你讲 AI 成本的时候,要按这三件事讲。不是按"我们用了什么技术"讲。

---

## 决策点 1 — 第一句话怎么讲

CFO 给你的时间通常很短 (15-30 分钟,跟 T18.3 的 exec 会议一个量级)。第一句话不能是技术。第一句话要直接回答他脑子里那三个问题里至少一个。

**错的开场**

> "我们的 AI 系统主要用了 OpenAI 的 GPT-4o 模型,搭配了 RAG pipeline,vector DB 用的是 Pinecone,目前每月 API 调用量大概 …"

CFO 听到这里已经开始想"这个人是不是把我当 engineer"。

**对的开场**

> "我先给你一个 framing — 这个 AI 项目的成本结构,跟传统的 software 不一样。Software 的成本是前期一次性 CapEx + 维护;AI 的成本是**持续的、按使用量计费的 OpEx**。所以我下面讲数字的时候,你心里要切换到 OpEx 模式来听。"

这段话做了几件事:

- 你直接讲 CFO 关心的事 (CapEx vs OpEx)
- 你 frame 了一个 mental model 给他 (传统 software vs AI)
- 你没用一句技术术语
- 你给了他一个 instruction ("切换到 OpEx 模式") — CFO 喜欢这种主动给框架的人

如果 CFO 是 AI 这件事的 skeptic,加一句:

> "这个 OpEx 模型对你来说有一个好处,也有一个坏处。好处是不用承担传统 IT 投资的 CapEx 风险 — 项目失败你可以快速止血。坏处是这个成本一旦上量,会持续增长,需要从 day one 就 model 进 forecast。"

主动讲"也有坏处"是高级招。CFO 听惯了 vendor 讲好处不讲坏处,你主动讲坏处他立刻把你区分出来。

---

## 决策点 2 — 把 AI 成本拆成 CFO 能算的几块

CFO 不需要知道 token、context window、embedding 是什么。他需要知道**这个成本由什么组成、各占多少、哪些是固定的、哪些是变动的**。

**该拆的几块 (按 CFO 关心的优先级排)**

| 成本块 | 性质 | CFO 关心什么 |
|---|---|---|
| **Model API 调用费** | 变动成本,随用量 scale | 单位成本是多少?上量之后能不能 negotiate? |
| **数据 / 基础设施** (vector DB、storage、compute) | 半固定,有 baseline + 变动 | baseline 多少?边际成本曲线是怎样的? |
| **第三方工具 / SaaS** (eval 平台、observability、orchestration) | 大部分固定 | 几年合同?能不能砍掉? |
| **人** (PM、engineer、analyst、运维) | 固定,但是大头 | 项目跑下来人的成本占比多少? |
| **隐性成本** (合规、安全、re-training) | 不可预测,容易被低估 | 这一块占预算的多少 buffer? |

讲的时候,**人**和**隐性成本**这两块要专门强调 — 大部分 PM 跟 CFO 讲成本只讲 API 费,但实际项目里这两块加起来经常占 60-70%。CFO 如果只听到 API 费,他会以为项目很便宜,后面预算超了对你的信任度直接清零。

**讲法 (举例: 一个内部 AI 项目年度成本 100 万澳币的 breakdown)**

> "我把这个项目的年化成本拆给你看。Model API 调用费,大概 8 万 — 不到 10%。这个数字会随用量 scale,如果未来覆盖到全公司,大概会到 25-30 万。第二块基础设施和数据,大概 12 万,基本固定,scale 不会显著增加。第三块第三方工具,大概 10 万,是几年合同。第四块也是最大一块,人的成本,大概 60 万,这是 4-5 个 FTE 的全成本。最后,我留了 10 万作为合规和 unexpected 的 buffer,这一块我建议不要砍 — 后面会跟你解释为什么。"

这种讲法的好处:

- CFO 一秒钟内看到结构 (大头在哪)
- 你主动给了 scale assumption (8 万 → 25-30 万)
- 你 flag 了 buffer 而且 frame 成"不要砍" (preempt 谈判)
- 100% 用 CFO 的语言 (FTE、年化、scale)

---

## 决策点 3 — 解释为什么 AI 成本不能像传统 software 一样 forecast

这是 CFO 听完上面之后会问的第一个深入问题:**"为什么不能像传统 software 那样给我一个稳定的 forecast?"**

很多 PM 答不好这个问题,因为他们自己心里也没想清楚。

**该答的版本**

> "AI 成本的不确定性,主要有三个来源,我跟你 walk through:

> 第一,**单位成本本身在快速下降** — 同样的 task 一年前要花 X,现在花 X/3,这个趋势短期不会停。所以我的 forecast 里 model API 那一块,12 个月之后的数字大概率会比现在低,但具体低多少不知道。

> 第二,**用量曲线不线性**。AI 项目一旦内部用得起来,使用量会以 viral 的方式增长 — 不是 user 数量乘以平均用量,是 user 数量 × 每个 user 找到的新 use case 数量。这是为什么需要 buffer。

> 第三,**模型的版本迭代会触发 re-engineering 成本**。每 6-12 个月会有一个新版本出来,我们要不要切?切的话有迁移成本,不切的话有竞争力损失。这一块是 hardest to forecast 的。

> 综合起来 — 我给你的不是一个 point estimate,是一个 range。range 的中位数我比较有信心,边界我没那么有信心。你做预算的时候,我建议按 60-th percentile 锁定 budget,留 30% 的 flex,而不是按中位数锁。"

这段话讲完,CFO 多半会安静几秒钟,然后点头。理由:

- 你承认了不确定性 (CFO 最讨厌假装确定的人)
- 你把不确定性拆成了三块,而且每一块都有原因
- 你给了一个具体的预算建议 (60-th percentile + 30% flex)
- "60-th percentile" 这种说法直接 hit CFO 的财务术语

---

## 决策点 4 — 怎么讲 ROI / 价值

CFO 听完成本一定会问:"这个项目能省多少钱 / 赚多少钱?"

**最容易犯的错: 给一个虚的数字**

错: "我们估计这个项目可以为公司节省 200 万。"

CFO 立刻问:"怎么算的?"你答不上来,信任度清零。

**对的讲法**

不要给一个 single 数字。给一个**算式 + assumption + sensitivity**。

> "我给你的不是一个数字,是一个算式 — 你来 challenge 哪个 assumption 我们就调哪个。

> 算式: AI 处理一个 case 的时间是人的 1/N,我们公司一年处理 M 个 case,每个 case 的全成本是 X。所以 AI 替代的 value 是 M × X × (1 - 1/N) × adoption rate。

> 关键 assumption:
> - N = 5 (我们 PoC 测出来的)
> - M = 10 万 / 年 (你们自己的数字)
> - X = 80 澳币 / case (按 FTE 全成本算出来)
> - adoption rate = 60% (保守估计,因为不是所有 case 都适合)

> 算出来是 384 万的年化 value。

> 但 sensitivity 是这样: 如果 adoption 只到 30%,value 降到 192 万。如果 N 实际只有 3 (不是 5),value 再降 30%。所以这个项目的 floor 大概是 100 万出头,ceiling 是 380-400 万,中位估计 250 万左右。"

这种讲法的好处:

- 你没承诺一个数字 (没风险)
- 你把每个 assumption 都暴露了 (让 CFO 自己 stress test)
- 你给了 floor / ceiling / median (CFO 用这三个数字做决策)
- 整个对话从"你说能省多少" 变成"我们一起算能省多少" — 这是巨大的关系转变

**关于 ROI / payback period**

CFO 喜欢这两个数字。算出来直接讲:

> "按 floor 估计,这个项目 14 个月回本。按中位估计,8 个月。按 ceiling 5 个月。所以即使最 pessimistic 情况,这个项目 2 年期 IRR 也是正的。"

如果你不确定算法,**不要硬编**。直接说:

> "ROI 和 IRR 这种数字,我希望跟你的 finance team 一起算,而不是我自己拍一个 — 你们的 cost-of-capital 和 internal hurdle rate 我没有数据。可以约一下你们 finance 那边的人?"

把球扔回去,而且扔得专业。

## 决策点 5 — CFO 最容易 push back 的几个点和怎么答

**CFO push back 1: "为什么不直接买一个 SaaS 工具,要内部搭?"**

> "这是一个公平的问题。我们考虑过 SaaS 路径 — 主要 trade-off 是: SaaS 总成本可能 30-50% 高,但 implementation 时间短;内部搭成本低,但有 6-12 个月的 ramp up。决定哪一种更适合,主要看两个 factor: 一,我们的 use case 有没有 differentiation (有的话内部搭值得);二,这个 capability 是不是 strategic (如果 strategic 你不会想 outsource 给 SaaS 厂)。我的建议是 [一个具体的方案],但这一块我希望你来权衡。"

把决策权交给 CFO,而不是 defend 一个固定方案。

**CFO push back 2: "AI 价格在跌,我们等一年再做不行吗?"**

这是经典 CFO 问题。答错你就丢了项目。

> "价格是在跌,但等待的 cost 也是真的。我帮你算一下:如果我们今年开始,12 个月之后系统跑起来,假设这一年价值 200 万。如果我们等一年再开始,2027 年我们才有产出 — 等待的机会成本就是那 200 万。AI 单位成本就算降 50%,我们项目的成本里 model API 只占 10% 不到,降 50% 也就省 4 万 — 远不如等待的机会成本。"

这种讲法用 CFO 自己的逻辑 (NPV、机会成本) 反驳 CFO 自己。CFO 会服气。

**CFO push back 3: "为什么这个项目要 4-5 个 FTE 这么多人?"**

这是 CFO 试图砍预算的标准动作。

> "我理解这个 instinct。Headcount 在 AI 项目里最容易被低估 — 大家以为 AI 是工具,实际是一个需要 PM + engineer + data + 运维 + 业务对接的完整 stack。我可以给你看 industry benchmark — Anthropic 和 OpenAI 自己的 deployment team 跟客户的人员比大概是 1:3 到 1:5,我们 4-5 个人放在我们这个 scale 是 lean 的。但如果 budget 真的紧,我可以给你一个 3 人版的 plan — 但你要 accept 这个版本 6 个月的 timeline 会拖到 9-12 个月,而且 scope 要砍掉 [具体那一块]。"

给一个 reduced 版本,但同时 frame 砍人的 cost (timeline 拖长 + scope 砍)。让 CFO 自己决定值不值。**永远不要直接同意砍 headcount 而不讲 trade-off**。

**CFO push back 4: "这个项目失败的话,我们损失什么?"**

最难答的一题,因为 CFO 在评估 downside。**不要 minimise**,但要 frame 成可控:

> "失败有几种 mode。一种是技术上做不出来 — 这种我们在 PoC 阶段会发现,损失大概 [PoC 阶段的预算],150 万左右。第二种是做出来但 adoption 起不来 — 这种损失是 PoC + production 投入,大概 400-500 万。第三种是 reputational / governance 出问题 — 这种 financial 损失可能小,但 reputational 损失很难量化。我建议我们在 PoC 阶段就 set 一个 kill criteria — 如果 [具体指标] 没达到,我们直接停。这样 worst case 我们的 exposure 是 PoC 那一块,不是整个项目。"

主动 propose kill criteria 是高级招。CFO 看到你愿意自己 set 止损线,信任度立刻上升。

## 一些 do / don't 速记

**Do**

- 第一句话直接 frame CapEx vs OpEx
- 把成本拆成 5 块,人和隐性成本必须讲
- 给 range 不是 single 数字,带 sensitivity
- 用 floor / ceiling / median 讲价值
- 主动 propose kill criteria
- 让 CFO 自己 challenge assumption,不是 defend 一个固定数字

**Don't**

- 不要从技术细节讲起 (model、token、context window 之一秒掉粉)
- 不要给虚的 ROI 数字 ("大概节省 200 万")
- 不要 minimise downside (CFO 立刻不信你)
- 不要 defend 一个固定方案 — 给 trade-off,让他决定
- 不要不懂装懂 — 不会算 IRR 就直接说要跟 finance team 一起算
- 不要用一句话同时讨好 CFO 和 CTO (你在跟 CFO 讲话的时候就 100% 用 CFO 的语言)

## 话术速查:16 条跟 CFO 解释 AI 成本直接抄走用的句子

### 开场 frame

**1. 第一句话 frame CapEx vs OpEx**
> "我先给你一个 framing — AI 项目的成本结构跟传统 software 不一样。传统 software 是前期 CapEx + 维护;AI 是持续的、按用量计费的 OpEx。下面我讲数字的时候,你心里要切换到 OpEx 模式来听。"

**2. 跟 skeptic CFO 主动讲坏处**
> "这个 OpEx 模型对你来说有好处也有坏处。好处是不用承担传统 IT 的 CapEx 风险,项目失败可以快速止血。坏处是一旦上量,这个成本会持续增长,需要 day one 就 model 进 forecast。"

### 拆成本

**3. 5 块拆解 (举例)**
> "我把年化成本拆给你看: model API 大概 8 万,基础设施 12 万,第三方工具 10 万,人 60 万,合规和 buffer 10 万。大头在人 — 这是大部分 AI 项目预算里最被低估的一块。"

**4. 强调 buffer 不要砍**
> "我留了 10 万作为合规和 unexpected 的 buffer,我建议不要砍 — AI 项目里 governance 和 re-training 是真实成本,只是不显眼。"

### 解释不确定性

**5. 三个不确定性来源**
> "AI 成本不能像传统 software 一样 point estimate,主要三个原因: 单位成本在快速下降、用量曲线不线性、模型版本迭代会触发 re-engineering 成本。我给你的不是一个数字,是一个 range。"

**6. 推荐按 60-th percentile 锁预算**
> "你做预算的时候,我建议按 range 的 60-th percentile 锁定 budget,留 30% 的 flex,而不是按中位数锁。这样 worst case 你不会 surprise。"

### 讲 value

**7. 给算式不是数字**
> "我给你的不是一个数字,是一个算式 — 你来 challenge 哪个 assumption,我们就调哪个。算式是 [M × X × (1 - 1/N) × adoption]。关键 assumption 是 [...]。"

**8. 给 floor / ceiling / median**
> "Floor 大概 100 万,ceiling 380 万,中位估计 250 万。即使按 floor,这个项目 2 年期 IRR 也是正的。"

**9. 不会算 IRR 就直说**
> "ROI 和 IRR 这种数字,我希望跟你的 finance team 一起算,而不是我自己拍 — 你们的 cost-of-capital 和 hurdle rate 我没有数据。可以约一下你们 finance 那边的人?"

### Push back 应对

**10. 应对"为什么不买 SaaS"**
> "我们考虑过 SaaS — trade-off 是: SaaS 总成本可能 30-50% 高但快,内部搭便宜但有 6-12 个月 ramp up。哪个合适看两个 factor: differentiation 和 strategic-ness。我的建议是 [方案],但这一块希望你权衡。"

**11. 应对"等一年再做"**
> "等待的 cost 是真的。如果我们今年开始,12 个月后产出 200 万价值。等一年再开始,等待的机会成本就是那 200 万。AI 单位成本就算降 50%,model API 只占我们成本 10%,降 50% 也就省 4 万 — 远不如机会成本。"

**12. 应对"为什么要这么多人"**
> "AI 项目里 headcount 最容易被低估。Industry benchmark 是 deployment team 跟客户的人员比 1:3 到 1:5,我们 4-5 个人是 lean 的。如果 budget 真的紧,我可以给你一个 3 人版的 plan — 但 timeline 会从 6 个月拖到 9-12 个月,scope 砍掉 [那一块]。你来决定值不值。"

**13. 应对"失败的话损失什么"**
> "失败有几种 mode: 技术做不出 (PoC 阶段发现,150 万)、adoption 起不来 (400-500 万)、reputational (难量化)。我建议 PoC 阶段就 set kill criteria — 如果 [指标] 没达到我们直接停。Worst case exposure 是 PoC,不是整个项目。"

### 主动 propose

**14. 主动 propose kill criteria**
> "我建议在 PoC 阶段就跟你 align 一个 kill criteria — 如果 [具体指标] 在 [时间] 之前没达到,我们 stop。这样你的 downside 是 capped 的。"

**15. 主动 propose budget cadence**
> "我建议预算按 quarterly review 来 release,而不是 annual 一次性批 — 这样你每季度有一次重新评估的机会,而我每季度有一次 demonstrate 价值的机会。"

### 不知道就直说

**16. CFO 问的某个 finance 问题你答不上**
> "这个我现在没法给你一个有依据的答案 — 我想跟你的 finance team 一起算,这样数字是你 trust 的。我会后约一下你那边的 [具体 role]。"

---

往大了说,CFO 不是你的敌人 — CFO 是这个项目最重要的盟友之一。他批不批预算决定项目能不能跑、跑多久、scale 不 scale 得到。

但 CFO 的语言系统跟你的语言系统不一样。他不需要变成你 — 你要主动切换到他的语言。具体来说就是:**CapEx vs OpEx、unit economics、predictability、ROI、payback、IRR、kill criteria、sensitivity** 这些词,你要用得跟他一样自然。

一旦他感觉"这个 PM 讲我能听懂的话",你后面要 budget、要 headcount、要 escalation,都会顺很多。AI 这一行,会跟 CFO 对话的 PM 是 minority。Demonstrate 一次,你就会被记住。
