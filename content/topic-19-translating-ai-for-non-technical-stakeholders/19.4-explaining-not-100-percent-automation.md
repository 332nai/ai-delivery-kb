# Explaining why 100% automation isn't the goal — to business users

The business team's default expectation of AI is 100% automation.

That isn't them being unreasonable — that's how they buy things. When the company decides to put in an AI system, the picture in the business team's head is: what 10 people used to do, now 1 person + AI can do. The budget is built on that ROI; the KPIs are set against that target.

Then you come to them and say: this system needs human-in-the-loop, certain cases can't ship without human review. Their first reaction is: **"So we still need those 10 people? Where's the ROI?"**

This conversation is a real test — because you can't let the business team get disappointed enough to walk away from the project, and you can't pretend "yes yes 100% automation, all good", or you're dead the first time production has an incident.

The business team is different from the sponsor: the sponsor might be far from day-to-day, you educate them once and it's fine; the business team is actually using this system every day. What they care about isn't a mental model — it's **whether their own day-to-day gets better or worse**.

Below.

---

## Decision 1 — First time talking to the business team, the first 3 sentences

The business team's pushback on "not 100% automation" is half-rooted in the suspicion that you're walking back the original promise. So the first thing your opener needs to do isn't explain the technology — it's **acknowledge their expectation was reasonable**.

**Wrong opener**

> "I want to set some expectations — AI systems actually can't be 100% automated, we need human-in-the-loop..."

The business team hears "set expectations" and "can't", and immediately goes defensive. That word combination = "I'm about to walk back what I promised you".

**Right opener**

> "I know when you bought into this project, you were expecting to reduce [the specific workload]. I want to walk through how we get there today — but with a nuance up front: 'reduce workload' and '100% automation' aren't the same thing, and the difference will shape how you use this system after launch."

What this does:

- You've acknowledged their original expectation (it was reasonable)
- You haven't said "can't do 100%", you've said "100% and reducing workload aren't the same thing"
- You've moved the conversation from "can I deliver" to "how do we define success"
- Not a single piece of jargon

After this, the business team usually leans in rather than back — they feel you're not backtracking, just reframing.

---

## Decision 2 — Reframe with the 80/15/5 split

The default in the business team's head is "AI replaces 100% of work" vs "AI replaces 0% of work". That binary is wrong. You give them a new mental model: **AI replaces some percentage, humans handle the rest, but humans handle it differently than before**.

**How to frame it**

> "Let me paint a picture.

> Today, 100% of cases go through [current process — e.g. manual handling]. The system we're designing isn't AI replacing 100%. It's this:

> - **80% of cases** — AI handles directly, no human involvement. That's true automation.
> - **15% of cases** — AI gives an answer but flags 'I'm not fully sure'. Human reviews, mostly clicks approve, 30 seconds each.
> - **5% of cases** — AI knows it can't handle it, escalates to a human, who handles it the old way.

> You used to have 100 people handling 100% of cases. Under the new system, human work becomes: don't touch the 80%, quick-review the 15%, deep-handle the 5%. Total human work shrinks to about 25% of original.

> So the ROI isn't 'from 100 people to 0', it's **'from 100 people to 25 + an AI system'**. That's a real, deliverable ROI. '100 to 0' is the ROI that will disappoint you three months in."

Why this works:

- Concrete percentages (80/15/5) make the abstract concrete
- ROI quantified (100 people → 25 + AI)
- Framing "deliverable" vs "will disappoint you" lets the business team pick — most pick deliverable
- You haven't said "we can't do 100%", you've said "100% is the wrong framing"

**The business team will ask**

> "Why can't it be 100%? What's that 5%?"

Don't give a vague answer. **Be specific about what the 5% is.**

> "That 5% is roughly three kinds of cases:

> One, **genuinely complex cases** — touching multiple systems, multiple exceptions, requiring case-by-case judgement. AI is unlikely to produce a confident answer.

> Two, **high-stakes cases** — where the cost of an error is high enough that even if AI got it right, governance requires human review (e.g. large refunds, legal disputes, regulator reporting).

> Three, **edge cases** — case types AI hasn't seen, e.g. a new product, a recent policy change. AI catches up over time, but there will always be new edge cases. This bucket never goes to zero.

> That 5% of cases usually accounts for 25-30% of total work time — because these cases take longer to handle. So even if AI automates 95% of case count, human work time still sits at around 30%. This belongs in your ROI math."

Proactively quantifying "case count vs work time" — this is the distinction the business team most easily conflates, and you preempt it.

---

## Decision 3 — Explaining why human review is a feature, not a bug

When the business team hears "human review", their first reaction is "so the AI isn't reliable enough". This is the misunderstanding. You proactively reframe — **human review isn't a fallback, it's a design choice**.

**How to frame it**

> "Let me reset the term 'human review'.

> What you might be thinking: human review = AI isn't good enough = project isn't done yet.

> In reality, production-grade AI systems treat human review as a feature, not a bug. Three reasons:

> **Reason one: trust building**. If an AI system goes 100% automated from day one with no human oversight, the first incident wipes out all trust overnight. A system with human review builds trust gradually, which is more robust.

> **Reason two: regulation / compliance**. In [specific industry — finance / healthcare / legal / HR], certain types of decisions cannot be fully automated by law. This isn't a technical limit, it's a legal requirement. Human-in-the-loop is by design, not by limitation.

> **Reason three: continuous learning**. The corrections that come out of human review are the fuel for the system to keep improving. Without humans reviewing, the system has no way of knowing which cases it's getting wrong, and quality plateaus.

> So '100% automation' is actually an anti-pattern — not because we can't, but because we shouldn't."

That last line — "not because we can't, but because we shouldn't" — is the core of the reframe. It turns "can't do it" into "deliberately don't". The business team's cognitive frame on this is completely different after they hear it.

**The business team's pushback**

> "But I'm still paying for those 25 people. The headcount cost isn't actually saved."

Fair pushback, don't dodge.

> "You're right — headcount cost doesn't go to zero. This is the most over-sold thing about AI projects. But a few nuances:

> One, **the work content levels up**. The original 100 people were doing 80% routine + 20% complex. Under the new system, those 25 people are doing 100% complex. Attrition goes down (routine work is draining), hiring quality can go up (more challenging cases).

> Two, **headcount cost isn't just salary**. Hiring, training, turnover, management overhead are all cost. Cut the team size in half, those hidden costs drop significantly.

> Three, **capacity elasticity**. With 100 people, you can't hire fast enough when volume spikes. With 25 + AI, AI scales infinitely; humans only handle the 5% complex tail — much more elastic.

> I can run a three-year TCO for you. You'll see — 'headcount cost didn't drop' is only true in year one. Cumulative three-year TCO drops [specific percentage] significantly."

Volunteering "year one looks the same" actually makes the business team trust you more.

---

## Decision 4 — The business team's real worry: their own job

On the surface, the business team is discussing ROI. **Underneath, there's an unspoken question in their head**: **"Will I be replaced by AI?"**

This worry won't be raised openly, but it runs through the whole conversation. If you don't surface it, it will quietly undermine their cooperation throughout the project.

**How to proactively address it (without over-promising)**

In a 1:1 — not a group meeting — with the business lead:

> "I want to be honest about one thing. A lot of people on your team are quietly thinking about where they sit after this AI launches. Let me share the pattern I've seen from similar projects:

> What doesn't happen: mass layoffs after AI launches. One, companies generally don't do this (PR disaster); two, complex cases still need these people.

> What does happen: the team's skill mix shifts. Routine work shrinks, complex / judgement / exception-handling grows. The people best at handling complex problems become more important; the people most skilled at routine work need to reskill.

> What I'd recommend: align with HR and leadership early on a transition plan — not because of fear of layoffs, but to give the team a career development path. If you don't raise this proactively, morale becomes a problem once AI launches."

Reframing "will the team be replaced" as "how will the team transition" gives the business team a constructive angle, rather than empty reassurance.

---

## Decision 5 — Demonstrate rather than explain

The business team is ultimately persuaded **not because you explained it well, but because they used it and it felt good**.

So once the verbal explanation is done, get the demo in front of them quickly. A demo beats explanation 100x.

**How to design a demo that actually works**

| Bad demo | Good demo |
|---|---|
| Cherry-picked perfect cases | Deliberately mix in 2-3 imperfect cases so the business team sees how the system handles non-perfect |
| AI auto-ships, human review process invisible | Full flow: AI proposes → confidence flag → human review UI → ship. Show whether review is 30 seconds or 5 minutes |
| PM speaks after the demo | Hand the keyboard over and let the business team try it themselves for 5 minutes |
| One case shown over and over | 5 cases run in sequence — let them see throughput |

**The question to ask after the demo**

> "Now that you've watched that — gut feel, after this system ships, will your day-to-day be better or worse than now?"

This question gets the business team to articulate their actual feelings. Don't ask "is the accuracy good enough" — they can't judge that. Ask what they can judge: **their own day**.

---

## A short do / don't

**Do**

- Acknowledge the business team's original expectation as reasonable
- Use the 80/15/5 split with concrete percentages
- Quantify "100 people → 25 + AI"
- Reframe human review as feature, not bug
- Address the "will my team be replaced" worry 1:1, privately
- Replace explanation with demo
- Mix imperfect cases into the demo deliberately

**Don't**

- Don't say "set expectations" or "can't do 100%"
- Don't be vague about what the 5% is — name the cases
- Don't address "team replacement" anxiety in a group meeting
- Don't pretend headcount cost goes to zero
- Don't run a cherry-picked demo
- Don't let the business team only watch — make them try
- Don't pretend "once they use it they'll love it"

---

Stepping back: the business team isn't like the CFO or legal. CFO and legal care about abstract risk and numbers. The business team cares about **their own daily life**.

The biggest mistake when talking AI to business users is trying to persuade them with abstract argument. Abstract argument works on the sponsor, the CFO, and legal — but it doesn't work here. They intuitively know they only find out whether they like it once they use it.

So your communication with the business team isn't verbal — it's **setting up an environment where they can directly experience it, and letting them update their own belief**. The verbal part is just for setting the frame, so they don't walk into the demo with the wrong expectation.

The moment a business user becomes an advocate for an AI system isn't after you've finished explaining. It's the second they use it and think "my day got easier". From that second onwards, they become the strongest ally on the project.

# 跟客户业务方解释为什么不能 100% 自动化

业务方对 AI 的默认预期是 100% 自动化。

这不是他们 unreasonable — 这是他们买东西的方式。当公司决定上一个 AI 系统,业务方头脑里的画面是: 之前 10 个人做的事,现在 1 个人 + AI 就能做。预算就是按这个 ROI 拍的,KPI 就是按这个目标定的。

然后你来跟他们讲:这个系统要 human-in-the-loop,某些 case AI 不能直接 ship,要人 review。业务方第一反应是:**"那不就还是要那 10 个人吗?ROI 在哪?"**

这场对话非常考验你 — 因为你既不能让业务方失望到放弃项目,也不能假装"会的会的100%自动化",否则 production 出 incident 你死定了。

业务方跟 sponsor 还不一样: sponsor 可能离 day-to-day 远,你 educate 一下就行;业务方是真的每天用这个系统的人,他们关心的不是 mental model,是**他们自己的日子会变好还是变难**。

下面拆开讲。

---

## 决策点 1 — 第一次跟业务方讲这件事,前 3 句话

业务方对"不能 100% 自动化"的反感,一半来自他们以为你在 walk back 当初的承诺。所以你的开场要做的第一件事不是解释技术,是**承认他们的预期是 reasonable**。

**错的开场**

> "我想跟你们 set 一下 expectation — AI 系统其实没办法做到 100% 自动化,我们需要 human-in-the-loop …"

业务方听到"set expectation"和"没办法",立刻进入 defensive。这个词组合 = "我要 walk back 当初承诺"。

**对的开场**

> "我知道你们当初买这个项目,期待的是减少 [具体那个工作量] 这件事。我今天想跟你们 walk through 怎么实现这个目标 — 但我要先说一个 nuance: '减少工作量'跟'100% 自动化'不是一件事,而且差别在哪里,会决定你们后面用这个系统的方式。"

这段话做了几件事:

- 你 acknowledge 了他们的原始预期 (是 reasonable 的)
- 你没说"不能 100% 自动化",你说"100% 自动化跟减少工作量不是一件事"
- 你把对话从"我能不能 deliver" 转到"我们怎么定义成功"
- 你没用一句技术 jargon

业务方听完通常会 lean in 而不是 lean back,因为他们感觉你没在 backtrack,只是在 reframe。

---

## 决策点 2 — 用 80/20 split 来 reframe

业务方脑子里的 default 是 "AI 替代 100% 工作" vs "AI 替代 0% 工作"。这个 binary 是错的。你要给他们一个新的 mental model: **AI 替代某个 percentage,人处理剩下的部分,但人处理的方式跟以前不一样**。

**讲法**

> "我给你画一个 picture。

> 你们现在 100% 的 case 都靠 [现有方式 — 比如人工处理]。我们 design 的系统不是让 AI 100% 替代,是这样:

> - **80% 的 case** — AI 直接 handle,人不参与。这是真的自动化。
> - **15% 的 case** — AI 给一个 answer,但 flag 出来'我不太 sure',人 review 一下,大部分情况点 approve 就行,30 秒一个。
> - **5% 的 case** — AI 知道自己处理不了,直接 escalate 给人,人按以前的方式处理。

> 你们原来 100 个人处理 100% 的 case。新系统下,人的工作变成: 不处理那 80%,quick-review 那 15%,深度处理那 5%。这一块工作的总量大概是原来的 25%。

> 所以 ROI 不是 '从 100 人减到 0 人',是 **'从 100 人减到 25 人 + AI 系统'**。这是真实的、可 deliver 的 ROI,而 '100 人减到 0 人' 是一个会让你们三个月之后失望的 ROI。"

这种讲法的好处:

- 用具体百分比 (80/15/5) 让 abstract 变具体
- 主动量化 ROI ('100 人 → 25 人 + AI')
- 用 "可 deliver" vs "会让你们失望" 这个 frame 让业务方自己选 — 大部分人会选 deliverable
- 没说"做不到 100%",而是说"100% 是个错的 framing"

**业务方一定会问**

> "为什么不能 100%?那 5% 的 case 是什么?"

不要 vague answer。**具体讲那 5% 是什么**:

> "那 5% 大致是几种 case:

> 一,**真的复杂的 case** — 涉及多个 system、多个 exception、需要 case-by-case judgement 的。这种 AI 大概率给不出有把握的答案。

> 二,**high stakes 的 case** — 出错的 cost 太高,即使 AI 答对了,governance 也要求人 review (比如涉及大额 refund、法律纠纷、监管 report)。

> 三,**edge case** — AI 没见过的 case type,比如新出的产品、新的 policy 变化。这一块 AI 需要一段时间 catch up,但永远会有新的 edge case 出现,所以这个 bucket 永远不会归零。

> 这 5% 的 case 通常占总工作量的 25-30% — 因为这种 case 处理时间长。所以即使 AI 自动化了 95% 的 case 数量,人需要处理的工作时间还有 30% 左右。这一块要算进 ROI。"

主动量化 "case 数量 vs 工作时间" 的区别 — 这是业务方最容易混淆的一点,你提前讲清楚。

---

## 决策点 3 — 解释为什么 human review 是 feature 不是 bug

业务方听到 "human review" 这个词,第一反应是"那就是 AI 不够 reliable"。这是误解。你要主动 reframe — **human review 不是 fallback,是 design**。

**讲法**

> "我想 reset 一下 'human review' 这个词。

> 你们脑子里可能在想: human review = AI 还不够好 = 项目还没完成。

> 实际上 production-grade 的 AI 系统 design 里,human review 不是 bug 是 feature。原因有三个:

> **原因一: 信任建立**。如果一个 AI 系统从 day one 100% 自动化、没有人 oversight,出第一个 incident 之后,所有信任会一夜清零。带 human review 的系统是逐步建立信任的,所以更 robust。

> **原因二: 监管 / 合规**。在 [具体行业 — 金融 / 医疗 / 法律 / HR] 里,某些类型的决策 by law 不能完全自动化。这不是技术限制,是法律要求。我们 design human-in-the-loop 是 by design,不是 by limitation。

> **原因三: 持续学习**。Human review 出来的 correction 是我们 system 持续 improve 的 fuel。如果完全没有人 review,system 永远没办法知道自己在哪些 case 上做错了,质量会 plateau。

> 所以 '100% 自动化' 其实是一个 anti-pattern — 不是 because we can't, 是 because we shouldn't。"

最后那句 "not because we can't, because we shouldn't" 是 reframe 的核心。把"做不到"变成"故意不做"。业务方听完之后 cognitive frame 就完全不一样了。

**业务方会 push back**

> "但是这样我还是要养着那 25 个人,人力成本没省下来啊。"

这是合理的 push back,不要回避。

> "你说得对 — 人力成本不会归零,这是项目最常被 oversell 的一点。但有几个 nuance:

> 一,**人的工作内容会升级**。原来 100 个人做的是 80% routine + 20% complex,新系统下这 25 个人做的全是 complex。这一块 attrition 会下降 (因为 routine 工作累人),hiring quality 可以更高 (因为 case 更有 challenge)。

> 二,**人的 cost 不只是 salary**。Hiring、training、turnover、management overhead 都是 cost。规模缩小一倍多,这些隐性 cost 都下降。

> 三,**capacity 上的弹性**。原来 100 个人,业务量 spike 的时候 hire 不及。新系统下 25 个人 + AI,AI 可以无限 scale,人只 handle 5% complex case,弹性大得多。

> 我可以帮你算一下三年的 TCO,你会看到 — 'people-cost 没省下来' 这种说法,只在第一年看是对的。三年累计 TCO 大概 [具体百分比] 是显著下降的。"

主动暴露"第一年看不出来"这个 nuance,反而让业务方信任你。

---

## 决策点 4 — 业务方真正的担忧:他们自己的工作

业务方表面上在讨论 ROI,**实际上他们脑子里有一个不说出来的问题**: **"我自己会不会被 AI 替代?"**

这个担忧不会公开讲,但贯穿整个对话。你不点破,这个担忧会一直影响他们对项目的合作。

**怎么主动 address (而不显得过度承诺)**

在 1-on-1 而不是 group meeting 上,跟业务方的 lead 单独说:

> "我想跟你 honest 讲一件事。你们 team 里很多人心里在想这个 AI 上线之后他们的位置怎么样。我可以 share 我从其他类似项目看到的 pattern:

> 不会发生的: AI 上线之后大规模裁员。这种事一,公司一般不会这样做 (PR 灾难); 二,实际工作里复杂 case 还需要这些人。

> 会发生的: 团队的 skill mix 会变。Routine work 减少,complex / judgement / exception handling 的工作增加。意味着 team 里最适合处理复杂问题的人会变得更重要,而处理 routine 最熟练的人需要 reskill。

> 我建议的: 你跟 HR 和 leadership 早一点 align 一个 transition plan — 不是怕裁员,是给团队一个职业发展路径。这件事如果你不主动 raise,等 AI 上线之后 morale 会成问题。"

把"团队会不会被替代"reframe 成"团队怎么 transition" — 这是给业务方一个建设性的 take,而不是空洞的安慰。

---

## 决策点 5 — 怎么 demonstrate 而不是 explain

业务方最终会被说服,**不是因为你讲得清楚,是因为他们自己用了之后感觉好**。

所以一旦 verbal explanation 做完,你要尽快把 demo 拉过来。Demo 比 explanation 强 100 倍。

**Demo 怎么 design 才有效果**

| Bad demo | Good demo |
|---|---|
| 用 cherry-picked perfect case | 故意 mix 进 2-3 个 imperfect case,让业务方看到 system 怎么 handle 不 perfect |
| AI 自动 ship,看不出 human review 流程 | 全流程 show: AI propose → confidence flag → human review interface → ship。让业务方看到 review 是 30 秒还是 5 分钟 |
| Demo 完 PM 讲 | Demo 完让业务方自己上手试 5 分钟 |
| 用一个 case 反复讲 | 用 5 个 case 一气 demo,让业务方看到 throughput |

**Demo 之后必问的问题**

> "你刚才看完,你直觉觉得这个系统 ship 之后,你 day-to-day 会比现在好还是难?"

这个问题让业务方说出他们真实的感受。不要问"你觉得 accuracy 够不够"这种他们没办法判断的问题。问他们能判断的:**他们自己的日子**。

---

## 一些 do / don't 速记

**Do**

- 第一句话 acknowledge 业务方的原始预期是 reasonable
- 用 80/15/5 split 给具体百分比
- 主动量化 "100 人 → 25 人 + AI"
- 把 human review reframe 成 feature 不是 bug
- 1-on-1 私下 address "团队会不会被替代"的担忧
- 用 demo 替代 explanation
- 故意 mix imperfect case 进 demo

**Don't**

- 不要说 "set expectation" 或 "没办法 100%"
- 不要 vague 描述那 5% — 要具体讲是什么 case
- 不要在 group meeting 处理"团队替代"焦虑
- 不要假装"人力成本会归零"
- 不要用 cherry-picked demo
- 不要 demo 完只让业务方"看",要让他们上手试
- 不要假装"用了就会爱上"

## 话术速查:15 条跟业务方解释 AI 直接抄走用的句子

### 开场

**1. Acknowledge 原始预期**
> "我知道你们当初买这个项目,期待的是减少 [具体那个工作量]。我今天想 walk through 怎么实现这个目标 — 但要先说一个 nuance: '减少工作量' 跟 '100% 自动化' 不是一件事,差别在哪里会决定你们后面用这个系统的方式。"

### 80/15/5 framework

**2. 介绍 80/15/5**
> "我们 design 的系统不是 AI 替代 100%。是: 80% case AI 直接 handle、15% case AI 给答案 + 人 quick-review 30 秒、5% case AI escalate 给人。你们的工作从 100% 处理 case,变成 quick-review + 深度处理 complex。"

**3. 量化 ROI**
> "原来 100 个人 100% case,新系统下大概 25 个人 + AI。ROI 不是 '100 减到 0',是 '100 减到 25'。这是可 deliver 的,而 '100 减到 0' 会让你们三个月之后失望。"

### 解释 5% 是什么

**4. 拆开 5% 的三种 case**
> "那 5% 大致三种: 真的复杂 case (需要多 system / case-by-case judgement)、high stakes case (出错 cost 高,governance 要求人 review)、edge case (AI 没见过的)。Edge case bucket 永远不会归零。"

**5. 解释 case 数 vs 时间**
> "5% case 通常占总工作时间 25-30% — 因为这种 case 处理时间长。即使 AI 自动化 95% case 数量,人需要处理的工作时间还有 30% 左右。这一块要算进 ROI。"

### Human review 是 feature

**6. Reset "human review" 的含义**
> "Human review 不是 bug 是 feature。三个原因: 信任建立 (逐步比一步到位更 robust)、监管合规 (某些决策 by law 不能完全自动化)、持续学习 (review 出来的 correction 是 system 改进的 fuel)。"

**7. 关键 reframe**
> "100% 自动化是 anti-pattern — not because we can't, but because we shouldn't。"

### 应对 "人力成本没省下来"

**8. 工作内容升级**
> "原来 100 人做 80% routine + 20% complex,新系统下 25 人做 100% complex。Attrition 下降 (routine 累人),hiring quality 可以更高 (case 更 challenge)。"

**9. 隐性成本**
> "人的 cost 不只是 salary — hiring、training、turnover、management overhead 都是。规模缩一半多,这些隐性 cost 都下降。"

**10. 弹性 capacity**
> "原来 100 人 spike 的时候 hire 不及。新系统 25 人 + AI,AI 无限 scale,人只 handle 5% complex,弹性大得多。"

**11. 主动暴露第一年看不出来**
> "我帮你算一下三年 TCO — '人力成本没省下来' 这个说法,只在第一年看是对的。三年累计 TCO 显著下降。"

### Address 团队焦虑 (1-on-1 use)

**12. 主动开场 (单独)**
> "想跟你 honest 讲一件事 — 你们 team 里很多人心里在想 AI 上线之后他们的位置。我可以 share 我从类似项目看到的 pattern。"

**13. 不会发生 vs 会发生**
> "不会发生的: 大规模裁员 — 一,公司一般不会 (PR 灾难); 二,复杂 case 还需要这些人。会发生的: skill mix 会变,routine 减少 complex 增加。"

**14. 建议 transition plan**
> "建议你跟 HR 和 leadership 早一点 align 一个 transition plan — 不是怕裁员,是给团队一个职业发展路径。不主动 raise 的话,AI 上线后 morale 会成问题。"

### Demo 之后

**15. 问业务方真实感受**
> "你刚才看完,直觉觉得这个系统 ship 之后,你 day-to-day 会比现在好还是难?"

---

往大了说,业务方跟 CFO 和法务不一样 — CFO 和法务关心的是抽象的 risk 和 number,业务方关心的是**他们自己每天的生活**。

跟业务方讲 AI,最大的错误是用 abstract argument 说服他们。Abstract argument 在 sponsor 那里有效,在 CFO 那里有效,在法务那里有效,但在业务方那里没用 — 因为他们直觉知道,只有他们用了之后才知道好不好。

所以跟业务方的 communication 不是 verbal,是 **set up 一个让他们能直接 experience 的环境,然后让他们自己 update belief**。Verbal 部分只用来 set frame,让他们不会用错的预期走进 demo。

业务方愿意 advocate 一个 AI 系统的瞬间,不是听完你讲解之后,是用过之后觉得"我的日子好过了"那一秒。从那一秒开始,他们会变成项目最强的盟友。
