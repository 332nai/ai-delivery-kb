# Explaining prompt injection, data leakage, and IP risk to legal

Legal hears risk the opposite way an engineer hears risk.

When an engineer talks risk, they say: "this could happen, but the probability is low, and we have mitigation". Legal hears that sentence and **only registers the "this could happen" part**. The mitigation and the probability caveat are filtered out. That isn't a fault — it's their training. Legal's job is to imagine worst case and find a way to lock worst case in a drawer.

When you talk AI risk to legal, you have to reframe how you speak. A few core shifts:

| Engineer's framing | What legal can actually hear |
|---|---|
| "Probability is low" | Legal doesn't care about probability — they care about "if it happens, what do we do" |
| "We have mitigation" | "If mitigation fails, what's the fallback?" |
| "This is a technical problem, we handle it" | "This is a governance problem, here's the policy we propose" |
| "This is rare" | "Rare but not zero, so we plan for it" |

A few common AI risk topics and how to walk them through, below.

---

## Decision 1 — First meeting with legal, the first 5 minutes

In your first meeting with legal, your goal isn't "get approval". It's "don't get them to hit the stop button".

A lot of PMs in their first conversation with legal try to sell — how cool the AI is, how valuable, how transformative. **That framing is a negative signal to legal**. The first thing they register when they hear enthusiasm is: this person isn't taking risk seriously, time to be on guard.

**The right opener**

> "[Name], I'm not here to sell you on the project — the sponsor has already signed off. I'm here to walk you through the risks I've already identified, how I'm planning to manage them, and get your input. If you see risks I've missed, please raise them."

What this does:

- You're not selling (legal exhales)
- You've already identified risks (you take it seriously)
- You're inviting them to find risks you missed (their expertise is respected)
- You're framing this as collaborative, not as approval-seeking

**The trap question legal often opens with**

> "Before we start — has this been deployed already?"

This is a trap. If you say "deployed", legal feels you've gone around them, and their guard goes up immediately. **The right answer**:

> "Not in production yet. We're in PoC, running in [specific environment, usually an isolated internal one]. I'm coming to you deliberately ahead of deploy, because I want your input designed into the production version, not bolted on after."

That sentence — "I'm coming to you deliberately ahead of deploy" — is crucial. Legal's biggest fear is being bypassed. You volunteer that you haven't bypassed them, and they immediately disarm.

---

## Decision 2 — Explaining prompt injection

Prompt injection is one of the technical risks most likely to make legal anxious in an AI project, because it touches unauthorised access, data exfiltration, and system manipulation — concepts legal already knows from traditional IT.

**The wrong framing**

> "Prompt injection is when someone uses a carefully crafted prompt to manipulate the LLM's behaviour into doing something the designer didn't intend. We use input sanitisation, output filtering, and dual model verification to defend against it..."

Legal hears "manipulate", "unintended", "defend" and their anxiety hits 7/10. Then you launch into technical mitigation, and they stay at 7/10.

**The right framing**

Don't lead with technical detail. Lead with **"if it happens, what's the worst case"**.

> "Prompt injection is a class of attack vector — in plain language, an attacker crafts an input that gets the AI system to do something the designer doesn't want it to do. In our project, worst case if prompt injection occurs is [specific description: e.g. an unauthorised user retrieves data they shouldn't see / the AI gives a response that breaches company policy / internal systems are reached indirectly via the AI].

> I manage this risk in two layers:

> Layer one is technical mitigation — input sanitisation, output filtering, and in critical paths we use dual model verification. This layer blocks most attacks.

> Layer two — and this is the layer more important to you in legal — is **post-incident response**. If mitigation fails, the SOP we're proposing is: [a specific response plan, including who owns it, escalation timing, customer communication template].

> What I want to walk you through isn't the tech — it's the SOP. Technical defence can never be 100%. Governance-layer response capability is what we can both actually sign off on."

What this does:

- Makes "worst case" concrete (legal can evaluate it)
- Splits mitigation into two layers; layer one in passing, layer two (governance) in detail
- Proactively says "technical defence can never be 100%" (legal hears honesty, trust goes up)
- Frames legal's focus as the SOP, not the technology
- "Both sign off on" — collaborative

**Legal's most common follow-up**

> "If an incident happens, what's our disclosure obligation?"

This is a legal question, not a technical one. **Don't fake knowing.**

> "Disclosure obligation I'd want you to lead on — in Australia we're touching the Privacy Act and the OAIC's notifiable data breach framework, and how those map onto an AI incident is your area, not mine. What I can provide is the technical fact pattern of any incident; you map fact pattern to obligation. Do we want to set up an incident playbook in advance, with the fact-pattern-to-obligation mapping written out?"

Pass the ball back deliberately, framing it as "your expertise". Legal feels respected, and is willing to lead.

---

## Decision 3 — Explaining data leakage

Data leakage in AI projects comes in two flavours. Legal tends to conflate them on first hearing, so you split them yourself:

**Type 1: Training data leakage**

Internal data is used to train the model, and the model (especially a hosted closed-source model) may "memorise" that data and surface it later in another user's query.

**Type 2: Inference-time data leakage**

When you send internal data to an external model provider (OpenAI, Anthropic etc.) via API, the data is at risk in-transit or while in the vendor's systems.

**How to talk through it**

> "Data leakage risk in AI projects has two flavours — I want to walk through them separately, because the mitigations differ.

> First is training data leakage. We use internal data to fine-tune the model, and in theory the model 'memorises' that data and could surface it in an unrelated query later. Our mitigation: we don't use OpenAI's or Anthropic's fine-tune service (we can't verify their isolation), only open-source models we self-host. That keeps the fine-tuned weights inside our environment.

> Second is inference-time leakage. When we run a query against a closed-source model, the data goes to the OpenAI / Anthropic API endpoint. Mitigation: one, an enterprise agreement with zero data retention clauses; two, for the most sensitive data, a self-hosted model — data never leaves.

> The legal implications differ — type 1 is more IP / trade secret, type 2 is more privacy / personal data. I'd like your eyes on whether the SOP we've drafted gives sufficient coverage on both."

Why this works:

- You split them yourself (legal usually doesn't; when you do it for them, they think you're thoughtful)
- Each has its mitigation, then you ask "is the coverage sufficient" — handing the judgement back to legal
- "Enterprise agreement", "zero data retention" — contract vocabulary they speak

**Legal's most common follow-up: "Can we just not use any external API?"**

Don't immediately defend. Acknowledge first:

> "Valid question. Not using external APIs at all is technically feasible — we replace with self-hosted models — but the trade-off is [model quality drops / certain use cases become impossible / cost goes up]. My preference is mixed: high-sensitivity data on self-hosted, everything else on vendor + enterprise agreement. But this is a risk-tolerance call — you set the threshold, we design the architecture. I can show you a few options, you pick."

Let legal set the risk threshold. This is an advanced move — legal has always been "the people who judge risk", never "the people who are told what the risk is".

---

## Decision 4 — Explaining IP risk

IP risk in AI projects comes from two angles, and legal usually worries about each in turn:

**Angle 1: IP ownership of AI-generated content**

Who owns what the AI generates? If the customer prompted it, does the customer own it? If the model provider's ToS says "you own the output", how is "own" enforceable across jurisdictions?

**Angle 2: Third-party IP used in training / inference**

The model was trained on copyrighted content from the internet (the model provider's doing). Does the content we generate using that model carry derivative work risk?

**How to talk through it**

> "Two main IP risk angles I'm seeing — let me walk through both.

> First is ownership of AI-generated content. We're using OpenAI / Anthropic, and both their enterprise ToS clearly state customer owns the output. But in edge cases (e.g. the output reproduces a copyrighted passage from training data), that "own" may sit in a grey zone. Our mitigation: we don't use AI to generate final-form external content (e.g. articles for publication), only drafts that go through human review before going out. That keeps the ownership chain at human → company, with AI as tool.

> Second is the derivative work risk on training data. In theory this is the model provider's responsibility — what they ingested when training is outside our control. In practice, if the AI's output is highly similar to a copyrighted source, we as the downstream user can still be dragged into the litigation. Mitigation: for higher-risk use cases (creative, marketing content) we run similarity checks against a plagiarism detection layer. I'd like your eyes on whether the SOP here is strong enough.

> One angle I haven't fully worked out: if model providers (OpenAI) get sued over training data in the future, what's our downstream user exposure? That's your area, not mine."

That last line — "haven't fully worked out" — is a senior PM move. Volunteering a specific legal question you don't understand makes legal see you as thoughtful and honest, not naive.

---

## Decision 5 — Framing governance, not technology

What legal ultimately signs off on isn't "I trust your technical mitigation". It's **"I see a governance structure, and if something goes wrong I know the process"**.

So every risk topic you discuss with legal needs to land on a governance artifact:

| Risk category | Governance artifact |
|---|---|
| Prompt injection | Incident response SOP + escalation matrix |
| Data leakage | Vendor risk assessment + data classification policy |
| IP / output ownership | Usage policy (which use cases AI is allowed for, which not) + review protocol |
| Hallucination | QA review process (which cases need human-in-the-loop) |
| Compliance (GDPR / Privacy Act) | DPIA (Data Protection Impact Assessment) |

You don't need to write each artifact in full yourself — but you need to propose a v0.1, and let legal iterate on it.

**How to frame it**

> "I'd recommend we set up five governance artifacts: incident SOP, vendor risk assessment, AI usage policy, QA review process, DPIA. I'll draft v0.1 of each; the final version is yours to own. What you'll be signing off isn't my word — it's a set of written governance. If we ever get audited or have an incident, this is what you point to."

"This is what you point to" is the key line. What legal ultimately worries about isn't abstract risk, it's **"if something goes wrong, how do I defend my own judgement"**. When you hand them a set of written artifacts, their own backside is covered. This is the core of selling to legal.

---

## A short do / don't

**Do**

- Open with "I'm not here to sell"
- Volunteer the risks you've already identified
- Frame each risk as "if it happens, what's the worst case", not "what's the probability"
- Split mitigation into two layers: tech + governance — emphasis on governance
- Proactively propose governance artifacts (SOPs, policies, playbooks)
- Say "I don't know" on legal questions, and let legal lead
- Let legal set the risk threshold; don't set it for them

**Don't**

- Don't pitch enthusiasm (how cool / transformative AI is)
- Don't lead with technical detail
- Don't say "probability is low"
- Don't minimise downside
- Don't frame legal's role as "approver" — frame it as "co-designer"
- Don't claim 100% on mitigation — 100% is a lie, legal sees through it instantly
- Don't come to legal after deploying

---

---

Stepping back: legal, like the CFO, isn't your adversary. They're a critical ally. But legal differs from the CFO: the CFO's job is to weigh upside and downside; **legal's job is to look only at downside, and lock it down**. That isn't legal being biased — it's the training of the profession.

So your conversations with legal shouldn't try to sell upside (not their job). What you want to demonstrate is: you see the downside, you've planned governance for it, and you're willing to let legal own the calls that matter.

Do those three things and legal shifts from "approver" to "co-designer". After that shift, every project you run gets faster — because legal stops being a bottleneck, and starts being an enabler.

# 跟法务讲 prompt injection、数据泄露、IP 风险

法务听 risk 的方式跟 engineer 讲 risk 的方式正好相反。

Engineer 讲 risk 的时候:"这个有可能发生,但概率比较低,我们有 mitigation"。法务听到这种讲法,**只听到了"有可能发生"那一句**。后面的 mitigation 和概率 caveat 完全过滤掉了。这不是法务的问题,这是他们的训练 — 法务的工作就是想象 worst case 然后想办法把 worst case 锁在抽屉里。

跟法务讲 AI risk,你要 reframe 自己的讲法。具体来说几个核心切换:

| Engineer 讲法 | 法务能听进去的讲法 |
|---|---|
| "概率比较低" | 法务不在乎概率,法务在乎"如果发生,我们怎么办" |
| "我们有 mitigation" | "如果 mitigation 失效,我们的 fallback 是什么" |
| "这是技术问题,我们 handle" | "这是 governance 问题,我们 propose 这样的 policy" |
| "这种事很罕见" | "罕见但不可能为零,所以我们 plan for it" |

下面拆几个 AI 项目最常见的法务问题怎么讲。

---

## 决策点 1 — 第一次跟法务见面,前 5 分钟

第一次跟法务的 meeting,你的目标不是"让他批准",是"让他不要把 stop 按钮按下去"。

很多 PM 第一次跟法务讲话,试图 sell — 讲 AI 多酷、多有价值、多 transformative。**这种讲法在法务那里是 negative signal**。法务听到 enthusiasm 的第一反应是:这个人没把 risk 当回事,要警惕。

**对的开场**

> "[Name],今天我主要不是来 sell 你这个项目的 — sponsor 已经签了。我来是想 walk you through 几个我已经识别到的 risk,以及我打算怎么 manage,听一下你的意见。如果你看到我漏的 risk,也 raise 给我。"

这段话做了几件事:

- 你不 sell (法务立刻松一口气)
- 你已经识别了 risk (说明你 take it seriously)
- 你主动 invite 法务挑 risk (法务的 expertise 被尊重了)
- 你 frame 这是 collaborative,不是 approval-seeking

**法务最容易问的开场反问**

> "在我们开始之前 — 这个项目你 already deployed 了吗?"

这个问题是 trap。如果你说"deployed 了",法务会觉得你绕过了他,警惕度立刻拉满。**正确答法**:

> "还没 deployed 到 production。我们现在在 PoC 阶段,跑在 [具体环境,通常是隔离的内部环境]。我故意在 deploy 之前来找你,因为想把你的 input 设计进 production version,而不是 deploy 之后再补。"

"我故意在 deploy 之前来找你"这句话非常重要 — 法务最大的恐惧是被 bypass,你主动声明没 bypass,他立刻 disarm。

---

## 决策点 2 — 解释 prompt injection

Prompt injection 是 AI 项目里最容易让法务紧张的技术 risk 之一,因为它涉及 unauthorised access、data exfiltration、system manipulation — 这些都是法务在传统 IT 项目里就熟悉的概念。

**错的讲法**

> "Prompt injection 就是有人通过精心构造的 prompt 来 manipulate LLM 的行为,让它做一些 designer 没预期的事情。我们用了 input sanitization、output filtering、和 dual model verification 来防御 …"

法务听到 "manipulate"、"unexpected"、"defence" 这几个词,焦虑度直接到 7/10。然后你讲了一堆技术 mitigation,他还是停留在那个 7/10。

**对的讲法**

不要 lead with 技术 detail。Lead with **"如果发生,最坏会怎样"**。

> "Prompt injection 是一类 attack vector — 简单讲是攻击者通过特殊构造的输入,让 AI 系统做出 designer 不想让它做的事。在我们这个项目里,如果发生 prompt injection 的最坏场景是 [具体描述: 比如未授权用户拿到不该看的数据 / AI 给出不符合公司 policy 的回应 / 内部系统被通过 AI 间接访问]。

> 我把这个 risk 分两层 manage:

> 第一层是技术 mitigation — input sanitization、output filtering、有些 critical 场景我们用 dual model verification。这一层挡住大部分 attack。

> 第二层 — 这个对你 (法务) 更重要 — 是 **post-incident response**。如果 mitigation 失效,我们 propose 的 SOP 是: [一段具体的 response plan,包括谁负责、什么时间内 escalate、给客户的 communication template]。

> 我想跟你 walk through 的不是技术,是这个 SOP。因为技术防御不可能 100%,governance 层的 response capability 才是你和我都能 sign off 的。"

这段话做了几件事:

- "最坏场景"具体化 (法务能 evaluate)
- 把 mitigation 分成两层,第一层一笔带过,第二层 (governance) 详细讲
- 主动说"技术防御不可能 100%" (法务听到诚实,信任度上升)
- frame 法务的关注点是 SOP,不是技术
- "你和我都能 sign off" — collaborative

**法务最常问的 follow-up**

> "如果发生了 incident,我们的 disclosure obligation 是什么?"

这是法律问题,不是技术问题。**不要装懂**。

> "Disclosure obligation 这一块我希望你来 lead 判断 — 我们在澳洲的话涉及 Privacy Act 和 OAIC 的 notifiable data breach 框架,这些 framework 怎么 apply 到 AI incident 上,你比我熟。我可以提供的是 incident 的技术 fact pattern,你来 map 到 obligation。我们要不要 set up 一个 incident playbook,把 fact pattern → obligation 这个 mapping 提前写好?"

把球 deliberately 扔给法务,而且 frame 成"你的 expertise"。法务被尊重,愿意 lead。

---

## 决策点 3 — 解释数据泄露 (data leakage)

数据泄露在 AI 项目里有两种,法务听到时容易混在一起,你要主动拆开:

**第一种: 训练数据泄露**

公司内部数据被用来 train 模型,而这个模型 (尤其是托管的 closed-source 模型) 可能把这些数据"记住",未来在其他用户的 query 里 leak 出来。

**第二种: 推理时数据泄露**

通过 API 把内部数据发给外部 model provider (OpenAI、Anthropic 等) 时,数据 in-transit 或者 in-vendor's-systems 的安全 risk。

**讲法**

> "数据泄露 risk 在 AI 项目里有两种,我想跟你分开讲,因为 mitigation 不一样。

> 第一种是 training data leakage — 我们用内部数据去 fine-tune 模型,理论上模型会'记住'这些数据,未来可能在 unrelated query 里 surface 出来。这一种我们的 mitigation 是: 不用 OpenAI / Anthropic 等 frontier model 的 fine-tune service (因为我们没法 verify 他们的隔离),只用我们自己 host 的 open-source 模型做 fine-tune。这样 fine-tuned weights 在我们的环境里,不出去。

> 第二种是 inference time leakage — 我们用 closed-source model 做推理时,每个 query 都会把内部数据发到 OpenAI / Anthropic 的 API endpoint。这一种 mitigation 是: 一,跟 vendor 签 enterprise agreement,有 zero data retention 条款;二,对最 sensitive 的 data 用本地的 self-hosted model,不发出去。

> 这两种 risk 的法律 implication 不一样 — 第一种偏 IP / 商业秘密,第二种偏 privacy / personal data。我希望你帮我看一下,我们的 SOP 对这两种 risk 的 coverage 是不是 sufficient。"

这种讲法的好处:

- 主动拆开两种 (法务通常不会自己拆,你拆给他他会觉得你 thoughtful)
- 每一种讲了 mitigation,但讲完之后说"你帮我看 coverage 是不是 sufficient" — 把判断权交给法务
- 提到 "enterprise agreement"、"zero data retention" 这种合同术语 (法务的语言)

**法务最常问的 follow-up: "我们能不能完全不用外部 API?"**

不要立刻 defend。先 acknowledge:

> "这是 valid 的问题。完全不用外部 API 是技术上可行的 — 我们用本地 model 替代 — 但 trade-off 是 [model quality 会下降 / 某些 use case 做不了 / 成本会上升]。我倾向的方案是 mixed: 高 sensitivity 的 data 走本地,其他走 vendor + enterprise agreement。但这是一个 risk tolerance 的判断 — 你想用什么 risk threshold,我们就 design 什么架构。我可以给你看几个 option,你来挑。"

让法务来挑 risk threshold。这是高级招 — 法务从来都是"判断 risk 的人",不是"被告知 risk 的人"。

---

## 决策点 4 — 解释 IP 风险

IP risk 在 AI 项目里有两个常见 angle,法务通常各 worry 一半:

**Angle 1: AI 生成内容的 IP 归属**

AI 生成的东西算谁的 IP?客户 prompt 出来的内容,客户 own 吗?如果 model provider 的 ToS 写 "you own the output",这个 "own" 在不同法域怎么 enforce?

**Angle 2: 训练 / 推理时用了第三方 IP**

我们的 model 用了 internet 上的 copyrighted 内容来 train (model provider 干的事),我们用这个 model 生成的内容,有没有 derivative work 风险?

**讲法**

> "IP risk 在 AI 项目里我看到两个主要 angle,我跟你 walk through:

> 第一个是 AI 生成内容的 ownership。我们用 OpenAI / Anthropic 的 model,他们的 enterprise ToS 都明确写 customer owns the output。但这个 "own" 在某些 edge case (比如 output 里 reproduce 了 training data 里的某段 copyrighted text) 可能有 gray zone。我们的 mitigation 是: 不用 AI 生成 final-form 的对外内容 (比如直接 publish 的文章),只用 AI 生成 draft,人 review 之后才出。这样 ownership 链条是人 → 公司,AI 是 tool。

> 第二个是 training data 的 derivative work 风险。这个理论上是 model provider 的责任,他们 train 的时候 ingested 什么我们不可控。但实际上如果 AI 生成的内容跟某个 copyrighted source 高度相似,我们作为 user 也可能被 drag 进诉讼。我们的 mitigation 是: 对 risk 高的 use case (创意、营销内容) 做 similarity check,跑一遍 plagiarism detection。这一块我希望你看一下 — 我们的 SOP 够不够 strong。

> 还有一个 angle 我自己没想清楚的: 如果未来 model provider (OpenAI) 因为 training data 被告,我们作为 downstream user 的 exposure 是什么?这一块你比我懂。"

最后那句 "我自己没想清楚的" — 高级 PM 才会这样讲。主动暴露一个你不懂的法律问题,反而让法务觉得你 thoughtful 而且 honest。

---

## 决策点 5 — 怎么 frame governance 而不是技术

法务最终签字依靠的不是"我相信你的技术 mitigation",是 **"我看到了一个 governance structure,如果出事我知道流程"**。

所以你跟法务讲的所有 risk,最后都要落到一个 governance artifact 上:

| Risk 类别 | Governance artifact |
|---|---|
| Prompt injection | Incident response SOP + escalation matrix |
| Data leakage | Vendor risk assessment + data classification policy |
| IP / output ownership | Usage policy (哪些 use case 是 AI-allowed,哪些不是) + review protocol |
| Hallucination | QA review process (human-in-the-loop 哪些 case) |
| Compliance (GDPR / Privacy Act) | DPIA (Data Protection Impact Assessment) |

每一个 artifact 你都不需要自己写完整 — 但你需要 propose 一个 v0.1,然后让法务来 iterate。

**讲法**

> "我建议我们 set up 5 个 governance artifact:incident SOP、vendor risk assessment、AI usage policy、QA review process、DPIA。每一个我可以 draft 一个 v0.1,但 final 版本你来 own。这样你 sign off 的不是我说什么,是一套 written governance — 这套东西如果未来被 audit 或者出 incident,你拿得出手。"

"你拿得出手"这句话非常关键。法务最终 worry 的不是抽象的 risk,是 **"如果出事,我自己怎么 defend 我的判断"**。你给他一套 written artifact,他自己的 ass 是 covered 的。这是 selling 给法务的核心招。

---

## 一些 do / don't 速记

**Do**

- 第一句话声明"不是来 sell"
- 主动 disclose 你已经识别的 risk
- 把 risk frame 成 "如果发生,最坏会怎样",不是 "概率多低"
- 把 mitigation 分两层: 技术 + governance,重点讲 governance
- 主动 propose governance artifact (SOP、policy、playbook)
- 不懂的法律问题直接说不懂,让法务 lead
- 让法务挑 risk threshold,不要替法务 set

**Don't**

- 不要讲 enthusiasm (AI 多酷、多 transformative)
- 不要 lead with 技术细节
- 不要说 "概率比较低"
- 不要 minimise downside
- 不要 frame 法务的角色是 "approver" — frame 成 "co-designer"
- 不要把 mitigation 讲到 100% — 100% 是 lie,法务一秒看穿
- 不要 deployed 之后才来找法务

## 话术速查:16 条跟法务讲 AI risk 直接抄走用的句子

### 开场

**1. 第一次见面开场**
> "[Name],今天我主要不是来 sell 你这个项目的 — sponsor 已经签了。我来是想 walk you through 几个我已经识别到的 risk,以及我打算怎么 manage,听一下你的意见。如果你看到我漏的 risk,raise 给我。"

**2. 应对"项目 deployed 了吗"的 trap**
> "还没 deployed 到 production。我们在 PoC 阶段,跑在 [具体隔离环境]。我故意在 deploy 之前来找你,因为想把你的 input 设计进 production version,而不是 deploy 之后再补。"

### Prompt injection

**3. 解释 prompt injection**
> "Prompt injection 是一类 attack vector — 攻击者通过特殊构造的输入,让 AI 系统做 designer 不想让它做的事。在我们这个项目里最坏场景是 [具体描述]。我把这个 risk 分两层 manage: 技术 mitigation + post-incident response SOP。我想跟你 walk through 的是 SOP,不是技术。"

**4. 主动说技术不能 100%**
> "技术防御不可能 100%,governance 层的 response capability 才是你和我都能 sign off 的。"

**5. 应对"disclosure obligation 是什么"**
> "Disclosure obligation 这一块希望你来 lead 判断 — 我们在澳洲的话涉及 Privacy Act 和 OAIC 的 notifiable data breach 框架,这些怎么 apply 到 AI incident 上,你比我熟。我可以提供 incident 的技术 fact pattern,你来 map 到 obligation。"

### Data leakage

**6. 主动拆开两种 data leakage**
> "数据泄露在 AI 项目里有两种,我想分开讲,因为 mitigation 不一样: training data leakage 和 inference time leakage。"

**7. 解释 training data leakage**
> "我们用内部数据 fine-tune 模型,理论上模型会记住这些数据。Mitigation 是: 不用 OpenAI / Anthropic 的 fine-tune service,只用自己 host 的 open-source 模型 fine-tune。这样 fine-tuned weights 在我们环境里,不出去。"

**8. 解释 inference time leakage**
> "Inference time 每个 query 都会把内部数据发到 vendor API。Mitigation 是: 跟 vendor 签 enterprise agreement,zero data retention 条款;最 sensitive 的 data 用本地 self-hosted model。"

**9. 应对"能不能完全不用外部 API"**
> "技术上可行 — 用本地 model 替代,但 trade-off 是 [model quality 下降 / 成本上升]。我倾向 mixed: 高 sensitivity 走本地,其他走 vendor + agreement。但这是 risk tolerance 判断 — 你想用什么 threshold,我们 design 什么架构。"

### IP 风险

**10. 拆 IP risk 两个 angle**
> "IP risk 我看两个 angle: AI 生成内容的 ownership,和 training data 的 derivative work 风险。"

**11. 讲 ownership mitigation**
> "我们用的 vendor enterprise ToS 都明确 customer owns the output。但 edge case 可能有 gray zone。Mitigation 是: 不用 AI 生成 final-form 对外内容,只生成 draft,人 review 之后才出。"

**12. 暴露一个自己不懂的法律问题**
> "还有一个 angle 我自己没想清楚的: 如果未来 model provider 因为 training data 被告,我们作为 downstream user 的 exposure 是什么?这一块你比我懂。"

### Governance

**13. Frame governance 而不是技术**
> "我建议我们 set up 5 个 governance artifact: incident SOP、vendor risk assessment、AI usage policy、QA review process、DPIA。每一个我 draft v0.1,final 版本你 own。"

**14. 关键 selling 招**
> "这套东西如果未来被 audit 或者出 incident,你拿得出手。"

### 让法务做决定

**15. 让法务挑 risk threshold**
> "这是 risk tolerance 的判断,不是技术判断。你设定 threshold,我 design 架构。我可以给你看几个 option,你来挑。"

**16. 不懂就直说**
> "这个我没法答你 — 我不是 lawyer。我能 provide 的是 [事实 / 数据 / 流程],你来 map 到 legal obligation。"

---

往大了说,法务跟 CFO 一样,不是项目的敌人,是关键盟友。但法务跟 CFO 又不一样: CFO 的工作是 weigh upside 和 downside;**法务的工作是只看 downside,把它锁住**。这不是法务的偏见,是法律行业的训练。

所以你跟法务的对话,不要试图 sell upside (那不是他的工作)。你要 demonstrate 的是: 你看到了 downside,你 plan 了 governance,你愿意 let 法务 own 关键判断。

如果你做到这三件事,法务会从 "approver" 变成 "co-designer"。这个转变之后,你后面的项目会快很多 — 因为法务不再是瓶颈,他变成了 enabler。
